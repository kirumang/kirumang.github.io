Research Landscape

Today's largest cluster—five VLA and foundation-model papers—converges on a theme distinct from the field's usual march toward bigger multimodal pretraining: adding a verification or self-monitoring layer on top of otherwise black-box action generation, rather than trusting a single forward pass. EmbodiedSkills treats every skill decision as an execution proposal that must pass a prerequisite check before running and an outcome check afterward; VerNav replaces expensive per-step autoregressive LLM reasoning in vision-and-language navigation with a lightweight verifier that only escalates to a full generator when uncertain, cutting decision latency more than 10× on Room-to-Room; and Knowing When to Stop reads a VLA's own cross-attention entropy to decide, training-free, when an action chunk has run out of grounding in the current observation. DroneCATS makes the underlying motivation explicit: evaluating MLLMs dropped directly into a drone control loop, it finds that small open models often navigate as well as frontier models but lose episodes by declaring arrival prematurely or never declaring it at all — spatial competence is not the bottleneck, protocol discipline is. REFACTOR-VLA and Facet-0 push structure earlier in the pipeline instead: the former learns a typed skill library gated by description-length and return-preservation tests (and finds that a bigger 430M-parameter world model actually hurt clustering versus a better training objective), the latter builds an explicit action-wrench critic so a manipulation policy can distinguish superficially similar motions by their contact outcome, reaching 82% success on sub-millimeter assembly versus 15% for the strongest baseline.

A parallel cluster of five papers pursues the same instinct from the control-theory side: keep a model-based or classical controller's guarantees while letting learning handle what it can't specify in advance. ProxPI proves, not just demonstrates, that centering MPPI's sampling distribution on an out-of-distribution learned policy traps the optimizer near a bad solution regardless of rollout budget, and fixes it by injecting the policy only as a soft proximity cost around nominal-centered sampling. SG-RL takes the mirror-image approach for RL-based MPC weight adaptation, keeping sampled closed-loop return as the objective while injecting bounded solver-derived gradients as auxiliary guidance, reaching PPO's best return with up to 70.6% fewer samples on real racing platforms with intentional model mismatch. Dual Process Motion Planning states the philosophy outright, borrowing "Thinking Fast and Slow" to pair a symbolic System-2 solver with experience-driven System-1 modules under a metacognitive controller. Even the more classical entries fit the pattern: the PID regulatability paper proves a long-standing engineering heuristic — tuning gains channel-by-channel in coupled MIMO systems — is theoretically justified rather than just convenient, while the Rao-Blackwellized POMDP paper narrows its efficiency gains to problems with tractable sufficient statistics rather than claiming a general fix. None of these are the flashiest results in the batch, but together they suggest the sim-to-real, learning-versus-classical-control debate is settling into hybrid designs with formal guarantees on the learned component's failure modes, not just empirical ablations.

A third theme is robustness to missing information, expressed at very different scales. SCARAB and the connectivity-aware exploration paper both design multi-robot coordination that assumes communication will fail — the former dropping GPS and RF entirely for a river-bridging swarm, the latter extending exploration graphs specifically to keep area partitioning stable as the communication graph itself becomes unstable. HitMem tackles the same problem for a single embodied agent's memory, adding temporal decay and a two-stage retrieval escalation so that objects moved by unobserved events don't force costly re-exploration. At the hardware level, the compact CVT robotic finger and MONORIGAMI folding actuator both push adaptivity into the mechanism itself rather than the controller: the finger's passive transmission amplifies grip force up to 4.19× purely from wire tension with no added actuators or sensors, and MONORIGAMI converts a single 3D-printed part into three different multi-DoF systems via programmed stiffness anisotropy alone. Facet-0: A Robotic Foundation Model for Contact-Rich Precise Manipulation

2026-09-01cs.ROcs.LG
Haoyuan Deng, Haichao Liu, Wenkai Guo, Yuan Ling, Zaijia Yang
Core Contributions
  • Predicts and values the contact consequences of its own actions rather than just predicting motion: a causal wrench history is aligned with vision-language semantics, and flow matching generates each action chunk jointly with the wrist-wrench profile it is expected to induce.
  • Trains a distributional Action-Wrench Critic on deployment rollouts specifically to distinguish motions with similar task progress but different contact outcomes — sharper credit assignment than typical sparse-reward manipulation RL.
  • Reaches 82% mean success on five sub-millimeter computer-assembly tasks versus 15% for the strongest baseline, with 0.5mm placement accuracy and 50ms command latency — a 5x+ jump that suggests real industrial-grade precision, not an incremental gain.
  • Trained on ManuFacet-1K, a 1,000-hour force-synchronized corpus spanning three embodiments and multiple manufacturing cells; scale and embodiment diversity may matter as much as the wrench-critic architecture itself.
  • Keeps RL restricted to executable Cartesian actions via a lightweight bounded actor for on-robot adaptation, while an auxiliary wrench head preserves predictive contact-coupling without directly commanding force — separates predicting contact from controlling it.
Show abstract

Real-world robotic assembly at sub-millimeter tolerances demands spatial precision, compliant interaction, and robustness to contact failures. We present Facet-0, a robotic foundation model that predicts and values the contact consequences of its actions. Facet-0 unifies multimodal representation learning and reinforcement learning (RL) post-training around a joint action-wrench proposal: a causal wrench history is aligned with vision-language semantics and kinematic state, and flow matching generates each action chunk together with the future wrist-wrench profile it is expected to induce. Deployment rollouts train a distributional Action-Wrench Critic to distinguish motions with similar task progress but different contact outcomes, while phase-aware rewards and contact-selective credit concentrate policy improvement on decisive interactions. To accommodate part-specific dynamics, a lightweight bounded actor reuses the frozen representation for on-robot adaptation; RL remains defined over executable Cartesian actions, while an auxiliary wrench head preserves predictive, non-commanded action-contact coupling. Trained on ManuFacet-1K, a 1,000-hour force-synchronized corpus spanning three embodiments and multiple manufacturing cells, the bounded task-adapted system reaches 82% mean success on five sub-millimeter computer-assembly tasks, compared with 15% for the strongest baseline, with 0.5 mm placement accuracy and 50 ms command latency.

Evaluating Multimodal LLMs as Generalist Vision-Language-Action Agents for Drone Control: Commanding, Approaching, Tracking and Searching

2026-09-01cs.ROcs.AI
Jaewoo Park, Minyoung Lee, Sukmin Seo, Moonbin Yim, Hyunwook Yoon
Core Contributions
  • Frames a sharp question: when an MLLM is dropped directly into a drone's control loop with its entire action space declared only in the prompt, does flying fail, or does acting fail?
  • Introduces DroneCATS-Agent with a swappable MLLM backbone and a benchmark spanning four capabilities — approach, track, search, multi-drone command — treating model choice as the independent variable rather than fixing one model and varying the task.
  • Key finding: small open models down to 2B parameters often navigate into the success radius more reliably than frontier models, but lose episodes by declaring arrival prematurely or never declaring it — spatial competence isn't the bottleneck, action-protocol discipline is.
  • Multi-drone commanding surfaces a specific small-model failure: blindly copying a single coordinate across distinct drone views instead of reasoning per-view — a shortcut, not genuine multi-agent understanding.
  • Reframes the open problem away from 'better navigation' toward 'sustaining a declared protocol and emitting the correct terminating action' at onboard compute cost — a different target than most VLA scaling work chases.
Show abstract

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are strong perceivers of images and video. We ask how far that reach extends into acting: dropping an MLLM directly into a drone's control loop, with its entire action space declared solely in the prompt. Recent systems approach this setting but increasingly narrow the model's decision-making. We widen it back. We introduce DroneCATS-Agent, an architecture where the MLLM is a swappable component, and DroneCATS, a benchmark treating the model as the independent variable. Beyond merely flying toward a pixel, our agent entrusts the model to yaw and search, deliberate when unsure, and self-declare arrival—all without fine-tuning or function-calling schemas. Evaluating frontier and open models across four core capabilities—approaching a visible target, tracking a moving one, searching outside the initial view, and commanding a multi-drone fleet—reveals that even the simplest embodied settings are far from solved. Crucially, to identify what breaks first at the edge, our roster scales down to 2B parameters. The findings expose a stark paradox: it is not the flying that fails. Small open models often navigate into the success radius more reliably than frontier models, yet lose the episode by declaring arrival prematurely or not at all. Multi-drone commanding amplifies this divide, with small models failing by blindly copying a single coordinate across distinct views. Viewed as vision-language-action agents, the models' spatial perception holds up, but their action protocol does not. What separates a deployable edge model from a frontier model is not navigation, but the discipline to sustain a declared protocol and emit the correct terminating action. The open problem is closing this gap at onboard compute costs—yielding a fast model that plans persistently and knows exactly when it is done—and DroneCATS is built to measure that distance.

EmbodiedSkills: A Unified Framework for Orchestrating, Training, and Deploying VLA Agents

2026-09-01cs.ROcs.AI
Wei Wang, Wenqiao Zhang, Yutong Lin, Yuqian Yuan, Tianwei Lin
Core Contributions
  • Frames every VLA skill decision as an 'execution proposal' that must pass a prerequisite check before running and an outcome check afterward, rather than trusting the model's action or skill choice outright — a verification loop wrapped around otherwise black-box VLA execution.
  • A shared executable-skill interface decouples skill selection from low-level VLA execution, so the underlying policy (tested here with Qwen3-VL plus OpenPI/pi0.5) can be swapped without changing the agent loop.
  • Task-adapted low-level policies reach 86.20% average success across 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks and 97.40% across four LIBERO suites, but only 12.5% on four memory-dependent RMBench tasks — verifying physical execution doesn't fix a missing memory or state-tracking capability.
  • Records planning, execution, verification, and recovery events as structured trajectories that double as supervision signal for future training — turning runtime logs into a data source, not just a monitoring tool.
Show abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) models map visual observations and language instructions directly to robot actions, but long-horizon tasks require more than action prediction. An agent must coordinate perception, planning, execution, progress verification, and recovery as the physical state evolves. An action prediction or a model-generated skill decision does not, by itself, guarantee that the proposed operation is valid in the current state or that its outcome will be verified. We propose EmbodiedSkills, a unified framework that treats each skill decision as an execution proposal: the runtime checks its prerequisites before execution and verifies the outcome afterward. A shared executable-skill interface connects high-level skill selection, bounded low-level VLA execution, and post-action verification within a single agent loop. Because this interface remains fixed, low-level VLA policies can be replaced or adapted without changing the agent loop. The interface also records planning, execution, verification, and recovery events as structured trajectories, which provide supervision for individual components and can support optional online adaptation when interactive feedback is available. We instantiate EmbodiedSkills with Qwen3-VL and OpenPI/pi0.5 on RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO. Task-adapted low-level VLA policies achieve an average success rate of 86.20% across 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks and 97.40% across the four LIBERO suites. These results establish the execution performance of the task-adapted low-level VLA policies used in EmbodiedSkills. On four memory-dependent RMBench tasks, the same task-adapted execution approach achieves 12.5% average success. The framework provides a trainable and inspectable agent layer for turning these policies into closed-loop embodied systems.

REFACTOR-VLA: Unsupervised Library Learning of Typed Motor Programs

2026-09-01cs.LGcs.AIcs.RO
Riyaaz Shaik, Chandru Venkataraman
Core Contributions
  • Tackles a specific unresolved question in VLA skill discovery — when are two action sequences 'behaviorally equivalent'? — with a Behavioral-Equivalence Kernel computed from rollouts of a learned world model, instead of clustering raw embeddings or delegating the judgment to an uncalibrated language model.
  • Wake/sleep architecture: the sleep phase clusters motor-program fragments via the kernel, the wake phase emits typed lambda terms from a Hindley–Milner-inspired vocabulary consumed by a rectified-flow action decoder, and abstractions are admitted only if they pass Minimum Description Length and return-preservation gates — abstraction is earned, not assumed.
  • Counterintuitive finding: enlarging the world model from 188M to 430M parameters worsened performance on all 4 LIBERO suites, while adding an auxiliary contrastive (InfoNCE) loss during world-model warmup is what actually improved clustering — training objective mattered more than raw capacity.
  • Reports Normalized Mutual Information averaging Δ=+0.184 above the strongest published baseline across all 4 suites (up to 0.915±0.013 on the goal suite, 95% bootstrap CI of [0.683, 0.729] across 12 providers), and produces the first real-LIBERO task-language skill library, using 2 of 3 admitted abstractions to rewrite all 256 sampled demonstrations.
Show abstract

Most vision-language-action (VLA) models — OpenVLA, π0, RT-2, RDT-1B — are monolithic: they emit raw motor commands or short action chunks without organizing behavior into reusable abstractions, so they degrade on long-horizon tasks and resist interpretation. Existing skill-discovery methods sidestep the core question of when two action sequences are behaviorally equivalent, either clustering contrastive embeddings or delegating the judgment to a language model uncalibrated to the robot's dynamics. We introduce REFACTOR-VLA, a wake/sleep system for learning reusable skills. Its sleep phase clusters motor-program fragments under a Behavioral-Equivalence Kernel (BEK) computed from rollouts of a learned latent world model M_φ; its wake phase emits typed lambda terms over a Hindley—Milner-inspired vocabulary, consumed by a library-conditioned rectified-flow action decoder. Abstractions are admitted only if they pass Minimum Description Length and return-preservation gates. On LIBERO we report two findings. First, enlarging the world model from 188M to 430M parameters worsened performance on 4 of 4 suites, so capacity alone does not help. Second, the training objective matters far more: adding an auxiliary supervised contrastive (InfoNCE) loss during world-model warmup substantially improves sleep-phase clustering, giving Normalized Mutual Information at n=3 seeds of 0.462 \pm 0.021 (object), 0.867 \pm 0.025 (spatial), 0.915 \pm 0.013 (goal) and 0.754 \pm 0.010 (LIBERO-10), and beating the strongest published baseline on all 4 suites by a mean Δ=+0.184. Across providers (n=12) the 95% bootstrap confidence interval for mean pairwise NMI is [0.683, 0.729] (mean 0.705). The sleep phase also yields the first real-LIBERO task-language library: the decoder uses 2 of 3 admitted abstractions and rewrites all 256 sampled demonstrations.

Knowing When to Stop: Adaptive Action Chunking via Internal Cross-Attention Dynamics in VLAs

2026-09-01cs.RO
Runze Xu, Xiaolong Shan, Shuang Dai, Yu Wang, Jincheng Yu
Core Contributions
  • Identifies an internal, training-free signal for adaptive action-chunk length in VLAs: as prediction horizon extends, action-to-observation cross-attention becomes more dispersed and its entropy rises toward a plateau, correlating with rising action-prediction error — the model's own attention pattern reveals when it's losing grounding in the current observation.
  • Uses this to trigger a truncation mechanism that detects sustained high-entropy plateaus and dynamically picks the execution horizon at inference time, reusing attention weights the policy already computes — adds negligible overhead versus fixed or other adaptive-chunking schemes.
  • Validated across two different VLA backbones (π0.5 and X-VLA) and three benchmark suites (RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO) plus three real-world manipulation tasks, improving average task success over both fixed-horizon and other adaptive-chunking baselines while preserving efficient closed-loop control.
Show abstract

Action chunking is a standard execution strategy in modern Vision-Language-Action (VLA) frameworks, but fixed execution horizons impose a trade-off between efficiency and accuracy. Short chunks require frequent inference and may cause oscillatory behavior, whereas long chunks can become misaligned with newly observed states. We address this limitation with an adaptive action chunking approach based on internal cross-attention dynamics in the action expert. We observe that, as the prediction horizon extends, action-to-observation cross-attention becomes increasingly dispersed and its entropy rises toward a plateau. This pattern is associated with higher action prediction error and provides an online signal that the current observation offers limited grounding for further open-loop execution. Based on this observation, we introduce a training-free truncation mechanism that detects sustained high-entropy plateaus and dynamically selects the execution horizon during inference. The method uses attention weights already computed by the policy and introduces negligible additional overhead. Evaluations on π_{0.5} and X-VLA across RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO, and three real-world manipulation tasks show improved average task success over fixed-horizon and adaptive chunking baselines, while preserving efficient closed-loop control. These results show that cross-attention dynamics can provide a practical internal signal for adaptive action execution in VLAs.

Does Imitation Learning Preserve Temporal Robustness in Dexterous Manipulation? An Expert-Learner Comparison Across Task Execution Speeds

2026-09-01cs.ROcs.LG
Clinton Enwerem, John S. Baras, Calin Belta
Core Contributions
  • Directly tests a robustness axis usually skipped in imitation learning papers: whether a policy preserves the expert's performance across execution speeds, not just across new scenes or objects.
  • Matches initial conditions and speedup factors between a scripted expert and an ACT learner on ParcelStow, a contact-rich parcel acquire-reorient-insert task, isolating temporal robustness from other confounds.
  • Both reach 100% success at nominal speed, but at the maximum demonstrated speed expert success is 84% versus 53% for ACT — equal nominal success masks a large robustness gap once execution speed varies.
  • Stage-level analysis pins 35 of ACT's 47 failures at max speed on insertion misalignment specifically; under a relative-motion handoff every ACT rollout retains the parcel through reorientation, yet only 64% finish the full task versus 95% after expert acquisition.
  • Across all evaluated policies and speeds, zero of 414 acquisitions without force closure complete the task — a hard mechanical precondition that no amount of policy speed-robustness can substitute for.
Show abstract

Dexterous manipulation policies learned by imitation are typically evaluated for robustness to variation in scenes, objects, or instructions, but their performance across task execution speeds is less often examined. This leaves open how much temporal robustness a learner retains relative to the expert it imitates. We compare an expert and learner under the same task conditions, initial-condition draws, and speedup factors. We instantiate the evaluation in ParcelStow, a contact-rich task in which the robot acquires, reorients, and inserts a parcel. The demonstrations span the speedup range for the manipulation phases after parcel acquisition. A scripted expert and an Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policy trained from the expert's demonstrations both achieve 100 percent task success at nominal speed. Their success rates diverge within the demonstrated range: at its maximum, expert success is 84 percent and ACT success is 53 percent. Two ACT policies with different parameter initializations show similar degradation, decreasing by 34 and 48 percentage points from nominal speed to the maximum demonstrated speed, compared with 16 points for the expert. Stage-level analysis shows that 35 of ACT's 47 failures at the maximum demonstrated speed are insertion misalignments. Under the relative-motion handoff, every ACT acquisition retains the parcel through reorientation and transfer in free space, but only 64 percent complete the overall task, compared with 95 percent after expert acquisition. Across all evaluated policies and speeds, none of the 414 acquisitions without force closure completes the task. Equal nominal task success therefore does not imply preservation of expert performance across execution speeds. Code, data, and evaluation scripts are available at https://github.com/coenwerem/parcelstow.

Peg-in-Bench: A Modular Benchmark for High-Precision Robotic Insertion

2026-09-01cs.RO
Yosel Delgado, José G. Buenaventura-Carreón, Floris Erich, Roman Mykhailyshyn, Tomohiro Motoda
Core Contributions
  • Addresses benchmark rigidity in peg-in-hole evaluation directly: existing benchmarks fix task configuration, making it hard to separate genuine robustness from overfitting to one setup — this benchmark instead ships fully 3D-printable modular components (multiple peg geometries, tolerance levels, configurable bases) that combine into a large space of insertion and assembly tasks.
  • Ships a scenario generation tool that produces standardized, machine-readable task configurations alongside the physical STL files — aimed at cross-lab reproducibility, not just a fixed physical kit.
  • By varying layout, orientation, and task structure while holding physical conditions controlled, the benchmark is explicitly designed to test generalization to unseen configurations rather than just performance on one fixed peg-hole pair.
Show abstract

High-precision insertion remains a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation due to the strict alignment requirements and contact-rich interactions involved. Although peg-in-hole tasks are widely used for evaluation, existing bench- marks often rely on fixed task configurations, limiting their ability to assess robustness and generalization across different insertion scenarios. This paper introduces a reconfigurable peg-in-hole benchmark designed to evaluate task generalization in high-precision insertion. The benchmark consists of a set of fully 3D-printable modular components, including multiple peg geometries, tolerance levels, and configurable base structures that can be combined to generate a large variety of insertion and assembly tasks. By varying object layouts, orientations, and task structures while maintaining controlled physical conditions, the benchmark enables systematic evaluation of adaptation to unseen scenarios. To support reproducibility, we additionally provide a scenario generation tool capable of producing standardized task configurations and machine-readable task descriptions. The scenario generation tool and the STL files of the benchmark pieces are available through the project repository: https://github.com/aistairc/peg-in-bench.

Non-Prehensile Throwing: A Reinforcement Learning Perspective

2026-09-01cs.RO
Abdullah Mustafa, Ryo Hanai, Ixchel G. Ramirez-Alpizar, Floris Erich, Ryoichi Nakajo
Core Contributions
  • Moves non-prehensile (grasp-free) throwing away from model-based optimization with simplified contact models and hand-crafted low-dimensional trajectory parameterizations, instead directly optimizing full joint-space trajectories via RL that can exploit sliding and rolling contact modes the simplified models can't represent.
  • Formulates the MDP as a dynamical system evolving joint state conditioned on target, object model, and initial configuration, planning joint-jerk trajectories offline at low control rate then upsampling to smooth high-rate velocity commands — separates trajectory-shape optimization from execution-rate control.
  • For sim-to-real, minimizes the robot-dynamics gap via minimum-jerk system identification and trains uncertainty-aware policies specifically for object-modeling errors; sensitivity analysis shows robustness to mass uncertainty but high sensitivity to dynamic friction, consistent with the sliding-based release mechanism — a clear failure-mode explanation, not just an aggregate number.
  • Achieves 99% simulated success generalizing to unseen objects, then deployed zero-shot on a UR5e near its 5 m/s end-effector velocity limit, throws objects as heavy as 790g and as large as 20×20×28cm to targets up to 350cm distance or 180cm elevation with a 97% real-world success rate.
Show abstract

Robotic throwing enables fast object transport and extends a robot's reachable workspace beyond traditional pick-and-place. While prehensile (grasp-based) throwing works well for graspable items, non-prehensile (grasp-free) throwing is better suited for large, heavy, and/or deformable objects. Existing approaches rely on model-based optimization with simplified contact models (e.g., dynamic grasping) and low-dimensional trajectory parameterizations, which limit solution quality and reachable workspace. We propose a reinforcement learning approach that additionally leverages sliding and rolling contact modes and directly optimizes joint-space trajectories without analytical contact models or custom parameterizations. The Markov Decision Process (MDP) is formulated as a dynamical system that evolves the robot's joint state conditioned on the throwing target, object model, and initial configuration. Joint-jerk trajectories are planned offline at a low control rate and upsampled into smooth, high-rate velocity commands for deployment. For sim-to-real transfer, we minimize the robot-dynamics gap through minimum-jerk system identification and train uncertainty-aware policies to mitigate object-modeling errors, particularly sensitivity to dynamic friction. In simulation, the policy achieves 99% success across thousands of configurations and generalizes to unseen objects. Sensitivity analysis shows robustness to mass uncertainty but high sensitivity to dynamic friction, consistent with the sliding-based release mechanism. Deployed zero-shot on a UR5e operating near its physical limits (5 m/s end-effector velocity), our method throws diverse objects including heavy (790 g) and large (20x20x28 cm) items to targets up to 350 cm distance or 180 cm elevation, achieving a 97% real-world success rate.

SG-AMP: Scene-Graph-Guided Active Perception and Semantics-Aware Motion Planning for Pepper Plants

2026-09-01cs.RO
Rohit Menon, Shiva Rudra Lolla, Niklas Mueller-Goldingen, Gokul Chenchani, Ribana Roscher
Core Contributions
  • Targets a specific agricultural perception gap: pepper-peduncle attachments are often unobserved from a distance, so the scene graph explicitly hypothesizes hidden attachments and directs close-range views toward them, rather than only revisiting regions already flagged as uncertain.

We present SG-AMP, integrating robust depth completion with input-conditioned uncertainty, persistent panoptic mapping, plant scene-graph reasoning, and semantics-aware active view-motion planning. Beyond inspecting uncertain observed regions, the scene graph explicitly hypothesizes unobserved pepper—peduncle attachments and directs close-range sensing toward them. Candidate views are selected according to expected information gain, while class-dependent motion costs distinguish protected peppers, peduncles, and stems from conditionally traversable foliage. 2026-09-01cs.RO
Leonardo Gargani, Matteo Frosi, Matteo Matteucci
Core Contributions
  • Fuses dual-antenna RTK-GNSS via an EKF for both position and heading — not position alone — specifically to counter localization drift over long-duration missions in open outdoor spaces, rather than targeting instantaneous accuracy.
  • Adds three controller-aware refinements to a mature coverage planner plus a Behavior-Tree mission manager for multi-goal execution, layered failure recovery, cost-aware goal selection, and autonomous return-to-charge docking — a systems-integration contribution rather than a new planning algorithm.
  • Validated across multiple real outdoor sites with varying geometry and obstacle density, sweeping 93.1% to 96.1% of the planned coverage area — the tight range suggests the stack generalizes across site conditions rather than being tuned to one.
