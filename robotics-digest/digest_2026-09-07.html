Research Landscape

Today's 30-paper batch shows the field treating data collection itself as a first-class research problem rather than an afterthought to policy learning. SPOT reframes what actually bottlenecks long-horizon humanoid teleoperation: not motion-retargeting fidelity, which is well studied, but the operator's "perceptual horizon" — how much spatial context they can maintain while narrow views, unstable robot-mounted cameras, and coupled head-camera control conspire against them. M3-Tele attacks a related gap from the sensing side, showing that jointly capturing tactile, force, and proprioceptive data during compliant whole-body teleoperation cuts force-tracking error from 4.132N to 0.346N, while CosmoH2G tackles a data-diversity gap — existing hand-to-gripper transfer datasets rarely capture complex spatial movements like rotations or flips — with a 6,189-episode dataset and a keyframe-then-interpolate architecture built specifically to survive compounding trajectory drift. Dex-X pushes furthest here, treating physics simulation itself as a "tactile completion engine" that reconstructs contact dynamics missing from ordinary human video, distilling the result into a policy that reaches 93% real-world success on cube picking despite a teacher that only hit 65.9% in simulation. A smaller cluster — FMSGOC and ComVLA (from the same team) — extends this data-economy theme to the wireless link itself, using language and vision-language priors to decide what's worth transmitting under 6G bandwidth constraints rather than compressing everything indiscriminately.

A second theme is empirical self-scrutiny inside the VLA and world-model literature. OpenWAM factors world-action model pretraining into composable modules specifically so that "what design choice actually matters" becomes an answerable, controlled experiment rather than a monolithic architecture choice, distilling its findings into three reusable principles. PACE notices that vision and force/torque signals drift substantially between simulation and hardware while proprioception stays comparatively stable, and builds its entire cross-modal supervision strategy around that asymmetry, reaching 93.3% real-world assembly success with only a 2.7-point sim-to-real drop and zero fine-tuning. ICI-VLA challenges the default assumption that new-task adaptation requires gradient updates at all, instead retrieving spatiotemporally aligned in-context demonstrations at a frozen policy's inference time. The most explicitly self-critical entry is the Greek-language VLA transfer study, which documents that several plausible evaluation metrics actively produce false conclusions — a color-histogram metric rewards noise, a single-goal benchmark scores nearly identically under correct and deliberately wrong instructions — and proposes "build a guaranteed null before trusting a metric" as a general lesson for low-resource robot-policy evaluation. Together these papers suggest a subfield increasingly interested in why methods work, or whether the numbers proving they work can even be trusted.

Safety in this batch operates at several layers that don't yet talk to each other. At the decision layer, the LLM-orchestrator safety benchmark finds that model family alone determines whether an LLM issuing humanoid commands stays near zero violations (Claude, Gemini) or drifts to repeated violations (GPT-4o-mini, up to 13 per session) — and that context-window management can cut violations for every other backend while nearly doubling GPT-4o-mini's. At the multi-agent policy layer, decentralized predictive shielding lets robots safely adapt pretrained policies at deployment without centralized coordination, explicitly fixing both the reactivity and centralization weaknesses of prior shields. At the physical, proxemic layer, CALM shows a humanoid's arm configuration alone shifts human stopping distance by 30–36cm, and demonstrates in a planning case study that reconfiguring the arms makes an otherwise-unreachable 1.10m approach goal achievable under an identical safety constraint — direct evidence that body configuration belongs in the planner, not just the comfort model. Elsewhere, safety-adjacent formal guarantees show up in less obviously "safety" contexts too: cTPO's decomposition algorithm provably preserves plan optimality while cutting solve time by four orders of magnitude, and the fall-anticipation paper exposes that standard time-series forecasting benchmarks assume periodicity that humanoid locomotion actually violates. Stacked together, the batch sketches what an end-to-end safety-aware humanoid stack would need — trustworthy high-level command generation, low-level reactive guarantees, and physically grounded proxemic models — without yet showing anyone building all three at once.

Foundation Models for Generalizable Semantic and Goal-Oriented Communication
2026-09-07 cs.LG, cs.AI, cs.RO, eess.IV Top author: W. Y. Poe (h=11)
Boliang Liu, Wint Yi Poe, Riccardo Trivisonno, Giuseppe Caire
Core Contributions
  • Targets a specific failure mode of learned semantic communication codecs: they overfit to training distributions and collapse at very low bit rates because they try to compress the whole signal instead of task-relevant content.
  • Decouples “what to send” from “how to reconstruct”: a vision-language foundation model picks a sparse set of goal-aligned semantic anchors to transmit, while a separate diffusion model, fine-tuned for masked completion, reconstructs everything else at the receiver.
  • Reaches 0.039 bits per pixel while holding semantic cosine similarity at 0.87-0.90 on CIFAR-10 and 0.83-0.86 on unseen ImageNet data, indicating the anchor-based approach generalizes past its training distribution rather than just fitting it.
  • Outperforms strong end-to-end compression baselines specifically at the lowest bit rates, the regime where prior systems degrade most sharply.
Show abstract

Semantic and goal-oriented communication is increasingly studied for 6G, but generalization beyond seen data remains a key weakness under tight rate budgets. Many existing systems overfit their training data and degrade sharply at very low bit rates because they attempt to compress the entire signal. We introduce Foundation Model-Guided Semantic and Goal-Oriented Communication (FMSGOC), a framework that uses broad visual-linguistic Foundation Model priors to mitigate overfitting. It further improves rate efficiency by concentrating bits on sparse, goal-aligned anchors and relying on generative foundation-model priors to reconstruct the masked regions. By decoupling what to send from how to reconstruct, a vision-language foundation model selects and transmits a sparse set of semantic anchors, while a pretrained diffusion model, fine-tuned for masked completion, reconstructs the image at the receiver. In our experiments, FMSGOC reaches 0.039 bits per pixel (BPP), maintains high semantic fidelity (cosine similarity 0.87-0.90 on CIFAR-10), remains robust on previously unseen inputs (0.83-0.86 on ImageNet), and shows good perceptual similarity (0.1278/0.1558, CIFAR-10/ImageNet), outperforming strong end-to-end baselines at lower bit rates.

ComVLA: Communication-Aware Split Inference for VLA Models in 6G-Connected Robotics
2026-09-07 cs.RO Top author: Boliang Liu (h=0)
Boliang Liu, Wint Yi Poe, Jingyun Di, Riccardo Trivisonno, Giuseppe Caire
Core Contributions
  • Extends the same team’s semantic-communication line of work specifically to VLA inference offloading: since VLA models are too large to run on-robot, cloud offload is common, but the wireless link caps how much sensing data can be sent per control step.
  • Key insight distinguishing it from prior token pruners and semantic codecs: language already indicates which visual tokens matter, so ComVLA uses language guidance to adapt the transmitted token budget to current channel capacity instead of compressing indiscriminately or ignoring the channel.
  • Cuts transmitted tokens from 512 to 32 on LIBERO, reducing inference compute by 74% and latency by 22% versus OpenVLA-OFT, at a cost of only 1.5 percentage points of task success (95.4% vs. 96.9%).
  • Explicitly validated to stay within capacity budget under Rayleigh and Rician fading channel models, tying the method to realistic wireless conditions rather than an idealized fixed-bandwidth assumption.
Show abstract

Connected robotics is an emerging 6G application where mobile robots follow natural-language instructions to manipulate physical objects. The Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that enable this are too large to run on the robot; a common trend is to offload inference to the cloud. The wireless link, however, limits how much sensing data the edge can transmit per control step. Two recent lines address this constraint: semantic communication codecs compress sensor data but require channel-specific retraining, and VLA token pruners select tokens from image but ignore the channel. Our insight is that the dense semantic information contained in the language already indicates which visual tokens matter. We propose ComVLA, a framework that uses this language guidance to adapt the VLA token budget to the channel capacity. Transmitting 32 tokens instead of 512 on the LIBERO benchmark, ComVLA cuts inference compute by 74% and inference latency by 22% versus the original OpenVLA-OFT baseline, at a cost of 1.5 pp in average task success (95.4% vs. 96.9%), and it stays within the capacity budget under Rayleigh and Rician fading. These results demonstrate that co-designing VLA inference and wireless communication is a practical direction for 6G-connected robotics.

SPOT: Spatial Perception-Oriented Long-Horizon Humanoid Teleoperation
2026-09-07 cs.RO Top author: Lixing Fang (h=0)
Lixing Fang, Ziyan Xiong, Sunli Chen, Zhiyang Dou, Chuang Gan
Core Contributions
  • Reframes what actually bottlenecks long-horizon humanoid teleoperation: not motion-retargeting fidelity but the operator’s “perceptual horizon” — how much task-relevant spatial context they can maintain over time.
  • Diagnoses three specific causes of a narrowed perceptual horizon in existing systems: narrow camera views miss peripheral events, robot-mounted cameras shake during locomotion, and coupled head-camera control means looking around accidentally moves the robot.
  • Decouples visual exploration from robot actuation entirely: a binocular fisheye feed is rendered onto a virtual hemisphere around the operator, so head rotation changes where they look within a wide, stabilized view rather than commanding the robot’s head or torso.

Show abstract

High-quality demonstration data is becoming a central bottleneck for training general-purpose humanoid robots. While recent humanoid teleoperation systems have made substantial progress in retargeting human motion to robot motion, long-horizon loco-manipulation requires another capability: operators must maintain task-relevant spatial awareness over time, e.g., object locations, surrounding environments, the robot's pose. We call the extent of this awareness the operator's perceptual horizon. However, existing methods often shorten this: narrow views miss peripheral events, robot-mounted cameras become unstable during locomotion, and coupled head-view control makes looking around interfere with robot motion. We present SPOT, a Spatial Perception-Oriented VR Teleoperation system for collecting long-horizon humanoid demonstration data by providing extended perceptual horizon. SPOT combines a robot-mounted binocular fisheye camera, a wide-field stereoscopic display, viewpoint-decoupled free-looking, and visual stabilization to provide a robot-centric view that is wide, stable, and actively inspectable. Unlike conventional egocentric interfaces, SPOT decouples visual exploration from robot actuation: the egocentric stereo observation is rendered on a virtual hemisphere around the operator, so natural head rotations change where the operator looks within the wide-field view rather than commanding the robot head, camera, or torso. We evaluate SPOT on perception-critical humanoid data-collection tasks spanning drop recovery, peripheral retrieval, large-workspace bimanual manipulation, fine alignment, and dynamic interaction. SPOT improves efficiency, accuracy, and recovery speed, demonstrating its effectiveness for user-friendly and scalable long-horizon humanoid data collection.

Under ubiquitous teleoperation environments with optically challenging conditions, an interface for tele-operated grasping that combines wearability with precise decoding of hand intentions (hand pose, gestures, and grasping force) is essential. Yet, existing interfaces often fall short in meeting these demands, compromising either the diversity of multiple intentions decoding or wearability. To address this, we developed a novel Multiple Intentions Decoding Human-Machine Interface (MI-DHMI) that integrates high-throughput surface electromyography (sEMG) sensors with hand-mounted and forearm-mounted inertial measurement units (IMUs). This effectively reduces decoding errors caused by sEMG variability during unconstrained upper-limb motions, thereby significantly enhancing robustness. The developed interface provides a new solution of a fully wearable, multiple intentions decoding system, offering effective support for ubiquitous teleoperation and contributing to the advancement of human-machine interaction research.

CosmoH2G: A Hand-to-Gripper Transfer Dataset and Baseline Method for Object Manipulation with Complex Spatial Movements
2026-09-07 cs.RO, cs.CV Top author: Hongxiang Zhao (h=0)
Hongxiang Zhao, Mutian Xu, Zeyu Jin, Yiming Hao, Shuguang Cui
Core Contributions
  • Targets a specific limitation of hand-to-gripper transfer methods: they work for simple, planar tasks but fail on the complex spatial movements (rotations, flips) that real manipulation often needs, and builds a large-scale dataset explicitly biased toward such movement complexity.
  • Collects 6,189 episodes across 1,254 objects with a handheld gripper for direct action mimicry, using a protocol that prioritizes motion complexity over dataset size for its own sake.
  • Identifies that naive end-to-end generation of full gripper pose sequences fails because small trajectory deviations compound rapidly under complex dynamics, motivating a two-stage design: predict sparse keyframes first, then generate the full trajectory conditioned on them.
  • Further mitigates drift by keeping only orientation learned end-to-end while post-optimizing translation using grasping heuristics and kinematic consistency — a hybrid learned/optimized correction rather than trusting the network’s raw output.
Show abstract

Transferring human hand demonstrations to robotic grippers has recently emerged as a cost-effective solution for robot learning. However, existing methods are largely confined to simple, planar tasks and fail to handle complex spatial movements (e.g., intricate trajectories involving rotations or flips) that are essential for robot manipulation. Motivated by this gap, we adopt an implicit, data-driven approach guided by fine-grained hand-pose motions. To this end, we introduce a scalable acquisition pipeline to collect hand-gripper paired demonstrations, governed by a rigorous protocol that prioritizes motion complexity and leverages a handheld gripper for seamless action mimicry. This yields a large-scale paired dataset comprising 6,189 episodes across 1,254 unique objects, exhibiting significantly higher spatial complexity than existing benchmarks. However, learning such complex mappings remains challenging. We observe that naive end-to-end generation of full gripper pose sequences is insufficient, as minor trajectory deviations compound rapidly under intricate dynamics. To address this, we propose a two-stage framework: Stage I predicts sparse gripper keyframes (initial and terminal) to simplify the mapping objective, while Stage II generates the full continuous action sequence conditioned on these keyframes. Furthermore, to mitigate cumulative drift, we keep the gripper's orientation being learned while post-optimizing its translation based on the grasping heuristic and kinematic consistency. In both simulation and real-robot experiments, our framework enables stable and precise hand-to-gripper transfer of complex spatial manipulations, significantly outperforming traditional baselines. Dex-X: Learning Visual-Tactile Dexterous Manipulation From Human Videos with Simulated Interaction
2026-09-07 cs.RO Top author: Ruoqu Chen (h=0)
Ruoqu Chen, Feixiang Ruan, Liu Cao, Zihao Wang, Botian Xu
Core Contributions
  • Targets a specific data gap in learning from human video: videos show contact-rich hand-object interaction but carry no tactile signal, and Dex-X treats simulation as a “tactile completion engine” that reconstructs the interaction physically to synthesize the missing supervision.
  • Distills a teacher policy trained on this simulated visual-tactile data into a deployable student operating on point clouds and real tactile sensing, rather than requiring tactile hardware during the human-video collection stage.
  • Demonstrates zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a dexterous hand-arm platform: the distilled policy reaches 93% success on real-world cube picking and 53% on the harder table-cleaning task, despite the teacher only reaching 65.9% average success in simulation across six task categories.
  • Shows zero-shot generalization to unseen object geometries on picking tasks, suggesting the recovered tactile supervision transfers useful contact structure rather than overfitting to training-object shapes.
Show abstract

Human videos are an abundant source of dexterous manipulation behaviors, but they lack tactile information that is crucial for contact-rich interaction. This raises a fundamental question: can robots learn deployable visual-tactile dexterous manipulation policies from human video demonstrations without robot-side data collection? We present DEX-X, a framework for learning visual-tactile dexterous manipulation from human videos through simulation. Our key insight is that simulation can serve as a tactile completion engine. Given monocular human demonstrations, DEX-X reconstructs hand-object interactions in simulation, where physically grounded contact dynamics provide tactile supervision unavailable in the original videos. Leveraging this recovered tactile information, we train visual-tactile dexterous manipulation policies and distill them into deployable policies operating on point-cloud observations and tactile sensing. We demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a dexterous hand-arm platform across diverse grasping and contact-rich tool-use tasks. The teacher policy achieves 65.9% average success across six task categories in simulation, while the distilled visual-tactile policy achieves 93% success on real-world cube picking and 53% on the challenging table-cleaning task. Zero-shot generalization to unseen object geometries is also observed on object-picking tasks. Our results suggest that simulated interaction is a key bridge between human videos and deployable dexterous manipulation policies, providing the missing physical supervision needed for scalable robot skill learning from Internet-scale human video data.

ICI-VLA: In-Context Imitation with Spatiotemporally Aligned Demonstrations for Vision-Language-Action Models
2026-09-07 cs.RO Top author: Songhua Yang (h=0)
Songhua Yang, Ziyu Liu, Xuetao Li, Ruqi Xiao, Kangxin Zhu
Core Contributions
  • Challenges the default assumption that adapting a VLA policy to new manipulation settings requires additional gradient updates: ICI-VLA fixes the policy at inference time and instead conditions action generation on retrieved in-context demonstrations.
  • Retains the base VLM’s native text-generation interface rather than adopting the action-specific multimodal fusion architectures common in mainstream VLA designs, and trains an RD-Encoder with Dynamic-Time-Warping-mined positives to align retrieved context to the current subtask’s phase and geometry.
  • Introduces Target Action Masking, a context-corruption training objective designed to stop the policy from directly copying the retrieved demonstration’s actions and instead force it to rely on the current observation.
  • Reaches 97.7% average success on LIBERO and 60.4% on RoboTwin 2.0 — 19.3 points above the highest reported RoboTwin 2.0 baseline — and 83.2% across four physical tasks.
Show abstract

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies are commonly adapted to new manipulation settings through additional gradient updates, which limits rapid deployment when task-specific data or compute is scarce. We present ICI-VLA, a training and retrieval framework that equips a text-action VLM with few-shot test-time adaptation through in-context demonstrations. Unlike mainstream VLA designs based on action-specific multimodal fusion, ICI-VLA retains the native text-generation interface. ICI-VLA updates its parameters only during offline training; at inference, the policy remains fixed and conditions action generation on retrieved micro-demonstrations. The framework decomposes long trajectories into short, semantically labeled examples and trains an RD-Encoder with positives mined by Dynamic Time Warping (DTW), aligning the retrieved context with the phase and geometry of the current subtask. We further introduce Target Action Masking, a context-corruption objective designed to reduce direct action copying and increase reliance on the current observation. ICI-VLA reaches average success rates of 97.7% on LIBERO and 60.4% on RoboTwin 2.0, exceeding the highest reported baseline average on RoboTwin 2.0 by 19.3 percentage points. It also achieves 83.2% across four physical tasks. These results indicate that a fixed VLA policy can benefit from conditioning on spatiotemporally aligned demonstrations at test time.

Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Contact-Rich Assembly via Proprioception-Anchored Cross-Modal Pretraining
2026-09-07 cs.RO, cs.AI Top author: Yuhan Wang (h=0)
Yuhan Wang, Yurou Chen, Hongye Jiang, Wenzhao Lian
Core Contributions
  • Observes that in sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich assembly, visual appearance and force/torque readings both drift substantially between domains, but calibrated joint positions and velocities stay comparatively consistent — and builds the whole method around exploiting that asymmetry.
  • Supervises vision and F/T representations by training them to predict proprioceptive state transitions, which implicitly suppresses domain-specific nuisance factors like lighting, texture, and sensor bias since those carry little information about joint motion.
  • Deploys policies trained on the frozen PACE features directly to hardware with no real-world fine-tuning and no object-pose tracking, a stronger sim-to-real claim than approaches that still require some real-world adaptation step.
  • Reaches 93.3% average real-world success across four contact-rich assembly tasks with only a 2.7-point sim-to-real drop, and stays robust to perturbations that substantially degrade pose-based and learned-fusion baselines.
Show abstract

Contact-rich assembly remains challenging because it requires submillimeter spatial accuracy and reliable interpretation of forces during sustained contact. Although simulation-based reinforcement learning offers a scalable training paradigm, discrepancies in visual observations, contact dynamics, and force/torque (F/T) measurements often limit policy transfer. We observe that proprioception is comparatively consistent across domains because calibrated joint positions and consistently computed joint velocities align closely between simulation and hardware. Based on this observation, we present PACE (Proprioception-Anchored Cross-Modal Encoder), which supervises temporal visual and F/T representations by predicting proprioceptive state transitions. Static domain-specific factors, including lighting, texture, and sensor bias, contain little information about joint motion; the proposed objective therefore encourages the encoder to suppress these factors while retaining task-relevant motion cues. Policies trained on frozen PACE features are deployed on hardware without real-world fine-tuning or object-pose tracking. Measuring Language Transfer in Robot Policies: Adding Greek to a Cosmos3 Vision-Language-Action Policy
2026-09-07 cs.RO, cs.AI Top author: Ayoub Kirouane (h=0)
Ayoub Kirouane, Georgios Giaples, Christos Petrocheilos
Core Contributions
  • Studies something other than translation: whether the evaluation methodology used to measure low-resource-language robot-policy performance can itself be trusted, using Greek added to a Cosmos3 VLA stack via machine-rephrased instructions and no architecture changes.
  • Demonstrates several plausible evaluation instruments actively produce false conclusions — a color-histogram metric rewards noise, a single-goal benchmark scores 84.6% on correct Greek versus 82.6% on deliberately wrong instructions, and training loss fails to predict actual Greek task success.
  • On a stricter 90-task discriminative suite with three seeds per arm, finds bilingual training yields a consistent 6.7-7.1 point margin over its control condition and reaches roughly two-fifths of English-level performance, while Greek-only training barely exceeds its own control.
  • Shows the policy overfits to the specific translator’s phrasing, and that training on seven phrasings per task roughly halves this penalty — while warm-starting from a language-adapted world model and unfreezing the text tower both actively hurt performance, a counterintuitive negative result.
Show abstract

Robot foundation models are trained and evaluated predominantly in English, and robot demonstration corpora do not exist for most languages. We study the addition of Greek to an open vision-language-action stack using only machine-rephrased instructions and no architecture changes. The main challenge is measurement rather than translation. Several plausible instruments produce false conclusions: a color-histogram metric rewards noise, a single-goal benchmark scores 84.6% under correct Greek and 82.6% under deliberately wrong instructions, training loss fails to predict Greek success, and single-run comparisons are dominated by seed variation. On a discriminative ninety-task suite with three seeds per arm, a multilingual text tower without Greek demonstrations remains at its wrong-instruction floor, while Greek-only training exceeds its control by at most 2.7 points. Bilingual training yields a consistent 6.7-7.1 point margin over its control and reaches about two fifths of English performance. The policy also overfits the translator's phrasing; training on seven phrasings per task approximately halves this penalty. Warm-starting from a language-adapted world model and unfreezing the text tower both degrade performance. The results support two practical requirements for low-resource robot-policy localization: build a guaranteed null before trusting a metric, and replicate low-resource-language results across seeds.
