Research Landscape

A clear cluster of today's papers is chasing VLA efficiency from different angles rather than assuming a bigger monolithic model is the only path forward. MoWAM drops expensive future-video generation from World Action Models in favor of a compact future-motion abstraction, SkipVLA hands free-space motion to a classical planner and only queries the VLA for contact-rich skills, and GeoAAC uses the geometry of a flow-matching denoising trajectory to decide, per rollout step and without extra training, how much action horizon to commit to. All three reach the same underlying conclusion from different directions: a VLA doesn't need to reason at full cost on every timestep, and the savings can come from architecture, hybrid planning, or inference-time signal rather than a distilled smaller model.

A second theme is squeezing more value out of scarce or fragmented robot data. Improving Cross-embodiment Transfer in LAMs fixes a latent-action leakage problem so motion learned on one robot transfers to another; Universal Navigation Interface sidesteps the need for the target robot entirely by collecting physically-constrained human demonstrations with a rollator walker and smartphone; and Co-VLA applies ADMM consensus optimization so fleets of robots at different sites can train a shared VLA without pooling raw data. Read together, these suggest the field is treating data scarcity and fragmentation as a first-class design constraint rather than something to be solved purely by scaling collection.

A third and connected thread is skepticism toward pure end-to-end learning for anything precision- or contact-critical. Hybrid Residual RL for Book Insertion, Execution-Aware Pre-Execution Ranking, and Compliance for Free all keep a geometric or classical backbone in charge of the bulk of the task and use learning only for the narrow, contact-sensitive residual — echoing SkipVLA's split above. Meanwhile a quieter but persistent group of papers returns to classical estimation rigor: Equivariant Filter Design fixes a covariance-consistency flaw in underwater IEKF navigation, Bayesian Continuum Robot Dynamics extends factor-graph estimation into the dynamic regime, and SLAMSqueezeBench argues that SLAM has been benchmarked in an unrealistic vacuum, isolated from the compute contention it actually faces on edge hardware. Alongside three agricultural robotics papers repurposing Boston Dynamics Spot for weeding and crop mapping, and two safety-critical driving papers (OPTED, AdvScene) moving post-training from open-loop imitation to closed-loop and adversarial refinement, today's batch reads less like a single trend and more like a maturing field consolidating around hybrid, calibrated, and resource-aware designs.

MoWAM: Explicit Future Motion Prediction for Efficient World Action Models
2026-09-17 cs.RO Jingjing Chen (h=37)
Jiayu Wang, Bin Zhu, Yue Yu, Jingjing Chen
Core Contributions
  • Targets the core inefficiency of World Action Models: generating full future video frames at inference is expensive, but earlier attempts to skip generation entirely lose the robustness benefit predicting the future provides.
  • Replaces pixel-level video prediction with a compact future-motion representation while a Mixture-of-Transformer still learns full visual dynamics during training, so the knowledge isn't lost — just made implicit at test time.
  • Turns the compact motion representation into a test-time compute lever: sampling multiple motion/action candidates and re-ranking them with a motion-aware progress verifier, so performance scales with more inference-time samples without retraining.
  • Outperforms representative WAM baselines on LIBERO/LIBERO-Plus and real-robot manipulation, with the largest gains specifically under distribution shift — exactly where implicit-only dynamics models tend to break.
Show abstract

World Action Models (WAMs) improve robot policy learning by incorporating future dynamics, yet explicitly generating future videos at inference introduces substantial computational overhead. Removing future generation improves efficiency, but leaves future dynamics only implicitly encoded in observation features, which can limit robustness under distribution shifts. We propose MoWAM, an efficient WAM that replaces future video generation with explicit future motion prediction. Instead of reconstructing the complete future scene, MoWAM models structured robot motion as a compact abstraction of the future, capturing how the robot is expected to evolve under the current scene and interaction constraints. A Mixture-of-Transformer architecture learns future visual dynamics during training while jointly predicting motion and action, allowing video generation to be removed entirely at inference while retaining an explicit representation of the future. The compact motion representation further enables efficient inference-time scaling by sampling multiple candidates of motion and action pairs and selecting among them with a motion-aware task-progress verifier. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and real-world manipulation tasks demonstrate that MoWAM achieves strong in-distribution performance, improved out-of-distribution robustness, and higher average real-world success than representative WAM baselines. In addition, performance improves as more candidates are explored, demonstrating that explicit future motion provides an effective and efficient basis for inference-time scaling.

Improving Cross-embodiment Transfer in Latent Action Models with Action-Similarity Supervision
2026-09-17 cs.RO cs.AI Yusuke Iwasawa (h=23)
Maxime Alvarez, Renzo Caballero, Tatsuya Matsushima, Yusuke Iwasawa, Yutaka Matsuo
Core Contributions
  • Attacks a specific weakness of Latent Action Models used for cross-embodiment learning: the same physical motion from two different robots can end up encoded as different latents, undermining the shared latent action space.
  • Instead of the common fix (an auxiliary loss predicting ground-truth action from the latent, which re-couples the latent to embodiment specifics), trains latent-action similarity to match ground-truth action-sequence similarity — the ground-truth actions are never directly predicted, so embodiment detail doesn't leak back in.
  • Tested via a genuinely disjoint-task cross-embodiment protocol on RoboTwin 2.0 — two bimanual robots each demonstrate different tasks, and each is evaluated closed-loop only on tasks the other robot demonstrated — a stricter generalization test than typical held-out splits.
  • Predicting latent actions instead of ground-truth actions more than doubles cross-embodiment success under this protocol; computing similarity on end-effector motion (not joint space) and comparing across robots gives the best results overall.
Show abstract

As generalist robot policies gain vision and language from web-scale pretraining, demonstrations remain costly to collect and tied to the robot that recorded them. Latent action models (LAMs) address both by learning latent actions from action-free videos that can be shared across embodiments, however, in practice, LAMs are sensitive to background visual noise, and the same motion from two different robots may be encoded with different latents. One solution to the background visual noise is to add an auxiliary loss predicting the robot action from the latent action, further associating the latent action space to the embodiment specific robot action space. We study a different use of the same labels, through action-similarity supervision. The similarity between any two latent actions is trained to match the similarity of the two ground-truth robot action sequences. The ground-truth actions are never predicted by the LAM, so the latent action does not need to encode embodiment specifics. We evaluate cross-embodiment transfer on RoboTwin 2.0 in a controlled setup, two bimanual robots demonstrate disjoint task sets, a policy is trained on all the demonstrations, and each robot is evaluated closed-loop on the tasks only the other demonstrated. With the policy architecture and its hyperparameters, the dataset, and the evaluation protocol fixed, predicting latent actions instead of ground-truth actions more than doubles cross-embodiment success. Given the same ground-truth actions, similarity supervision transfers better than an auxiliary loss that predicts the ground-truth action during the LAM training. Computing the similarities on end-effector motion rather than joint-space motion, and letting the loss compare latent actions across the two robots, gives the best approach of the study.

GeoAAC: Geometry-Based Adaptive Action Chunking from Denoising Trajectories in VLA Policies
2026-09-17 cs.RO cs.AI cs.LG Yi Bin (h=22)
Xin Chen, Sen Chen, Yujuan Ding, Jian Liu, Guoqing Wang
Core Contributions
  • Identifies that fixed action-horizon chunking in flow-based VLA policies mismatches real rollouts, since different task phases need different amounts of control precision and closed-loop feedback.
  • Uses the geometry of the Flow Matching denoising trajectory itself as a free reliability signal — geometric variation across action prefixes correlates with predictive uncertainty — so the adaptive horizon comes from a single generation pass with no extra training.
  • Builds a "horizon-wise geometric profile" from this signal to decide the action horizon dynamically per rollout step, rather than tuning one fixed horizon for the whole task.
  • Tested across two VLA backbones (GR00T N1.5, π0.5) on four benchmarks including real-world manipulation, with up to 8.7 points simulation improvement and real-world success rising from 53.3% to 74.4% — a large gain for a training-free inference-time change.
Show abstract

Action chunking is widely used for action generation and execution in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, yet existing approaches commonly use a fixed action horizon. During a rollout, different task stages may require different levels of action continuity, control precision, and closed-loop feedback, making a fixed horizon unable to accommodate changing control requirements. We propose GeoAAC, a geometry-based adaptive action chunking method for flow-based VLA policies that adjusts the action horizon according to the reliability of the current action prediction. We show that the geometry of Flow Matching denoising trajectories provides process-level information for characterizing prediction reliability, with geometric variation across action prefixes remaining positively correlated with predictive uncertainty. GeoAAC uses this prefix-wise geometry to construct a horizon-wise geometric profile and adaptively determine the action horizon from a single generation without additional training. Experiments with GR00T N1.5 and pi0.5 on LIBERO, LIBERO-Pro, RoboCasa365, and real-world manipulation tasks show consistent improvements over fixed-action-horizon baselines and existing adaptive methods, including up to 8.7 percentage points in simulation and an increase in average real-world success rate from 53.3% to 74.4%.

Workspace Models: Lightweight Robotic Memory via Saliency-Driven Supervision
2026-09-17 cs.RO cs.AI Pulkit Agrawal (h=18)
Nitish Dashora, Douglas Chen, Idan Shenfeld, John Marangola, Pulkit Agrawal
Core Contributions
  • Addresses the cost of giving manipulation policies long-term memory: prior approaches query a VLM in-the-loop at deployment time to compress history into task-salient information, which is expensive to run live.
  • Moves the expensive VLM reasoning to train time only — a VLM identifies what history matters for the task, distilled into a lightweight "workspace token" via a set-reconstruction decoder loss, so deployment queries the cheap learned token instead of the VLM.
  • The workspace token acts as a drop-in replacement for raw observations at deployment, letting policies solve memory-intensive tasks without any VLM calls in the control loop.
  • Reports the token is not just more efficient but actually improves policy performance over VLM-in-the-loop baselines in both simulation and hardware — compression acting as useful regularization, not just an efficiency compromise.
Show abstract

Complex robotic manipulation tasks frequently require a long-term memory of past events and actions. As conditioning on full histories renders policies prone to spurious correlations and degrades performance, many approaches to policy memory involve compressing historical information through expensive VLM queries in-the-loop to process only task-salient information. In this paper, we propose an alternative approach in which computationally intensive VLM queries are made during train-time to learn a lightweight latent memory that can be efficiently queried at deployment time. Our representation, which we call the workspace token, is trained by (1) using a VLM to identify current and historical information necessary for completing a task, then (2) distilling these into the workspace token using a set-reconstruction decoder loss. In both simulation and hardware, we show that the workspace token can be used as a drop-in replacement for observations during deployment, enabling policies to solve memory-intensive tasks without the need for VLM reasoning in-the-loop. Interestingly, we found that workspace tokens are not only more lightweight but also lead to better policy performance.

Co-VLA: Consensus-based Federated Training for Vision-Language-Action Models
2026-09-17 cs.RO Michael Muehlebach (h=15)
Haolong Li, Guner Dilsad Er, Michael Muehlebach, Joerg Stueckler
Core Contributions
  • Applies federated learning to VLA training specifically because robot data is naturally siloed across robots/tasks/locations and centralizing it is often impractical — a data-governance problem as much as a technical one.
  • Uses ADMM-based consensus optimization rather than naive federated averaging, which matters because robot clients have heterogeneous local data distributions that simple averaging handles poorly.
  • Shows the same consensus algorithm supports both full-model federated training and parameter-efficient fine-tuning with fixed-rank and rank-adaptive adapters — one unified recipe covering multiple deployment budgets.
  • Reports performance comparable to centralized training in both regimes — the key claim that would let organizations collaboratively train shared VLAs without pooling raw robot data.
Show abstract

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for general-purpose robot learning, with performance improving as models and datasets scale. Scaling robot data collection, however, remains challenging because data are naturally distributed across robots, tasks, and locations, making centralization costly or impractical. Federated learning offers a way to train on decentralized robot data, but applying it to VLAs requires accounting for heterogeneous robot client data distributions. We present Co-VLA, which applies consensus optimization using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) to federated VLA training. We show that the same algorithm supports both full-model training and parameter-efficient fine-tuning with both fixed-rank and rank-adaptive adapters. The name Co-VLA reflects both consensus and collaboration: clients with different local robot datasets collaboratively train a shared model without sharing their data. Our experiments demonstrate that Co-VLA achieves performance comparable to centralized training in both full-model training and parameter-efficient fine-tuning settings.

Hybrid Residual Reinforcement Learning for Contact-Rich Robotic Book Insertion
2026-09-17 cs.RO Richard Dazeley (h=28)
Tianyuan Liu, Rutherford Agbeshi Patamia, Benjamin Champion, Akansel Cosgun, Richard Dazeley
Core Contributions
  • Studies a narrow but genuinely hard problem — sliding a grasped book into a tight shelf slot — where millimeter-scale error causes jamming, and asks how much control should be geometric vs. learned rather than assuming end-to-end RL is the answer.
  • Keeps a nominal task-space controller in charge of the insertion/seating motion and lets residual PPO supply only bounded corrections plus the release decision, with just the open-retreat-reclose recovery scripted — a minimal-intervention design.
  • In simulation across 512 fixed conditions, hybrid control reaches 98.5% success versus 37.9% for nominal control alone — a striking gap that shows how much residual correction is worth in tight-tolerance contact tasks.
  • On real xArm7 hardware the gap narrows but persists (63.3% vs 26.7% success, failures cut from 22 to 11 of 30), winning 13 of 15 matched conditions where the two controllers diverge — real transfer, not just simulated gains.
  • Robustness holds to 1.5x initialization perturbation (>87% success), with failures concentrated only at the geometric limit of very tight clearances, giving a clear picture of the approach's boundary.
Show abstract

Placing a grasped book into a tight shelf is a compact but difficult contact-rich control problem: millimetre-scale pose error can turn a geometrically valid approach into jamming, failed release, or incomplete seating. We study this final phase after grasp acquisition and global approach, and ask how control authority should be divided between known geometry and learned behaviour. Our method retains a nominal task-space controller for structured insertion and seating, while residual PPO supplies bounded local corrections and decides when to release. Only the brief open-retreat-reclose transition is scripted. For the final policy used on hardware, a deployment-matched simulation evaluation over 512 fixed conditions yields 98.50 percent mean success (0.23 percentage-point sample SD) across three independent training runs, compared with 37.89 percent for nominal control. On the physical xArm7, 60 trials over 30 matched conditions show the same qualitative advantage: residual control raises success from 26.7 percent to 63.3 percent, reduces failures from 22 to 11, and wins 13 of the 15 matched conditions in which the two controllers differ. Robustness tests show that performance remains above 87 percent under initialization perturbations up to 1.5x, while very tight clearances expose the geometric limit of local correction. These results support a hybrid design in which geometry preserves reliable task structure and learning is concentrated on the contact-sensitive behaviour that fixed rules handle poorly.

Execution-Aware Pre-Execution Ranking for Grasp-Conditioned Robotic Placement
2026-09-17 cs.RO Richard Dazeley (h=28)
Tianyuan Liu, Rutherford Agbeshi Patamia, Benjamin Champion, Richard Dazeley, Akansel Cosgun
Core Contributions
  • Points out that a "geometrically valid" placement can still fail because the chosen grasp changes end-effector pose, collision geometry, and transport motion — so grasp and placement selection can't really be optimized separately.
  • Frames placement as ranking grasp-placement candidates before planning begins, with separate heads for "will planning succeed" and "will execution succeed given planning succeeded" — decomposing failure modes explicitly instead of one monolithic score.
  • On a 1,235-scene, 30-object dataset with scene-group held-out splits, top-1 success improves from 72.84% (full-pool cuMotion baseline) to 85.78% for joint grasp-placement selection.
  • Transfers to real xArm7/MoveIt hardware with zero robot-specific retraining, and of the cases that pass its own Top-5 preflight check and begin execution, 81.25% actually succeed — suggesting its confidence estimates are reasonably well-calibrated.
Show abstract

A geometrically valid placement can still be difficult to execute because the selected grasp changes the required end-effector pose, collision geometry, and transport motion. Placement is formulated as a pre-execution ranking problem in which supplied grasp-placement candidates are scored before planning. The model combines a typed target-conditioned point cloud with three pose descriptors and hierarchical heads for planning success and execution success conditioned on planning. On a 30-object, 1,235-scene dataset with scene-group-held-out splits, three-seed top-1 success on covered test groups reaches 85.63 +/- 1.08% for joint selection and 79.84 +/- 0.16% for fixed-target ranking. For the designated frozen seed-42 checkpoint, top-1 success improves from 72.84% to 85.78% over full-pool cuMotion for joint ranking and from 59.65% to 79.67% for fixed-target ranking. Frozen transfer to xArm7/MoveIt requires no xArm-specific retraining. Across 27 locked cases, 13 complete end to end (48.15%). Of the 16 cases that pass Top-5 preflight and begin execution, 13 succeed (81.25%). Candidate-level deployment-feasibility prediction reaches 81.25% recall, 85.20% specificity, and 83.23% balanced accuracy.

SkipVLA: Skipping VLA Steps with Classical Planning for Fast Robot Manipulation
2026-09-17 cs.RO Zachary K. Kingston (h=15)
Kaivalya Agrawal, Md Ashiqur Rahman, Raymond A. Yeh, Zachary Kingston
Core Contributions
  • Notes that VLA policies remain slow specifically because prior latency-reduction work still runs a learned policy for the entire task, including free-space motion that classical planners already solve quickly and reliably.
  • Hybridizes a pretrained VLA with a classical motion planner: the planner handles free-space transit, and the VLA is only queried for contact-rich skills like grasping and placing — restricting the expensive model to where it's actually needed.
  • Reuses the VLA's own frozen vision-language backbone to predict target poses for the planner, learning this predictor without collecting new demonstrations — leveraging what the large pretrained model already learned.
  • Across 13 LIBERO tasks and three real pick-and-place tasks on a 6-DoF YAM arm, achieves up to 2.5x faster task completion and notably lower energy use while matching the original VLA's task success rate — speed without an accuracy trade-off.
Show abstract

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are a class of generalist robot policies that map camera images and language instructions directly to robot actions. While promising, these models remain slow at test time, particularly for long-horizon tasks that require many queries to the policy. Recent efforts reduce VLA latency by distilling smaller models, overlapping asynchronous action chunks, or pairing the VLA with a fast low-level policy, but still run a learned policy for the entire task. In contrast to VLA, classical motion planners quickly find collision-free motions, but require an explicit goal and have no semantic understanding of the task. In this work, we present SkipVLA, a hybrid policy that combines a pretrained VLA with a classical motion planner, using the planner for free-space motion and querying the VLA only for contact-rich skills such as grasping and placing. SkipVLA reuses the frozen vision-language backbone of the VLA to predict a target pose for each planned motion, and learns this predictor without additional demonstrations introduced into the system by using what was already learnt by the large VLA. We evaluate SkipVLA with three VLAs on 13 LIBERO tasks in simulation and three pick-and-place tasks on a physical 6-DoF YAM arm, demonstrating up to 2.5x faster task completion and significantly lower energy consumption while achieving the same task success rate.

Compliance for Free: Learning Identifiable Impedance via Bilateral Teleoperation
2026-09-17 cs.RO Adrià Colomé (h=14)
Harsha Guda, Adrià Colomé, Carme Torras
Core Contributions
  • Identifies a specific identifiability problem: from pose and force alone, you can't separate an operator's intended equilibrium position from their intended stiffness, which is why standard teleop interfaces can never supply compliance labels no matter how they're instrumented.
  • Solves the gap using four-channel bilateral teleoperation, where the leader arm provides an independent measurement of the intended equilibrium — enabling per-axis stiffness to be identified by regression using only joint-torque sensing already present, at zero extra hardware or annotation cost.
  • Fine-tunes a VLA to output stiffness alongside pose using these per-timestep, direction-dependent compliance labels, rather than relying on hand-specified task structure, privileged simulation state, or dedicated force/tactile sensors as prior work did.
  • On a Franka Research 3 wiping task, it's the only policy among five compared that actually changes contact force when instructed to wipe "firm" vs. "normal" (6.4N → 9.1N RMS, Cohen's d = 0.89, p = 0.023) — direct evidence the compliance signal was learned meaningfully.
Show abstract

Vision-language-action models tell a robot where to move, but not how hard to push. Contact-rich tasks depend on that second quantity, compliance, yet no widely used demonstration interface records it. The obstacle is identifiability as realized pose and measured force cannot separate the operator's intended equilibrium from their stiffness, so VR controllers, SpaceMouse and handheld grippers cannot supply compliance supervision even in principle. Prior compliance-output policies work around this with hand-specified task structure, privileged simulation contact state, or dedicated force and tactile hardware. Four-channel bilateral teleoperation removes the ambiguity directly by using the leader arm as a separate measurement of the intended equilibrium, making per-axis stiffness identifiable by regression using only the joint-torque sensing already on the manipulator. This yields per-timestep, direction-dependent compliance labels at zero annotation cost, which we use to fine-tune a VLA to emit stiffness alongside pose. On a Franka Research 3 wiping task, ours is the only policy of five whose contact force changes when the instruction asks for a firm wipe rather than a normal one (6.4N (normal) to 9.1N (firm) RMS, Cohen's d = 0.89, p = 0.023).

Time-Efficient Iterative Learning Planning for Safety-Critical Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance
2026-09-17 cs.RO Quan Quan (h=28)
Zhiyi Chen, Shuli Lv, Chen Min, Yong Xu, Jian Sun
Core Contributions
  • Extends Iterative Learning Planning, a lightweight traversal planner, to handle dynamic obstacles by pairing it with an anticipatory risk-blended control barrier function (ARB-CBF) rather than bolting on a full replanning stack.
  • The ILP layer learns traversal-speed and steering-bias profiles offline via a fractional-power update tied to local obstacle risk, while ARB-CBF only intervenes at runtime to enforce hard safety guarantees — splitting "what's efficient" from "what's provably safe."
  • Comes with explicit complexity analysis: ILP replanning scales O(kN) in iterations and waypoints, and ARB-CBF execution is linear — a level of algorithmic transparency often missing from learning-based planners aimed at constrained platforms.
  • Real-world and simulation experiments show it beats optimization-based baselines on both time-efficiency and safety with lower compute overhead, aligning with its target use case of onboard-constrained mobile robots.
Show abstract

Autonomous mobile robots require timeefficient planning and safety-critical dynamic obstacle avoidance under constrained onboard computation. While Iterative Learning Planning (ILP) offers lightweight and efficient traversal planning, it lacks explicit mechanisms for dynamic obstacle perception and avoidance. This article extends ILP to safety-critical navigation in dynamic environments by integrating an anticipatory risk-blended control barrier function (ARB-CBF). The extended ILP learns traversal-speed and steering-bias profiles via a fractionalpower update based on local obstacle risk, generating nominal control commands that ARB-CBF modifies at runtime for real-time safety guarantees. Algorithmic analysis demonstrates that the ILP replanning stage scales at O(kN) for k iterations and N waypoints, while ARB-CBF executes with linear complexity. Comprehensive simulations and real-world experiments validate the framework, demonstrating superior temporal efficiency and safety with lower computational overhead compared to optimizationbased baselines, making it highly suitable for resourceconstrained platforms.

SmellDiffusion: Diffusion-Based Quadruped Navigation with Olfactory Scene Graphs
2026-09-17 cs.RO D. Tsetserukou (h=27)
Faith Ogunwoye, Iana Zhura, Hajira Amjad, Timofei Kozlov, Didar Seyidov
Core Contributions
  • Tackles gas-source-finding robotics with an open-vocabulary olfactory scene graph that preserves gas identity, which matters because a robot dispatched to a named leak needs species-specific handling.
  • Identifies that in 28 of 424 simulated source-wind configurations the concentration peak sits over 0.5m from the true source, then fixes it with a geometric gate that detects 9/10 held-out displacements at 0.64 precision, cutting error on displaced cases 60% (1.468m → 0.592m) while only invoking correction on 14/204 cases.
  • Compares diffusion-based trajectory generation against classical gas-guided A* head-to-head rather than assuming the generative approach wins: best-of-ten diffusion roughly matches A* on gas exposure (0.0476 vs 0.0455), while plain A* stays fastest overall — an honest result showing diffusion isn't a free lunch here.

Show abstract

A robot sent to a named gas leak must preserve gas identity, estimate the source, and navigate to the resulting goal. We present SmellDiffusion, a simulation pipeline that represents species-specific gas zones in an open-vocabulary olfactory scene graph and shares the selected goal between classical and diffusion planners. Its key components are a peak-local geometric gate for selective source correction and diffusion-based, gas-guided trajectory generation. Among 424 unique source-wind configurations in solved flow, 28 have a concentration peak displaced more than 0.5m from the source. A source-independent geometric gate, calibrated only on the training split and evaluated at the observed peak, detects 9 of 10 held-out displacements at 0.64 precision. Gating a precomputed forward-matching correction reduces mean error on the displaced cases from 1.468m to 0.592m (60%), using matching for only 14/204 cases. All-case mean error falls from 0.205m to 0.180m. All planners receive the same scene-graph source estimate as their goal. In a controlled comparison, best-of-ten diffusion achieves mean gas exposure comparable to gas-guided A* (0.0476 versus 0.0455). A single diffusion proposal takes 41.7ms, compared with 72.3ms for gas-guided A*, although best-of-ten sequential sampling increases total runtime. Plain A* also reaches the same goal and remains the fastest and shortest-path method. Six matched Gazebo runs give mean robot-to-source errors of 0.39m for A* and 0.31m for diffusion.
