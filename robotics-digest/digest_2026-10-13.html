🔭 Research Landscape

Today's batch is dominated by world-action models (WAMs) becoming general-purpose robot backbones. GreenWAM (#19) is the most ambitious: one model trained across 20 robot families in a shared anatomical action space, serving both as a top WorldArena world model and as a competitive policy on RoboTwin, LIBERO, and CALVIN. UniW(A)M (#22) takes a complementary route, using a mixture-of-experts to share dynamics between world modelling and several WAM interfaces, then routing per action chunk (88.3% vs 76.7% real-world success). LoopWAM (#2) shows WAMs can learn from the full quality spectrum of deployment rollouts, including failures, raising RoboChallenge success from 23.7% to 43.3% over three rounds. ForceWAM (#8) and ILA (#7) bring force and tactile signals into the action/future representation itself. Yet the evaluation papers urge caution: WorldOracle (#14) is the first benchmark that checks world-model rollouts against paired real executions, and #28 shows a learned grid world model (FloWM) can be beaten by a 14 KB Kalman tracker on out-of-view object memory, failing badly on motions outside its built-in velocities.

The second theme is self-improving agentic robotics, appearing in at least five papers. ChunkAct (#1) borrows action chunking from VLAs to cut LLM calls 2.9× while improving success; EmbodiedEvo (#5) and SEAR (#17) let coding agents accumulate reusable Skills/Lessons, and both find that procedural, generic routines transfer while task-specific solutions do not. SEAR adds rigorous controls asking whether agents are really learning or just re-rolling, finding that on easy tasks re-rolling does as well. The survey on Physical RSI (#12) frames the goal formally and concludes that no current work yet achieves verified recursive gains. On the policy-learning side, EqC (#18) uses energy-landscape consistency to decide when to ask for help, and ACCRUE (#30) provides continual off-policy RL with near-zero forgetting, cutting real-robot training time by 31–69%. Read with yesterday's D-World, a clear direction is forming: frozen foundation models plus external, inspectable memory that improves through experience.

Third, data and robustness are increasingly measured, not assumed. LIBERO-RIG (#11) shows nine VLAs drop from 0.93 to 0.51 success under hardware/setup perturbations (camera bumps, control rate, gripper pads) that visual augmentation cannot fix. AXIS-Bench (#4) systematically studies how demonstration quality should enter VLA pretraining, finding quality conditioning beats filtering. Human-data pipelines (EgoExplorer #3, EgoMatrix #15, Steertuning #20) compete on fidelity and efficiency, with Steertuning reaching 71% across 12 new home environments from 5 human demos and 2 minutes of fine-tuning. RoboFQE (#21) offers a rollout-free way to rank policies from offline data, sidestepping the compounding errors that WorldOracle-style evaluations expose in video world models.

GreenWAM: A World Foundation Model for Robotics

📅 2026-10-13cs.RO👤 Gennady Sidorov (h=n/a)
Gennady Sidorov, Valerii Kornilov, Denis Grankin, Polina Fedotova, Aleksey Postnikov
Core Contributions
  • A single world foundation model serving as both world model and policy across 20 robot families, via a shared anatomical action space in common joint coordinates.
  • Joint training in visual, action-conditioned, and world-action modes; policies split an action expert off the generator.
  • Leads WorldArena 1.0 on trajectory, depth, and 3D accuracy; ranks near the top on RoboTwin 2.0 and gets the best zero-shot LIBERO-plus result among compared methods.
  • On a real humanoid it beats policies adapted from π0.5 and Cosmos 3 Edge, and can be distilled to a single-step real-time policy.
Show abstract
A robot needs a world model that predicts how its actions will change the scene and a policy that chooses those actions. Models are usually built and evaluated for only one of these roles, and every robot records its actions in its own format. We ask whether one robot world model can serve both roles across different bodies, and what makes this work. We present GreenWAM, a world foundation model for robotics, together with the recipe that produces it: (1) a shared anatomical action space that describes 20 robot families, from bimanual arms to dexterous humanoids, in common joint coordinates; (2) joint training on these families in visual, action-conditioned and world-action modes; and (3) policies that split an action expert off the generator. As a world model, GreenWAM attains top-tier trajectory, depth and 3D accuracy on WorldArena 1.0, ahead of every world-action model and video generator in our comparison. As a policy, it scores among the top entries of the RoboTwin 2.0 leaderboard, reaches the highest zero-shot LIBERO-plus success among the compared methods and is competitive with dedicated vision-language-action and world-action policies on CALVIN and LIBERO. We further adapt GreenWAM to a real humanoid, where it succeeds more often than policies adapted from $π_{0.5}$ and from Cosmos 3 Edge, and distill it into a single-step policy for real-time control. Controlled experiments yield three findings: robot specialization improves world prediction and control together; an action expert helps when split off at the start of adaptation; and keeping world-model modes during policy adaptation preserves robustness to scene changes.

UniW(A)M: A Unified Mixture-of-Experts Framework for World and World-Action Models

📅 2026-10-13cs.ROcs.AIcs.CVcs.LG👤 Jingbo Xu (h=n/a)
Jingbo Xu, Jianjie Fang, Zhuohang Li, Zihao Song, Zhaolu Wang
Core Contributions
  • Unifies world models and multiple world-action model variants in one diffusion-Transformer MoE, so prediction and control share learned dynamics.
  • Shared experts hold common dynamics while reusable capability experts give each interface its own conditional pathway.
  • A plug-in router selects the best WAM interface per execution chunk with the backbone frozen, raising OOD success from 63.3% to 76.7%.
  • 88.3% real-world success vs 76.7% for the strongest baseline, and co-training with world modelling improves every action interface.
Show abstract
World models (WMs) and world-action models (WAMs) focus on future world prediction and executable action generation, respectively. Although these models learn shared physical dynamics and interaction semantics from embodied trajectories, they are typically trained in isolation, which restricts knowledge reuse between prediction and control. The central challenge is therefore to develop a unified learning framework that shares these dynamics while preserving the distinct modeling capabilities required by each paradigm. We propose UniW(A)M, a mixture-of-experts world-(action) model built upon diffusion Transformers that unifies action-conditioned world modeling and diverse WAM variants. Shared experts capture common dynamics, while atomic capability experts are reused across interfaces so that each interface keeps its own conditional pathway. At deployment, a plug-in adaptive interface router (PAIR) learns from a small subset of target-task chunk scenarios to select a suitable WAM interface for each execution chunk, balancing task success and execution efficiency while keeping the backbone frozen. We evaluate UniW(A)M on LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, and three real-world manipulation tasks, where it achieves 88.3% average real-world success versus 76.7% for the strongest baseline. On WorldArena, it leads action-conditioned world modeling on all three tasks. AC-WM co-training contributes to action generation on every action interface and PAIR raises OOD success from 63.3% to 76.7%.

LoopWAM: Closed-Loop Generalist Policy Improvement from Deployment Rollouts

📅 2026-10-13cs.ROcs.CV👤 Wenxuan Guo (h=n/a)
Wenxuan Guo, Yingping Liang, Zhaoguo Wang, Ziyuan Li, Angyuan Ma
Core Contributions
  • Learns from all deployment rollouts, successes, suboptimal runs, and failures, from itself and other policies, rather than filtering to expert-quality data.
  • Source-dependent noise sampling unifies action learning and action-conditioned world modelling; quality steering keeps video supervision from failures while down-weighting their actions.
  • Three deploy-and-train rounds lift RoboChallenge Table30-V2 success from 23.7% to 43.3%; shirt folding rises from 13.2% to 55.6% in two rounds.
  • Also reaches 94.5% on RoboTwin 2.0 and 49.5% on RoboCasa365 without extra embodied pretraining, showing failures are useful training signal for world models.
Show abstract
Scaling expert demonstrations alone does not enable generalist robot policies to improve from deployment. Real-world rollouts contain successes, suboptimal behavior, and failures: imitating every recorded action can reinforce poor behavior, while filtering out failures discards evidence of physical interactions and their consequences. We introduce \textbf{LoopWAM}, a closed-loop world-action model that learns directly from this full quality spectrum, using rollouts from both itself and other deployed policies. Source-dependent noise sampling unifies behavior learning and action-conditioned world modeling, while quality steering and quality-weighted supervision retain full video supervision across qualities and regulate action imitation. Video and action tokens interact throughout parallel denoising, allowing learned consequences to inform action generation directly. On RoboChallenge Table30-V2, three deployment-and-training rounds raise success from 23.67\% to 43.33\%, a 19.7-percentage-point gain, with a final score of 56.80. LoopWAM also achieves 94.5\% average success on RoboTwin~2.0 and 49.5\% overall success on RoboCasa365 without additional embodied pretraining, obtaining the best overall results among the compared methods across all three benchmarks. On challenging shirt folding task, two rounds of deployment-driven improvement after single-task post-training raise success from 13.20\% to 55.56\%. These results demonstrate closed-loop improvement from mixed-quality experience under both multi-task learning and single-task post-training. \href{https://loop-wam.github.io/}{\textcolor{orange}{Project Page}}

ForkCast: Predictive Recovery for Vision-Language-Action Policies via Counterfactual Futures

📅 2026-10-13cs.RO👤 Chenru Wen (h=n/a)
Chenru Wen, Huajian Zeng, Haokai Ding, Jian Kang, Anqing Duan
Core Contributions
  • Decides whether and how to intervene in a frozen VLA by comparing predicted futures of three options: continue (Commit), move the camera (Reobserve), or replan the arm (Replan).
  • A candidate-conditioned world model plus a task-progress head replaces separate failure detectors and hand-set thresholds.
  • Raises pooled success under injected occlusion and slip from 13.3% (frozen π0.5) to 30.0% on RoboTwin 2.0.
  • Transfers to two other VLA backbones without retraining and improves real-robot success after adaptation.
Show abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies perform manipulation tasks using camera images and language instructions, but object slip or visual occlusion can disrupt execution. Recovery is typically learned during policy training or delegated to a monitor that triggers a scripted repair after detecting a failure. We introduce ForkCast, a predictive recovery framework that compares counterfactual futures to determine whether and how to intervene in a frozen VLA policy. At each decision point, ForkCast evaluates three candidates: executing the policy's nominal action chunk (Commit), adjusting the camera viewpoint (Reobserve), and replanning the arm trajectory (Replan). A candidate-conditioned world model forecasts the latent outcome of each candidate at a common future horizon, and a shared task-progress head scores the predicted outcomes. By evaluating the policy's own continuation alongside the repair candidates, a single comparison based on task progress determines both whether and how to intervene, without a failure detector, an intervention threshold, or updates to the VLA backbone. On five simulated RoboTwin 2.0 tasks with injected occlusion and object slip, ForkCast increases pooled success from 13.3% for the frozen $π_{0.5}$ policy to 30.0% and transfers to two additional VLA backbones without updating ForkCast. After adaptation on real-robot candidate rollouts, ForkCast also improves success over the base policy under both clean and visibility-limited conditions. Code will be released to benefit the research community.

Model-Based Online Decision Making via Generative Trajectory Planning

📅 2026-10-13cs.LGcs.AIcs.ROeess.SY👤 Haldun Balim (h=n/a)
Haldun Balim, Yilun Du, Na Li
Core Contributions
  • Uses a diffusion model as a proposal distribution over action sequences for model-based planning, not as the policy itself.
  • Samples candidate sequences from the prior and refines them with learned dynamics, reward, and value models, avoiding high-dimensional optimization from scratch.
  • Matches or exceeds model-free, diffusion-policy, and model-based baselines on continuous control and long-horizon manipulation.
  • Stays stable in regimes where strong baselines degrade, offering a cleaner way to combine generative models with planning.
Show abstract
Online decision-making faces a tension between efficient inference and long-horizon reasoning. Value-based methods rely on short-horizon bootstrapping that limits the propagation of long-term information, while trajectory optimization methods plan explicitly but must solve a high-dimensional optimization problem at every decision step. We propose Generative Trajectory Planning (GTP), a model-based framework that addresses this tension by learning a generative model as a proposal distribution over action sequences. Instead of optimizing sequences from scratch, GTP samples candidate sequences from a diffusion prior and refines them using learned dynamics, reward, and value models, enabling efficient trajectory-level reasoning. Across continuous control and long-horizon manipulation tasks, GTP matches or exceeds state-of-the-art model-free, diffusion-policy, and model-based baselines, and remains stable in regimes where strong baselines degrade. Our results suggest that generative models are best understood not as policies, but as structured proposal distributions for planning, offering a new perspective on how to integrate generative modeling with model-based decision-making.

Grid or Nodes? Probing Object Memory in World Models Under Partial Observability

📅 2026-10-13cs.ROcs.CVcs.LG👤 Justin McMillen (h=n/a)
Justin McMillen, Yasin Yilmaz
Core Contributions
  • Compares a learned grid world model (FloWM) with an oracle-fed Kalman tracker for remembering objects that leave the camera view.
  • Shows that probe choice alone moves FloWM's out-of-view recall from 66% to 89%, a caution for world-model memory evaluations.
  • The tracker matches FloWM out of view using 14 KB vs 5.6 MB, and keeps 93% on diagonal motion where FloWM falls to 11% even after fine-tuning.
  • Argues for object-centric node memory, while noting a learned version must still replace the oracle and handle noise, long gaps, and bounces.
Show abstract
A robot's world model must remember objects that leave the camera's view. FloWM, a learned world model, keeps them in a spatial grid of features that it moves with five built-in velocities. A node memory instead keeps each object's position and velocity as numbers. We compare them in FloWM's own block world, with an oracle-fed Kalman tracker as the node memory. How the grid is read changes its score: a single-cell probe finds 66% of out-of-view blocks, while FloWM's own probe 89%. FloWM also scores 8 points higher in view although no frame is shown, which we attribute to its write-back of its own predictions. Out of view, the tracker reaches FloWM's range in 14KB against 5.6MB, with no probe. On diagonal motion, outside its five velocities, FloWM finds 11% of blocks even after fine-tuning, while the unchanged tracker keeps 93%. Faster blocks defeat both until one more allowed speed takes the tracker to 94%, where FloWM's built-in shifts would need 25 copies of its grid. A learned node memory must still replace the oracles and handle velocity under noise, long gaps, and the bounce.

ChunkAct: Action Chunking for Agentic Robotics

📅 2026-10-13cs.ROcs.AIcs.CLcs.CV👤 Bingxin Xu (h=n/a)
Bingxin Xu, Wenrui Bao, Xinxin Liu, Jiawei Zhou, Yuzhang Shang
Core Contributions
  • Observes that once an LLM agent picks a sub-goal, the following primitives are routine, so reasoning over the full history at every primitive is wasted compute.
  • Introduces action chunking for agentic robotics: one LLM call launches a chain of primitives that runs autonomously and returns control only on failure.
  • Offline compilation of memory into chunks makes test-time execution memory-free, so LLM cost scales with decisions rather than primitives.
  • On LIBERO-Pro: +5.0 pts success with 2.9× fewer LLM calls and 4.4× fewer input tokens than the prior agentic SoTA.
Show abstract
Agentic robotics places a large language model (LLM) agent in the robot control loop, where it composes perception, motion, and vision-language-action (VLA) tools under the guidance of a memory and thereby generalizes to new tasks without retraining. This generality comes at a steep price in efficiency. Every primitive requires a round of LLM reasoning over the full interaction history, so the cost of an episode grows with the number of primitives. We find this reasoning largely redundant: once a sub-goal is chosen, its primitives follow a routine already prescribed by memory. We present ChunkAct, which introduces action chunking to agentic robotics. Online, a single call launches a chunk of chained primitives, which executes autonomously and returns control only upon failure. Offline, ChunkAct compiles the memory into these chunks, so execution is memory-free at test time and the planner is reserved for decisions that require reasoning. LLM computation therefore scales with the number of decisions rather than the number of primitives. On LIBERO-Pro, ChunkAct improves task success by 5.0 points over the state of the art in agentic robotics while requiring 2.9x fewer LLM calls, 4.4x fewer input tokens, and 2.3x fewer output tokens.

EmbodiedEvo: Self-improving Coding Agents for Physical Intelligence

📅 2026-10-13cs.RO👤 Wenyi Li (h=n/a)
Wenyi Li, Yizhe Chi, Youjie Zheng, Chunyu Liu, Xiaoqiu Wang
Core Contributions
  • Treats code as both the policy and the agent's workspace, extending SWE-agent-style inspect/edit/execute loops to physical tasks.
  • The agent evolves reusable Skills and Lessons on a single Playground scene, then freezes this harness for unseen scenes.
  • Sets a new best on RoboDojo-Sim (score 44.48, 37.8% success); Skills/Lessons match raw-history performance at 53% lower execution cost.
  • Experience transfers down the model scale: lessons from a stronger model raise weaker executors' success by 5.5–8.9 pts.
Show abstract
Bringing general-purpose agents into the physical world calls for an executable representation of physical intelligence. Compared with vision-language-action (VLA) models, which map observations and language instructions directly to actions, code makes control logic explicit and reusable. Code can serve not only as a policy representation but also as the workspace in which the agent solves a task. Rather than following a prescribed workflow of planning, execution, and verification, the agent can autonomously inspect the state, revise and execute code, and use feedback to choose the next step. The success of this SWE-style loop in digital environments motivates its extension to physical tasks. Yet physical execution remains prone to recurring failures. To retain and refine experience for later scenes, we introduce EmbodiedEvo, where a coding agent evolves reusable Skills and Lessons through feedback on a single Playground scene, then freezes the resulting harness for diverse new scenes. On RoboDojo-Sim, EmbodiedEvo achieves an average score of 44.48 and a success rate of 37.82\%, surpassing all officially verified results. Paired tests show the largest gains on memory-dependent tasks and successful reuse of coordinated procedures, while fine contact control remains difficult. Experience from stronger models also improves weaker executors: Astra's experience raises Sol and Luna's success rates by 8.86 and 5.50 percentage points over no experience. Compared with raw interaction history, Skills and Lessons achieve comparable task performance at 53.0\% lower execution cost.

SEAR: Self-Evolving Agentic Robotics

📅 2026-10-13cs.ROcs.AIcs.CL👤 Xinyi Wang (h=n/a)
Xinyi Wang, Bangzheng Li, Hanyu Wang, Lin Zhang, Andrea Yaoyun Cui
Core Contributions
  • Asks whether self-evolving robot agents truly learn or just get more attempts, adding controls for retries, task familiarity, privileged state, budget, and luck.
  • Covers three modes (code-as-policy, agent-as-policy, auto-research VLA fine-tuning) across 7 frontier LLMs, 330 sim and 5 real tasks, and over four years of agent runtime.
  • Learning curves depend on budget and task difficulty: on easy tasks re-rolling short attempts does as well as evolving.
  • Generic control code transfers across tasks and simulators while task-specific solutions do not; a text-only model keeps pace with a comparable multimodal one.
Show abstract
Frontier language-model agents can now improve robots on their own: they write control code, test it, and keep what works as skills and memories for the next task. We introduce SEAR (Self-Evolving Agentic Robotics), a framework and benchmark built around two questions. Is the agent really learning, or just trying more often? And does what it learns still help on new tasks, in a new simulator, and on a real robot? The framework runs an agent in three modes: it writes code that becomes the policy (code-as-policy), acts live in the control loop (agent-as-policy), or works as a researcher that fine-tunes a vision-language-action model (auto-research). The benchmark adds controls that separate retained experience from repeated attempts, familiar tasks, privileged simulator state, larger budgets, and luck. Across seven frontier language models, 330 simulated and five physical tasks, and over four years of agent runtime, we highlight three findings. First, the learning curve of a robotic agent depends both on the overall time budget and the task difficulty relative to model capability. Harder tasks make longer sessions worthwhile; on easier tasks, re-rolling short attempts does just as well. Second, control code and shared routines transfer across tasks and simulators, while task-specific solutions do not. On a real robot, we observe a performance gap between agent-designed practice and novel human-designed scenes. Third, different models evolve more effectively in different ways. Some do best acting live, others writing code first. And a text-only model keeps pace with a comparable multimodal one: perception helps with error assessment, but agentic reasoning and tool use can matter more. We release the benchmark and codebase to support future research.

Who Improves the Robot? Toward Physical Recursive Self-Improvement

📅 2026-10-13cs.AIcs.LGcs.RO👤 Hai Zhou (h=n/a)
Hai Zhou, Fan Yang, Zeyi Chen, Lejun Zhang, Chenyang Fu
Core Contributions
  • Defines Physical Recursive Self-Improvement: physical experience updates both a robot's capabilities and its improvement procedure.
  • Proposes an L0–L5 ladder separating capability vs meta-level updates and within- vs across-task gains, reserving L5 for independently verified recursive gain.
  • Concludes that no surveyed work yet demonstrates Physical RSI as defined, a useful reality check amid self-improvement claims.
  • Sets research priorities: reliable feedback, causal attribution, safety preservation, and benchmarks.
Show abstract
Robots are increasingly autonomous in task execution, yet their improvement remains largely human-driven. This study investigates Physical Recursive Self-Improvement (Physical RSI), in which physical experience guides lasting updates to both a robot's capabilities and its improvement procedure, making subsequent improvement more effective across tasks. Physical RSI faces challenges from noisy observations, continuous dynamics, costly trials, and irreversible consequences. We present an L0-L5 framework that differentiates capability-level from meta-level updates and within-task from across-task gains, reserving L5 for independently verified recursive gain. We study existing methods around harness adaptation and model training, alongside safety and control mechanisms that govern execution and successive updates. Our analysis reveals that, despite substantial progress in self-improvement, the surveyed literature does not yet demonstrate Physical RSI as defined by our framework. We identify research priorities in reliable feedback, causal attribution, safety preservation, and benchmarking, outlining a path toward robots that autonomously improve how they learn while ensuring safety. The GitHub repository for Physical RSI is available at https://github.com/SAIL-Research-Lab/Physical-RSI.

AutoAgent0: An Agentic Runtime for Safe Closed-Loop Driving

📅 2026-10-13cs.RO👤 Jiageng Liu (h=n/a)
Jiageng Liu, Seth Z. Zhao, Zewei Zhou, Alejandro Huerta, Aidan Bayer-Calvert
Core Contributions
  • Frames closed-loop driving safety as a test-time execution problem rather than a training problem.
  • Wraps existing end-to-end driving experts with an independent detection module, a rule-based trajectory verifier, and an agentic recovery planner.
  • When nominal trajectories fail verification, the runtime composes alternatives from action primitives and refines them using verifier feedback.
  • Improves closed-loop robustness on Fail2Drive and NavSafe-∞ across multiple experts without retraining any of them.
Show abstract
Safe autonomous driving requires more than accurate planning at a single frame: it demands reliable closed-loop execution in dynamic environments. Although end-to-end driving policies have advanced rapidly, the accumulated closed-loop errors, especially in unseen scenarios, can push vehicles into states from which the same policy struggles to recover. We address the challenge by treating closed-loop driving safety as a test-time execution problem. We introduce AutoAgent0, an agentic runtime and harness framework that augments existing driving experts with complementary detection skills, rule-based action verification, and feedback-driven recovery. To address inaccurate scene perception, an independent detection module constructs scene context using complementary perception skills, and a rule-based verifier checks proposed trajectories against this context before execution. When the nominal trajectories are unreliable, the orchestrator of the runtime activates Recovery Planning, where an agentic loop constructs alternative action proposals from action primitives and iteratively refines them using verification feedback. This design combines independent scene assessment with feasibility checks and online recovery, addressing both perception errors and the limited recovery capabilities of individual experts. Evaluations on Fail2Drive and NavSafe-$\infty$ demonstrate improved closed-loop robustness over single-expert execution in long-tail and safety-critical scenarios. These results demonstrate that runtime supervision can improve closed-loop robustness across multiple driving experts without retraining the underlying policies.

EqC: Equilibrium Energy Control for Introspective Lifelong Robot Learning

📅 2026-10-13cs.RO👤 Haodong Yang (h=n/a)
Haodong Yang, Runhan Huang, Bowen Zhang, Iofai Lei, Zunzhe Zhang
Core Contributions
  • Gives generative policies an explicit energy landscape so they can tell when their own behavior is no longer well supported.
  • Action refinement is cast as equilibrium-seeking optimization; the geometry of refinement provides a competence signal.
  • Energy-Gated DAgger triggers expert takeover only on persistent energy inconsistency, collecting recovery data near the competence boundary.
  • Works with both Diffusion Transformer and flow-matching VLA policies, progressively expanding autonomy in real bimanual tasks.
Show abstract
Long-term robot autonomy depends on more than effective action generation: a policy must recognize when its behavior is no longer sufficiently supported and use assistance to improve. We introduce Equilibrium Energy Control (EqC), a framework for introspective lifelong robot learning built around an explicit conditional energy landscape. EqC casts generative action refinement as equilibrium-seeking optimization, while the geometry of the refinement process reveals whether the generated behavior remains consistent with the learned landscape. This competence signal enables Energy-Gated DAgger, where persistent energy-consistency violations trigger expert takeover and the resulting recovery trajectories are aggregated for subsequent policy updates. EqC integrates naturally into both Diffusion Transformer policies and flow-matching-based vision-language-action (VLA) policies. Across simulation and real-world bimanual manipulation, EqC matches or improves control performance and robustness while enabling timely intervention near the policy's competence boundary. A real-world study further shows how these interventions progressively expand the range of situations handled autonomously, providing a practical path from static generative controllers toward self-improving robot systems.

ACCRUE: Continual Off-Policy Reinforcement Learning for Robot Foundation Models

📅 2026-10-13cs.ROcs.AIcs.LG👤 Changyeon Kim (h=n/a)
Changyeon Kim, Kimin Lee, Yashraj Narang, Yijie Guo
Core Contributions
  • Continual off-policy RL on top of robot foundation models that both learns new tasks efficiently and retains old ones.
  • Carries lightweight actor and critic networks across tasks, reusing value knowledge, while distilling the actor toward cached targets on a few retained episodes.
  • Fixed visual features keep the inherited learner and replay compatible even when the base policy is updated.
  • Near-zero forgetting on contact-rich assembly sequences and 31.0–68.5% less real-robot training time.
Show abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training improves robot foundation models (RFMs) beyond their demonstrations, but real-world deployment demands more: learning new tasks efficiently while retaining previously acquired behaviors. Existing continual RL methods on top of VLAs mitigate forgetting but rely on on-policy updates with limited forward transfer, whereas off-policy methods improve single-task sample efficiency without addressing learner persistence across tasks. We propose ACCRUE, a continual off-policy RL framework that carries lightweight actor and critic networks across tasks, allowing each stage to reuse behavioral and value knowledge acquired through prior RL. LLM2R achieves the highest part accuracy and success rate on all three PartNet categories, the best rotation error, translation error, and part accuracy on PartNeXt, and the lowest translation error and fastest inference on Breaking Bad, where only a fracture-specialized method is more accurate. Its advantage grows with the number of parts: at 100 parts it exceeds GARF by 33.5 points in part accuracy on Breaking Bad and Assembler by 29.1 points on PartNet, and it transfers zero-shot to unseen categories with more than 16 points higher part accuracy than the strongest of three baselines.

Show abstract
Recent advances in zero-shot voice cloning systems make it possible to reproduce a recognizable voice from a short recording and attach it to arbitrary virtual characters. Prior work suggests that familiar vocal identity can carry over associated personality impressions to new targets such as robots or virtual characters. However, these works have mainly examined familiarity and personalization effects rather than directly testing whether a cloned familiar voice systematically transfers personality impressions to an embodied virtual character. Such transference raises the concern that cloned voices could be used to manipulate people into engaging with virtual characters as capable social actors. Across two studies, we investigated whether personality impressions transfer when a virtual character speaks with the cloned voice of a familiar person. Study 1 examined this question in a real-world university setting using the cloned voice of a lecturer familiar to the participating students. Study 2 examined the same question online using voices of social media content creators and members of their communities. Drawing on the concepts of identity activation, trait transfer, and person reconstruction, we show that evidence of personality transfer was limited in both studies. Observed effects were selective and did not generalize across traits or study contexts. In the online study in particular, recognizable voice identity cues activated expectations about the speaker but did not reconstruct the broader person impression. Qualitative responses further indicated that judgments were frequently shaped by perceived artificiality and by whether voice and appearance fit together. Our findings moderate common concerns that cloned voices straightforwardly manipulate people into perceiving artificial characters as capable social actors.
