🔭 Research Landscape

Today's batch is dominated by one question: what should a robot foundation model predict, and what should it be grounded in before it acts? Five papers extend the World Action Model (WAM) recipe of jointly generating video and actions, each adding a different missing ingredient. AW-0 (#9) adds executable code as a third generative stream so skills become reusable programs; PhysiCalWorld (#10) adds a one-shot physics descriptor so rollouts stop drifting on unseen mass or friction; Relational Geometry Forcing (#18) shows that aligning pointwise 3D features actually hurts action learning and that pairwise relations are the fix; CW-WAM (#25) adds contact wrench as a predicted modality; and DepState-JEPA (#30) grounds a latent state in metric depth. Read together, they say that pixels alone are an under-constrained target, and the field is converging on adding structure (programs, physics, geometry, force) at training time while keeping inference cheap.

The second theme is the post-deployment life of a policy. TIDE (#13), SimPT (#20), ACT-Flow (#15) and ASTEROID (#3) all try to absorb corrections or exploration without a heavy actor-critic stack: provenance-conditioned supervised learning from human interventions, simulator-compiled progress graphs that automate those interventions, a critic used only to weight residual targets during training, and privileged-teacher distillation on the student's own on-policy history. Post-hoc kernel-variance heads (#6) supply the missing trigger, telling a single VLA when it is out of distribution at roughly 50x lower scoring cost than ensembles, and FlowVLA (#21) shows the flip side: some tasks like pouring need an explicit physical-completion estimate that a vision-only policy cannot see. The shared bet is that supervised objectives on well-chosen data can replace much of online RL.

A third thread is skepticism about what success metrics reveal. NavBind (#23) finds that higher navigation success does not mean a VLA follows its instruction, the PAPAV survey (#2) counts that only 5 of 64 agent benchmarks assess Anticipate and 3 assess Verify, TARSphere (#17) finds general MLLMs can beat embodied-tuned ones, and the thermal-HRI study (#22) documents hallucinated actions. Meanwhile the agentic-harness papers (SERA #28, CausGround #12, the recursive self-improvement position paper #11) push the opposite direction, treating a frozen VLM or VLA as a component to be wrapped in editable memory, causal knowledge and self-revision. Expect the next round of work to pair these: harnesses that improve, evaluated by diagnostics that test whether the improvement is real.

Agentic World Action Models

2026-10-21 cs.RO 👤 Roei Herzig (h=24)
Zhuoyang Liu, Dantong Niu, Botong Zhang, Boning Shao, Jing Wang
Core Contributions
  • Fuses two usually separate ideas: world action models give physical foresight but no compositional reuse, while code-based agents give reuse but no predictive grounding. AW-0 models code, future video and actions as three coupled streams in one mixture-of-transformers.
  • Code plays a dual role, structuring multi-step prediction inside the world model and storing skills as parameterized routines that can be reused later.
  • Introduces an automated curation pipeline that labels robot and human videos with aligned programs and consolidates recurring routines into a skill library.
  • Improves over VLA and WAM baselines on RoboTwin 2.0 and real bimanual tasks, with gains in language following, semantic reasoning and robustness.
Show abstract
Recent world action models provide visual foresight and future prediction for robot manipulation, but lack explicit mechanisms for compositional skill reuse. Agentic robotics combines code-based planning and reasoning with reusable skills, but often lacks predictive grounding in physical dynamics. We introduce AW-0, an agentic world action model framework that integrates code-based planning with joint video and action prediction. We jointly model code, future video, and robot actions as three coupled generative streams within a unified mixture-of-transformers architecture. Within this architecture a code expert generates parameterized skill routines and control logic while video and action experts generate future visual states and low-level motor commands. Code serves two complementary roles: it structures multi-step prediction and control within the world model and represents skills as reusable programs for subsequent agentic execution. We further introduce an automated curation pipeline that labels robot and human video trajectories with aligned programs and consolidates recurring routines into reusable skill libraries. Evaluations on RoboTwin 2.0 and physical bimanual manipulation tasks show improved task performance over VLA and WAM baselines, alongside gains in language following, semantic reasoning and robustness, demonstrating that agentic code enables connection between planning, prediction and execution within WAMs.

PhysiCalWorld: In-Context Physics Calibration for World Models

2026-10-21 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CV 👤 Nima Fazeli (h=21)
Yunhao Luo, Wentao He, Yinpei Dai, Hyelin Nam, Hongze Fu
Core Contributions
  • Diagnoses why world models drift on novel scenes: mass distribution, joint damping and friction are not visible in a single frame, so predictions regress to the training prior.
  • Calibrates by in-context prompting: a short exploratory interaction clip is encoded once into a compact physics-descriptor token that conditions later predictions, with no fine-tuning or analytical physics model.
  • Trains end to end with the latent prediction loss alone, with no physics labels, yet the descriptors form a physically structured latent space that generalizes beyond the training ranges.
  • Improves rollout accuracy and visual planning success across four environments, and since the token is extracted once, the inference overhead is negligible.
Show abstract
Existing world models typically perceive a scene through current visual frame or a narrow history window. However, these observations may not reveal the physics properties that determine transition dynamics, such as an object's mass distribution, joint damping, or surface friction. Without calibrating these physics factors for world models, predictions tend to regress toward an empirical prior and induce severe drift in novel test scenes. To this end, we propose PhysiCalWorld, which calibrates action-conditioned world models to the instance-specific dynamics via in-context prompting, without fine-tuning or analytical physics modeling. Our method encodes an exploratory interaction clip collected within the test scene into a compact physics descriptor token, which grounds subsequent predictions in the scene's actual physical behavior. PhysiCalWorld is trained end-to-end with the latent prediction loss alone, without physics labels. At inference time, the descriptor token is extracted once and can be reused across rollouts with negligible compute overhead. We evaluate PhysiCalWorld across four environments covering diverse physics variations and show that it substantially improves rollout prediction accuracy and visual planning success rate. The learned descriptors also form a physically structured latent space and generalize beyond the physics ranges seen during training.

Relational Geometry Forcing for World Action Models

2026-10-21 cs.RO 👤 Jian-Hua Yang (h=16)
Yuan Xu, Yixiang Chen, Qisen Ma, Jiabing Yang, Peiyan Li
Core Contributions
  • Finds that the common approach of pointwise alignment to a 3D foundation model helps video features but degrades action learning, because dense geometric targets over-constrain compact action features.
  • Relational Geometry Forcing aligns pairwise feature relations instead, with three terms: intra-frame, cross-view, and action-temporal relations that tie action dynamics to scene geometry evolution.
  • It is applied only at training time, with no extra geometry branch or inference module, so deployed cost is unchanged.
  • Gives consistent gains across diverse manipulation scenarios and WAM paradigms in simulation and the real world.
Show abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) have shown strong potential for robot manipulation by jointly learning visual dynamics and executable actions. However, their video and action objectives do not explicitly constrain the 3D geometry that is crucial for precise manipulation. Recently, geometry-aware WAMs introduce depth or 4D prediction branches, whereas representation alignment methods supervise intermediate WAM features with pretrained 3D foundation models. Yet we find that such pointwise alignment benefits the video features but degrades action learning, since dense geometric feature targets overly restrict compact action features. To address this limitation, we propose Relational Geometry Forcing (RGF), which transfers geometric priors from a frozen 3D foundation model by aligning pairwise feature relations instead of pointwise features. Specifically, RGF applies three complementary relational objectives, where intra-frame and cross-view relations transfer scene geometry and multi-view correspondence to the video features, while action-temporal relations couple action dynamics to geometric scene evolution. Applied only during training, RGF requires no explicit geometry branch or extra inference module. Extensive experiments in simulation and the real world demonstrate the effectiveness of Relational Geometry Forcing, showing consistent gains across diverse manipulation scenarios and WAM paradigms.

CW-WAM: A Structured Contact-Wrench World Action Model for Force-Sensitive Manipulation

2026-10-21 cs.RO 👤 Lu-Qi Wang (h=10)
Houzhu Ding, Xinda Qi, Luqi Wang, Ming Yang
Core Contributions
  • Argues that force-sensitive tasks need contact information vision cannot supply, and makes contact wrench a co-predicted modality alongside video and motion.
  • A pair-causal attention mechanism structures motion-wrench dependencies, inspired by hybrid motion-force control, and a long fixed-length wrench memory retains interaction history.
  • Predicted wrench chunks serve as force references for a 20 Hz outer-loop admittance controller, bridging slow model queries and fast force feedback.
  • Insertion success rises from 25% to 70% for square pegs (1 mm clearance per side) and from 40% to 95% for power adapters, and force-reference RMSE drops from 4.8 to 2.7 N for insertion and 2.0 to 1.3 N for wiping.
Show abstract
Force-sensitive manipulation requires contact and load information that is not provided explicitly by visual observations. We present CW-WAM, a world action model that jointly predicts video, motion, and contact wrench. Inspired by hybrid motion-force control, it contains a unified interaction module that treats both motion and contact wrench as modeling targets and predicts time-aligned motion-wrench chunks as control references. A pair-causal attention mechanism structures the motion-wrench dependencies, while a long fixed-length wrench memory retains past interaction information. The predicted wrench chunk acts as a force reference for a 20-Hz outer-loop admittance controller within a slow-fast control system that maintains force feedback and smooth motion between model queries. CW-WAM improves contact prediction and increases the insertion success rate from 25% to 70% for square pegs with 1-mm clearance per side and from 40% to 95% for power adapters, compared with WAM without wrench input. It also improves lifting-height accuracy across different loads. Our framework with an admittance controller reduces force-reference RMSE from 4.8 to 2.7 N for peg insertion and from 2.0 to 1.3 N for wiping, respectively, with smaller force fluctuations during successful execution. Project website: https://cw-wam-project.github.io/

DepState-JEPA: Learning Depth-Grounded State Representation for Visual Perception and Manipulation

2026-10-21 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Jian-Hua Sun (h=9)
Xiujian Liang, Liuyuan Na, Zehao Du, Jiacheng Xu, Weixi Song
Core Contributions
  • Builds on V-JEPA 2.1 with a dual-stream RGB-depth predictor, using depth both as an observation and to ground both modalities in metric 3D space, summarized in a shared latent state called DepState.
  • Modality-private features are retained for decoding, and ray-conditioned readouts predict local features without target depth at inference, so depth need not be available at test time for prediction.
  • After an action-conditioned transition the same decoders predict future RGB and depth features, with robot-state readouts, object-trajectory supervision and forward-kinematics joint-TCP consistency shaping the state.
  • Ablations credit depth content, metric coordinates and future-state matching, with additional control gains from FK regularization and stronger RGB-encoder transfer after RGB-D adaptation.
Show abstract
Predictive representations for robot manipulation should connect scene geometry with the state changes induced by actions. Our intuition is that understanding what an action will change starts with understanding where things are: complementary RGB and depth observations ground a common scene representation from which to infer those changes. Guided by this intuition, we introduce \textbf{DepState-JEPA}, a dual-stream RGB--Depth joint-embedding predictive architecture that connects spatial understanding with action-conditioned prediction through a shared latent state, \textbf{DepState}. Built on V-JEPA~2.1, the framework uses depth both as a complementary observation stream and to ground both modalities in metric 3D space, aggregating shared information into DepState while retaining modality-private features for decoding. Ray-conditioned readouts predict local features without target depth; after an action-conditioned transition, the same decoders predict future RGB and depth features using the predicted state and fixed initial private features. Robot-state readouts provide recursive proprioceptive inputs; object-trajectory supervision and calibrated forward kinematics shape state learning through motion targets and joint--TCP consistency. Comparisons show stronger RGB-encoder transfer after RGB-D adaptation, feature completion, conditioned visual forecasting, and manipulation success. Within-model ablations identify contributions from depth content, metric coordinates, and future-state matching, with additional control gains from FK regularization.

Asymmetric On-Policy Distillation Learns In-Context Exploration

2026-10-21 cs.ROcs.AIcs.LG 👤 Roozbeh Mottaghi (h=46)
Sriyash Poddar, Yanda Bao, Jacob Krantz, Matthew Chang, Xavier Puig
Core Contributions
  • Targets partial observability: imitation of a privileged expert averages actions over hidden states and never explores, while RL is too sample-hungry. ASTEROID labels the student's own on-policy history with the privileged expert's actions so exploration emerges from supervision.
  • The key trick is asymmetry: the expert never needs to explore, yet a history-conditioned student trained on its own contexts learns to search while uncertain and exploit once the hidden state is revealed, behaving like posterior sampling.
  • Across eleven environments (discrete exploration, manipulation, navigation, Procgen) it reaches about 2x the return under fixed exploration budgets with about 100x fewer interactions than RL baselines, and beats frontier models at exploration.
  • Transfers from simulation to vision-denied real-robot manipulation, showing that in-context exploration can be trained cheaply and then used on hardware.
Show abstract
Sequential decision-making under partial observability is difficult because agents must explore to infer hidden, task-relevant information before acting effectively. Learning such information-gathering behaviors tabula rasa via online reinforcement learning is sample-inefficient, whereas offline imitation averages expert actions across hidden states and fails to learn exploration. In this work, we propose ASTEROID, a simple and effective student--teacher supervised learning framework that can learn test-time exploration. Although a privileged expert never needs to explore, we demonstrate that a history-conditioned student can learn to do so when trained with on-policy contexts: the student first collects on-policy context under partial observability, and the privileged expert provides action labels conditioned on that context. By distilling expert actions directly conditioned on the student's on-policy history, the student learns to systematically resolve partial observability, i.e., search while uncertain and exploit once the hidden state is revealed. This enables efficient test-time exploration (similar to Bayesian posterior sampling) without incurring the impractical training-data costs of reinforcement learning. Across a diverse range of eleven different environments, including discrete exploration benchmarks, robotic manipulation, navigation, and Procgen environments, ASTEROID achieves ~2x higher returns under fixed exploration budgets, uses ~100x fewer interactions than RL baselines, and outperforms frontier models at exploration. Finally, our method transfers policies trained in simulation to vision-denied real-world robot manipulation, exhibiting efficient exploration and increasing success by +40% over standard distillation. Overall, it provides a simple yet surprisingly effective framework to learn efficient test-time exploration.

Post-hoc Epistemic Uncertainty for Vision-Language-Action Policies

2026-10-21 cs.ROcs.LGstat.ML 👤 T. Neumuth (h=32)
Johannes Keller, Albrecht M. Bloße, Adrian Lindenmeyer, Thomas Neumuth, Daniel Schneider
Core Contributions
  • Replaces costly policy ensembles for epistemic uncertainty with post-hoc kernel-variance heads: a Gaussian-process conditional variance computed from the embeddings of a single trained policy, with no gradient updates and the policy left untouched.
  • On SmolVLA with LIBERO-10 it matches a two-policy ensemble in correlation with task success and in active-learning demonstration selection, while cutting uncertainty-scoring time by up to about 50x.
  • Shows that the standard LIBERO-10 protocol mostly rewards detecting differences in coverage between tasks, so controlled perturbations are proposed as a better test of within-task uncertainty.
  • Modality-specific heads flag unfamiliar cameras, instructions or states, while fused heads catch unfamiliar combinations of individually familiar inputs, which is what a deployed system needs to decide when to ask for help.
Show abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies need to recognize gaps in their training knowledge to request useful demonstrations or assistance in new situations, especially when failures are costly. Disagreement within an ensemble of independently trained policies can guide these decisions, but requires training and running multiple policies. We propose post-hoc kernel-variance heads that estimate epistemic uncertainty from the embeddings of a single policy. Each head computes the conditional variance of a Gaussian process conditioned on embeddings from the training demonstrations. Fitting requires no regression targets or gradient updates and leaves the policy unchanged. In experiments with SmolVLA on LIBERO-10, the kernel variance heads perform competitively with two-policy ensemble scores in task-level correlation with task success, in demonstration acquisition during active learning, and in referral to a stronger supervisor policy as a proxy for human assistance. For SmolVLA, the head on the vision-language backbone reduces uncertainty-scoring time by up to approximately a factor of 50 compared with ensemble scores, while also substantially reducing setup costs. We also show that the established evaluation protocol on LIBERO-10 primarily rewards recognition of coverage differences between tasks, while execution variability weakens assessment of within-task rankings. Thus, controlled perturbations provide a complementary evaluation: modality-specific heads identify unfamiliar camera, instruction, or state inputs, while fused heads can detect unfamiliar combinations of individually familiar inputs. With a supervisor proxy trained on the evaluated perturbation families, the decoder head improves within-task referral over ensemble disagreement. These findings show that useful uncertainty estimates for adaptive robot learning can be obtained from a single policy.

Training in Deployment: Human-in-the-loop Online Post-Training for Robot Manipulation

2026-10-21 cs.RO 👤 Xia-Tian Zhu (h=20)
Chun Gu, Xiaofei Wei, Yiming Chen, Bozhou Zhang, Juntong Zhang
Core Contributions
  • Targets the mismatch between offline teleoperated demonstrations and the contact-rich recovery states a deployed policy actually visits; offline post-training adapts slowly and online RL needs fragile critics.
  • TIDE uses provenance-conditioned supervised learning, updating the policy online from human interventions and policy rollouts collected in deployment, with no learned critic, reward model or action scorer.
  • On four real bimanual tasks it improves milestone-normalized progress and full-task completion over the warm-start policy and over matched extra offline demonstrations.
  • Each online post-training session finishes in under an hour with asynchronous learning and checkpointing, making adaptation-in-deployment practical.
Show abstract
Large pretrained robot foundation models provide strong priors for manipulation, yet adapting them to target tasks with offline teleoperated demonstrations often fails to cover the contact-rich, recovery, and precision-critical states encountered during deployment. Existing methods mitigate this issue by augmenting offline demonstrations with human-in-the-loop data or online reinforcement learning. Despite this, offline post-training adapts slowly because it cannot immediately absorb deployment experience, while online RL typically relies on value functions or auxiliary actor-critic models, increasing both training complexity and instability. We present TIDE (Training In DEployment), a human-in-the-loop online post-training system. TIDE introduces a provenance-conditioned supervised learning framework that updates the policy online from human interventions and policy-generated rollouts collected during deployment, without a learned critic, reward model, or action scorer. Across four real-world bimanual manipulation tasks, TIDE improves milestone-normalized task progress and binary full-task completion over the warm-start policy and matched additional offline demonstrations, and performs comparably to the fixed-policy collection baseline while making updated checkpoints available during deployment. Each task-specific online post-training session, from the start of deployment rollouts until the final checkpoint is available, completes in less than one hour while learning and checkpoint handling run asynchronously.

Learning to Absorb Corrections: Critic-Weighted Residual Flow for Vision-Based Insertion

2026-10-21 cs.RO 👤 Jin-Rong Yang (h=18)
Yiqing Yuan, Zhi Li, Jinrong Yang, Mingshan Sun, Mei Yu Zhi
Core Contributions
  • Tackles precise vision-based insertion by letting an Action Chunking Transformer absorb successful executions while a conditional residual flow policy learns corrections relative to the updated prior.
  • A chunk-level critic scores final composed actions and weights residual candidates for flow matching, but is used only in training, so deployment needs just RGB and one residual sample per chunk.
  • Reaches a five-task mean success of 66% after 20,000 post-initialization steps, 18 points above the strongest baseline, across plug, USB and key insertion, cable routing and needle threading.
  • With the residual disabled, the base ACT improves from 16% to 76% mean success, showing the online loop also improves the prior rather than only patching it.
Show abstract
Vision-based insertion requires correcting alignment errors while retaining useful behavior learned from demonstrations. We present ACT--Flow, an online learning framework that couples an adaptive Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) with a conditional residual flow policy. Following anchor-guided warmup, ACT absorbs successful executed actions through behavioral cloning, while residual targets are recomputed relative to the updated prior. A chunk-level critic learns from successful and failed interactions and evaluates final composed actions. Its estimates weight generated residual candidates for flow matching, complemented by supervision from successful real executions. Candidate scoring is confined to training; deployment uses RGB observations and samples one residual per action chunk. We evaluate the framework on power-plug, USB, and key insertion, together with cable routing and needle threading. Results show a five-task mean success rate of 66% at 20,000 post-initialization interaction steps, exceeding the strongest baseline by 18 percentage points at this checkpoint. Evaluations at matched checkpoints with the residual disabled show ACT's mean success increasing from 16% to 76%. Component ablations further support the complementary roles of behavior absorption, value weighting, and successful-action supervision.

SimPT: An Automated Simulation Framework for Intervention-Based VLA Post-Training

2026-10-21 cs.RO 👤 Zi-Peng Ye (h=12)
Caicheng Wang, Zipeng Ye, Zhexuan Zhou, Zilong Huang, Xi Zhang
Core Contributions
  • Removes the human and the real robot from algorithm iteration for intervention-based VLA post-training by building the whole loop in simulation: rollout, corrective data collection, training and standalone evaluation.
  • An Agent-Compiled Progress Graph tracks task progress from privileged simulator state and keeps stable snapshots, so a CuRobo expert recovers from the most advanced feasible stage. This cuts recovery attempts by 75.0% and raises recovery success from 93.4% to 98.9%.
  • Compares RECAP, HG-DAgger, DPO+SFT and FlowPRO on seven RoboTwin tasks, then develops RECAP-CF-LPF with cross-fitting and low-pass filtered value predictions (76.50% to 79.36% in simulation).
  • The simulation finding transfers: real-robot Ethernet cable insertion rises from 72% to 96% over 25 scenarios.
Show abstract
Human-in-the-loop (HIL) post-training improves vision-language-action (VLA) policies through expert corrections, but costly human supervision and repeated real-robot evaluation limit algorithm exploration and development. We introduce SimPT (Simulation-based Post-Training), a simulation framework that automates intervention-based VLA post-training by integrating policy rollouts, corrective data collection, policy training, and standalone evaluation. To automatically determine intervention timing and recovery strategies, we develop an Agent-Compiled Progress Graph that tracks task progress using privileged simulator state and retains stable snapshots during policy execution. After a rollout fails, a CuRobo-based expert attempts recovery from these snapshots, starting at more advanced task stages and falling back to earlier ones as needed to generate corrective trajectories. We implement and compare four post-training methods, RECAP, HG-DAgger, DPO+SFT, and FlowPRO, across seven RoboTwin manipulation tasks. Relative to a fixed-interval snapshot baseline, the Progress Graph reduces recovery attempts by 75.0% and increases recovery success from 93.4% to 98.9%. Using SimPT, we develop RECAP-CF-LPF, a refinement of RECAP that combines cross-fitting and low-pass filtering of value predictions. This refinement increases the overall success rate from 76.50% to 79.36% in held-out simulation trials. In real-robot HIL post-training, it increases the success rate from 72% to 96% across 25 scenarios of an Ethernet cable-insertion task. These results demonstrate the utility of SimPT for exploring and developing VLA post-training algorithms.

FlowVLA: Learning Flow-Aware Physical Completion for Quantitative Robotic Pouring

2026-10-21 cs.RO 👤 Tu-Tian Tang (h=11)
Liwen Yang, Yutian Zhang, Jiaqi Zhai, Li Yang, Tutian Tang
Core Contributions
  • Names a concrete failure of whole-task VLAs: during steady pouring, image and pose barely change while mass accumulates, so the decision to return is ambiguous.
  • FlowVLA separates motor generation (a shared π0.5 action expert) from physical-completion estimation, fusing vision, joints, wrist wrench and a recurrent force belief into a transferred-mass estimate that triggers return.
  • Achieves 13.1 g goal-balanced MAE, lower than all tested VLA baselines, with 53.7% of attempts both completing and landing within ±10% of requested mass.
  • Interpolates to held-out 150/250 g targets without retraining and is checked on three further container-fluid settings, supporting force-aware completion as a general VLA module.
Show abstract
During steady pouring, images and robot pose may remain nearly unchanged while transferred mass accumulates, making the return decision ambiguous for a whole-task vision-language-action (VLA) policy. We present FlowVLA, which separates motor generation from physical-completion estimation. A shared $π_{0.5}$ action expert learns approach, pour, and return with boundary-aware supervision. A target-independent estimator fuses visual, joint, wrist-wrench, and recurrent force-belief histories to maintain an estimate of transferred mass. The controller initiates return when this estimate plus predicted residual outflow reaches the requested mass. In coffee-pot/water experiments, FlowVLA achieves 13.1 g goal-balanced mean absolute error (MacroMAE), lower than all tested VLA baselines. Across all attempts, 53.7% both complete the task and finish within +/-10% of the requested mass. The same model interpolates to held-out 150/250-g targets without retraining. Independently trained models further evaluate the formulation in three additional container-fluid settings.

Multimodal Embodied Agents: A Unified Capability-centric Perspective from Computer-Use to Robot-Use

2026-10-21 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CVcs.MAcs.MM 👤 M. Shou (h=53)
Yanzhe Chen, Ziyi Yang, Jifeng Zhu, Qiming Huang, Ruihe An
Core Contributions
  • Proposes PAPAV (Perceive, Anticipate, Plan, Act, Verify) as a function-level lens that lets computer-use agents, robotic systems and embodied agents be compared without arguing about architectures.
  • Argues that moving from screens to bodies changes the constraints (partial observability, unverifiable outcomes, irreversibility), so Anticipate and Verify matter far more for robots than for GUI agents.
  • Audits 64 benchmarks and finds evaluation concentrated on Act: only 5 assess Anticipate and 3 assess Verify, so task success hides the capabilities most responsible for reliability.
  • Useful as a checklist for benchmark designers: it names which capability a new benchmark is actually measuring rather than reporting success alone.
Show abstract
Multimodal agents (MMAs) sustain interaction through reasoning, memory, tools, and feedback, most visibly as computer-use agents, while robotic systems (RSs) couple sensing and actuation under physical dynamics. We define multimodal embodied agents (MMEAs) as goal-directed systems that couple multimodal task reasoning with physical action and revise decisions from the resulting feedback. This raises a central question: what changes when a multimodal agent moves from computer-use to robot-use? We introduce PAPAV, a unified capability-centric framework of five recurring functions: Perceive the current state, Anticipate action effects, Plan a feasible course, Act through an interface or body, and Verify the outcome. Defined by function rather than architecture, these capabilities let PAPAV identify what all three share and then compare MMEAs with MMAs and with RSs. Five physical constraints, from partial observability to unverifiable outcomes, leave fewer choices fixed in advance and less room to reverse errors. Across 64 benchmarks, explicit evaluation centers on Act; only 5 assess Anticipate and 3 assess Verify, leaving both largely hidden behind task success. These constraints also frame the open challenges we identify. PAPAV therefore provides a common basis for designing reliable physical agents and measuring progress across all five capabilities rather than task success alone.

Toward Recursive Self-Improvement in Physical AI

2026-10-21 cs.ROcs.AI 👤 Ziyang Luo (h=21)
Meng Luo, Zechen Bai, Shanqing Xu, Yanlin Li, Kaixin Li
Core Contributions
  • A position paper that asks what recursive self-improvement, where a system improves its own improvement process, means for physical AI, where trials are costly, observations partial and mistakes sometimes irreversible.
  • Traces pathways from improving robot capabilities to improving the processes that produce them, and states when gains carry forward: feedback must address the remaining learning need, the receiving component must be able to use it, and the gain must survive task or hardware change.
  • Finds existing physical studies, mostly in manipulation, show behavior improvement, benefits to later learning and procedure improvement, but none yet isolate gains attributable to recursion.
  • Distills evaluation principles and research directions, giving the community a concrete test for claims of self-improving robots.
Show abstract
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) describes a system that improves not only its behavior but also the process by which it improves. Recent digital agents test this idea by rewriting their own code and checking each version with executable tests. Bringing these mechanisms into the physical world requires learning from partial observations through costly trials, while accounting for changing operating conditions and potentially irreversible consequences. We position RSI within robot learning and trace pathways from improving robot capabilities to improving the processes that generate them. We examine how physical experience can strengthen these improvement mechanisms and when the resulting gains can support further self-improvement of the mechanisms themselves. Gains carry forward only when feedback addresses remaining learning needs, the receiving component can use the change, its value survives changes in tasks or hardware, and continued practice remains safe and affordable. Existing studies, concentrated in manipulation, support behavioral improvement, benefits to later learning, and procedure improvement. Some systems also change the improver's knowledge or tools, but the physical studies reviewed here have yet to isolate gains attributable to recursion. We synthesize these findings into evaluation principles and research directions for machines that, trial by trial, safely improve the way they improve.

2026-10-21 cs.RO 👤 Zhong-Yuan Wang (h=20)
Yijun Shen, Diqi He, Jinbang Guo, Wanyue Zhang, Jiaming Mi
Core Contributions
  • Addresses the fact that MLLMs do not know environment-specific action preconditions and effects, and that existing fixes need costly retraining or reuse unstructured experience poorly.
  • CausGround distills interaction experience into reusable, non-parametric action-centered causal knowledge over a factorized world state of object attributes and relations.
  • At planning time it reasons backward over preconditions and forward over effects, so the MLLM plans against grounded dependencies instead of guessing.
  • Raises VirtualHome task success from 24.9% to 53.3%, RoboCasa closed-loop planning from 43.0% to 57.0% and SWITCH interaction accuracy from 25.8% to 31.9%, with real-robot demonstrations.
Show abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) show promising reasoning capabilities, but reliable physical interaction requires planning that accounts for when actions are executable and how they change the world. These preconditions and effects are environment-dependent and often not captured by MLLMs, making adaptation challenging. Yet existing approaches either require costly environment-specific training or rely on unstructured interaction experience that is difficult to reuse. Therefore, we introduce CausGround, a non-parametric causal interaction grounding framework that distills interaction experience into reusable action-centered causal knowledge. CausGround identifies action preconditions and effects and organizes them in a factorized world-state representation capturing object attributes and inter-object relations. At planning time, task-relevant causal dependencies are grounded in current observations to support backward reasoning over preconditions and forward reasoning over effects for action selection and multi-step planning. We evaluate CausGround on simulated high-level planning, interaction reasoning, and real-world robot manipulation. Moreover, real-world experiments show that CausGround reasons backward from MLLM-interpreted goals to derive executable high-level action sequences for sequential manipulation. These results demonstrate that reusable action preconditions and effects support long-horizon embodied planning across diverse environments without parameter updates.

2026-10-21 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 E. Szymańska (h=18)
Bjorn Stragier, Emilia Szymańska, J. Micah Prendergast, Lukas Rosenberger Schmid
Core Contributions
  • Solves a construction-site problem: registering a scale-ambiguous, sparse, semantic VSLAM map to a 2D floor plan when the building is only partly built and visually repetitive. Prior methods handled at most two of these four difficulties.
  • Defines a single robust, differentiable, class-balanced semantic-geometric energy used consistently for initialization, matching and local refinement.
  • Offers two global initializations, one from a single inaccurate user correspondence and one from a cross-modal matching network, each finding candidates that are then refined directly on the energy.

Show abstract

Visual capture is attractive for its low cost and availability, but aligning a visual simultaneous localization and mapping (VSLAM) map to a floor plan is difficult; VSLAM recovers geometry only up to an unknown similarity transform, the sparse point cloud is cross-modal to the symbolic floor plan, sites are only partially built during capture, and construction sites are visually repetitive, degrading map quality while removing distinctive features. Existing methods address at most two of these challenges. We thus introduce Reading the Room(RR), a global-local approach to register scale-ambiguous, sparse, semantic VSLAM maps to partially built floor plans. Core Contributions
  • Observes that on a two-wheeled inverted pendulum, navigation and balance objectives conflict in a way that depends on pitch, so a fixed reward weighting is wrong in some states.
  • Introduces a pitch-conditioned scalarization coefficient β(s) that shifts weight between stabilization and navigation during PPO training, with no PID/LQR layer or extra hierarchy.

Show abstract
Thermal cameras enable robots to perceive human activities in darkness while revealing less personally identifiable appearance information and fewer details of the surrounding environment than RGB cameras. As vision-language models (VLMs), originally developed for RGB imagery, are increasingly integrated into embodied systems for scene understanding and high-level planning, thermal sensing offers a promising way to extend activity perception to low-light and privacy-sensitive settings. However, the failure modes of VLMs in thermal human activity understanding for human-robot interaction (HRI) remain underexplored. We fill this gap by evaluating seven locally deployable VLMs, including models developed for physical AI and robotics, on two person-centric thermal datasets. Our diagnostic tasks examine fine-grained action recognition, activity timeline reconstruction, and the ability to withhold unsupported answers. Paired RGB recordings serve as a performance reference, while controlled variations in frame sampling, thermal input presentation, and reasoning are used to assess whether the observed errors can be mitigated. Our results show that models can recognize broad activity categories but struggle with fine-grained actions such as coughing, sneezing, and yawning. They also produce inaccurate event timelines and hallucinate actions involving objects. Simple thermal input preprocessing and additional reasoning offer only limited, model-dependent gains. Abstention behavior also varies across models even under explicit instructions, limiting their reliability for practical robotic use.
