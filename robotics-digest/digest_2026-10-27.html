🔭 Research Landscape

The dominant theme is making world-action models (WAMs) lean and useful. TAPESTRY (#4) turns a pretrained video diffusion transformer into a policy by treating each action as a single patch token, then drops video generation at inference to emit an action chunk in one DDIM step (84 ms). GAWM (#27) goes smaller still: fewer than 100M parameters, a frozen DINOv3 encoder and no robot or video pretraining, yet it beats billion-parameter WAMs on RoboTwin 2.0. Trace-WAM (#28) argues the video target is the wrong thing to predict and swaps in future 3D point motion as an auxiliary signal, while SWIM (#18) makes the same argument for JEPA world models by learning which state features matter from an inverse-dynamics model. Taken together, these papers say the useful content of a world model is the task-relevant dynamics, not pixels, and that this is what lets it shrink.

A second thread is what to do with a policy after it is trained. Best-of-N analysis (#22) shows the standard way of measuring selection headroom overstates it (true selectable share 24-66%), and a fail-gated selector lifts closed-loop success from 76.9% to 81.6%. R2A (#9) retrieves motion primitives from training demonstrations to correct a frozen VLA under visual shift (up to +40% real-world), VLA-Stem (#16) bolts on an RL reflex layer for obstacles (52.8% vs 23.7% collision-free success) and AFIL (#7) steers sampling away from failure modes. All four leave base weights alone, a pragmatic stance that contrasts with TOPS (#17) and ThinkWorld (#30), which instead reshape the generative prior or the reasoning space during training.

Data and embodiment transfer form the third theme. OpenJaws (#2), RoboSTD (#5), HumiTransfer (#6), Ego-CEVR (#14) and CARD (#3) each find a different way around robot-in-the-loop teleoperation, and CARD's finding that kinematic retargeting keeps contact on under 10% of frames is a useful warning that geometry alone is not enough for dexterity. That points toward the contact-rich results in #12 (95.2% of 4,800 in-hand goals, drops cut 75% by a safety filter) and the sobering base-compliance measurements in #24, where only 54-70% of a 100 N command reached the workpiece. The field is converging on cheap human data plus explicit contact and safety modeling as the route to reliable manipulation.

TAPESTRY: A Single Backbone for Video Generation and Robot Control

2026-10-27 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CVcs.LG 👤 K. Srinivasan (h=16)
Jeremy A. Collins, Seungjae Lee, Rachanon Wachakorn, Krishnan Srinivasan, Animesh Garg
Core Contributions
  • Converts a pretrained video diffusion transformer into a policy by mapping each action timestep to a single patch token through a linear projection into the VAE patch space, so one backbone handles both video and control.
  • Trains with three diffusion objectives per minibatch (video, joint video+action, action-only), letting human and robot video supply dynamics priors without requiring action labels everywhere.
  • At inference it skips video generation and denoises an action chunk in a single DDIM step, 84 ms per chunk on consumer hardware, much cheaper than joint video-action policies.
Show abstract
Pretrained video generation models encode rich priors about physical interactions, but leveraging them for robot control typically requires either resource-intensive video generation at test time or action heads trained from scratch that do not fully utilize the pretrained backbone. We introduce Tokenizing Actions as Video Patches in a Shared Transformer Policy (TAPESTRY), a world action model that repurposes a pretrained video diffusion transformer into a robotic policy without requiring video generation at inference. Our key idea is to treat robot actions as single-patch tokens in the video model's latent space. A lightweight linear projection maps each action timestep to the patch dimensions of the pretrained VAE, and the resulting tokens are processed by the same patchifying layers and transformer backbone used for video. The shared backbone is optimized through three diffusion objectives sampled per minibatch element: Video diffusion uses VAE latents from robot and human videos to establish dynamics priors. Joint video+action diffusion trains on robot demonstrations using a coupled diffusion timestep and an asymmetric attention mask. Action-only diffusion denoises actions conditioned on clean image latents. At test time, the model generates a compact action token sequence in a single DDIM step from pure noise, producing smooth action chunks at 84 ms per action chunk on consumer hardware and avoiding the cost of full video generation. Across simulation benchmarks and real-robot experiments, TAPESTRY achieves strong performance with substantially lower inference cost than comparable joint video--action policies. More information can be found at https://tapestry-wam.github.io.
#27 h=1

GAWM: A Compact Latent World-Action Model for Multi-Task Robot Manipulation

2026-10-27 cs.RO 👤 Mu-Yuan Liu (h=1)
Yang Liu, Xiaoli Liu, Liuzhao Li, Yuduo Song, Muyuan Liu
Core Contributions
  • Shows a world-action model does not need a billion-parameter backbone: GAWM predicts future observations in the latent space of a frozen DINOv3 encoder with under 100M parameters and no robot or video pretraining.
  • Compresses each camera view into a few tokens and supervises forecasts at two future horizons with dense patch features of future frames, forcing the tokens to encode future spatial layout; an action decoder regresses action chunks from current and predicted tokens.
  • Outperforms billion-parameter WAMs on RoboTwin 2.0, is comparable to billion-parameter VLAs on LIBERO and has lower action-prediction error and latency than a fine-tuned VLA on a dual-arm robot; the latent world model helps most when data or training time is limited.
Show abstract
World-action models (WAMs) incorporate the prediction of future observations into robot policy learning. However, most WAMs build on billion-parameter vision-language or video-generation backbones, which incur high training cost and inference latency. In this letter, we present GAWM (Geometry-Aware World Model), a compact multi-task WAM that predicts future observations in the latent space of a frozen DINOv3 encoder. GAWM compresses the features of each camera view into a small set of tokens. A lightweight predictor, conditioned on the task rather than on actions, forecasts these tokens at two future horizons. The forecast is supervised with the dense patch features of future frames, which requires it to encode the spatial layout of the future scene. An action decoder directly regresses a chunk of future actions from the current and predicted tokens. With fewer than 100M parameters and no robot or video pretraining, GAWM outperforms billion-parameter WAMs on RoboTwin 2.0 and performs comparably to billion-parameter VLAs on LIBERO. On a dual-arm robot, it achieves lower action-prediction error on held-out demonstrations than a fine-tuned billion-parameter VLA, with substantially lower inference latency. Ablation studies show that the latent world model substantially improves performance when training data or training time is limited. Code is available at https://github.com/gaoxiang12/ga-wm.

Trace-WAM: Learning to Act via 3D Motion Prediction for World Action Models

2026-10-27 cs.RO 👤 Xu-Qing Hu (h=1)
Xu Hu, Changjie Wu, Linzhe Shi, Hang Zhang, Chen Yang
Core Contributions
  • Argues future-video prediction wastes capacity on background appearance, and adds future 3D motion prediction (point correspondence, image-plane displacement, depth change) as an auxiliary objective focused on interaction dynamics.
  • A three-branch Mixture-of-Transformer jointly learns video, motion and action, but the action expert does not consume predicted trajectories, so inference is still one visual pass plus action denoising.
  • Shows strong in-distribution and out-of-distribution results on RoboCasa-GR1, LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, with clearest gains over Fast-WAM when demonstrations are limited or distribution shifts occur.
Show abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) learn representations for robot control by coupling future visual prediction with action generation. Successful manipulation depends primarily on how the robot and task-relevant objects evolve, whereas future-video prediction must also reconstruct the appearance and background of the entire scene. We therefore propose Trace-WAM, which augments video-action learning with future 3D motion prediction as an auxiliary objective. This objective supervises temporal point correspondence together with image-plane displacement and depth change, directly describing the interaction dynamics and spatial changes involved in manipulation. To this end, we adopt a three-branch Mixture-of-Transformer (Tri-MoT) architecture to jointly learn video prediction, motion prediction, and action generation. The motion expert predicts future trajectories from current visual features, allowing motion supervision to shape the representations used for action generation. Because the action expert does not consume predicted trajectories, inference retains the current-observation policy path: a single visual forward pass followed by action denoising. Experiments on RoboCasa-GR1, LIBERO, and LIBERO-Plus demonstrate strong in-distribution performance and out-of-distribution generalization, with particularly clear gains over Fast-WAM under limited demonstrations and distribution shifts.

SWIM: Learning What Matters from Behaviors for JEPA World Models

2026-10-27 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Qing-Jun Cui (h=5)
Jiaming Hu, Shi Bo, Yan Zheng, Tian Wang, Florian Dubost
Core Contributions
  • JEPA world models predict embeddings but need not encode task-relevant state; hand-picked or uniformly weighted state supervision overcounts redundant features and lets irrelevant ones obscure differences.
  • SWIM learns state-feature weights with an inverse-dynamics model that predicts recorded actions from states and future displacements, then uses the weighted metric to align latent and state distances.
  • The inverse-dynamics model is training-only, so planning from images costs nothing extra; it improves planning success over uniform weighting on five simulated manipulation tasks.
Show abstract
Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) enable world models to plan from images by predicting future embeddings. However, their visual prediction objectives do not explicitly incorporate task-relevant state information, so the physical state that determines task progress may not be reliably captured in the latent space. Supervising world models with simulator state can make planning more informative, but it requires deciding which state features matter. Features are typically selected by hand or weighted uniformly, and a uniform weighting gives every standardized state feature equal influence, so redundant coordinates can overcount the same variation, while irrelevant ones can obscure task-relevant differences. To address this limitation, we propose SWIM (State Weighting with Inverse-Dynamics Model), which learns state weights from offline trajectories by predicting recorded actions from current states and observed future displacements. The learned weights quantify each feature's contribution to action prediction and define a weighted state metric for state-supervised training, which we demonstrate by aligning latent and state distances. The inverse-dynamics model is used only during training, so the resulting world model plans from images with no additional planning-time computation. Across five simulated robotic manipulation tasks, SWIM consistently improves planning success over uniform state weighting.

How Much Can Best-of-N Help a World Action Model? Measuring Selectable Headroom and Realizing It in Closed Loop

2026-10-27 cs.ROcs.CVcs.LG 👤 Ming-Kai Liu (h=3)
Haojia Gao, Yue Cao, Yumin Liu, Haohua Que, Mingkai Liu
Core Contributions
  • Shows that the usual headroom estimate for best-of-N selection (run each candidate once, keep the best) conflates candidate quality with downstream randomness, so it overstates what a selector can realize.
  • Introduces a denoised counterfactual protocol (reproducible branching, repeated continuations, held-out oracles, common random numbers) over 100,000+ rollouts and four WAMs; selectable share of native samples is only 24-66% for three WAMs.
  • Finds that imagined futures from action-conditioned WAMs carry as much outcome information as real futures, which Cosmos Policy's value head leaves unused.
  • A fail-gated selector that deviates only when failure is predicted raises success from 76.9% to 81.6% on training tasks and 62.6% to 69.8% on unseen tasks.
Show abstract
World action models (WAMs) predict the visual consequences of the actions they generate, which makes test-time selection a natural way to improve a fixed policy: imagine the futures of candidate actions and execute the best one. The benefit of selection is bounded by the selectable headroom, how much better the best candidate truly is, but the standard estimate, which executes each candidate once and keeps the best outcome, conflates candidate quality with the randomness of everything that follows. We introduce a denoised counterfactual protocol, based on bitwise-reproducible branching, repeated continuations, held-out oracles, and common random numbers, that measures the headroom a selector can realize, and use its measurements to build a fail-gated selector. Across four WAMs, two benchmarks, over 100,000 branched rollouts, and pre-registered tests, we find that (i) the standard estimate overstates the selectable headroom of native samples: bounds from 17 continuations per candidate place its selectable share between 24% and 66% for three WAMs; (ii) structured action perturbations create up to 16 points of selectable headroom; and (iii) in both WAMs with action-conditioned imagination, imagined futures carry candidate-outcome information with no detectable loss relative to real futures, information that the value head of Cosmos Policy leaves unused. Deviating from the policy chunk only when a readout of the WAM predicts failure, the selector raises closed-loop task success from 76.9% to 81.6% on held-out episodes of training tasks, with the largest gain where the policy fails most often, and from 62.6% to 69.8% on unseen tasks.

ThinkWorld: World Imagination Reinforces Chain-of-Dynamics Reasoning

2026-10-27 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CV 👤 Chaehyun Kim (h=0)
Chaehyun Kim, Chi-Pin Huang, Jisoo Kim, Min-Hung Chen, Yu-Chiang Frank Wang
Core Contributions
  • Represents each action thought as a Chain-of-Dynamics, a sequence of discrete motion tokens capturing intermediate dynamics from the current world to a future state, usable as a common action representation across embodiments.
  • Reinforces CoDs by predicting the future each induces and rewarding those that match the observed outcome, so human videos without action labels still give direct supervision from state transitions.
  • Human and robot experience thus refine one reasoning space; the abstract reports effective human-to-robot transfer and generalization to unseen tasks in simulation and real robots, but gives no numbers.
Show abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have made substantial progress in robotic manipulation, but their generalization remains constrained by the diversity and coverage of action-labeled robot demonstrations. Human videos provide much broader behavioral and environmental experience, while learning from them faces a fundamental trade-off between scalable data and explicit action grounding. We introduce ThinkWorld, a VLA learning framework that grounds action reasoning in world dynamics and strengthens it through reinforcement learning with world imagination. ThinkWorld represents each action thought as a Chain-of-Dynamics (CoD), a sequence of discrete motion tokens that captures the intermediate dynamics connecting the current world to a future state and serves as a common action representation across embodiments. We first initialize CoD with action-labeled demonstrations, then predict the future induced by each CoD and reinforce those whose predictions better match the observed outcome. This enables videos without action annotations to provide direct supervision from observed state transitions, allowing human and robot experience to refine the same reasoning space. Experiments in simulation and on real robots demonstrate effective human-to-robot transfer and strong generalization to unseen tasks and environments.

TOPS: Reshaping Flow Source and Transport for Multi-Task Robot Manipulation

2026-10-27 cs.ROcs.AI 👤 Jia-Qiang Bai (h=5)
Tuo An, Chuhao Zhou, Jiahua Dong, Jingliang Li, Jiaqi Bai
Core Contributions
  • Questions the default of one shared Gaussian source and linear path for all tasks in flow-based multi-task policies, and adapts both per task without changing the architecture.
  • Builds multimodal task sources from reverse-mapped expert action sequences so the language instruction chooses where generation starts, then reshapes the transport schedule using source-target covariance mismatch, with an analysis showing a lower bound on velocity-prediction risk.
  • With one integration step lifts LIBERO-10 success from 53.44% (ten-step flow matching) to 69.67% at about one-sixth the inference time, and on a real upper-drawer task from 20% to 80%; gains transfer to pi0.5.
Show abstract
Flow-based policies for language-conditioned multi-task manipulation commonly share a single $\textit{Gaussian}$ source prior and a common linear transport path across tasks. However, reliably executing multiple tasks with distinct motion requirements remains challenging for a shared policy. We introduce $\textbf{TOPS}$ ($\textbf{T}$ask-$\textbf{O}$riented $\textbf{P}$riors and $\textbf{S}$chedules), which adapts the flow source prior and interpolation schedule to each task without modifying the network architecture. TOPS first constructs multimodal task sources from reverse-mapped expert action sequences, allowing language instructions to select where generation begins. Guided by source-target covariance mismatch, it then slows the transition toward expert actions early in denoising and accelerates it later along directions requiring expansion. Under a $\textit{Gaussian}$ endpoint model, our analysis shows that this schedule adjustment can lower an upper bound on velocity-prediction risk. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and CALVIN demonstrate improved performance across in-distribution, perturbation and cross-environment settings, alongside better data efficiency. Using one integration step, TOPS raises LIBERO-10 success from $53.44\%$ to $69.67\%$ compared with ten-step Standard Flow Matching (FM), while reducing inference time to approximately one-sixth of the baseline. TOPS also yields consistent gains when transferred to the large-scale flow-based $π_{0.5}$ policy across all four evaluated settings. Real-world experiments further demonstrate improved task success, including an increase from $20\%$ with standard FM to $80\%$ with TOPS on the upper-drawer opening task.

Retrieve to Act: Motion Primitive Graph Retrieval for Robust VLA Execution

2026-10-27 cs.ROcs.AI 👤 Daniel Sonntag (h=7)
Pham Tri Quang, Bao-Ngoc Dao, Minh Duc Nguyen, Duc-Duy Nguyen, Hung T. Ho
Core Contributions
  • Treats VLA robustness to visual shift as a retrieval problem in motion space rather than trying to clean up the visual features that the shift corrupts.
  • Organizes successful demonstrations into an offline spatiotemporal motion primitive graph; a query-dependent GNN localizes current execution in it and retrieves compatible future primitives.
  • A learned dimension-wise gate blends retrieved priors with the frozen VLA's actions, keeping local reactivity; across GR00T N1.7, pi0.5 and StableVLA it gains up to +12% in simulation and +40% in real-world tests with no fine-tuning.
Show abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies demonstrate remarkable semantic generalization, yet remain highly vulnerable to out-of-distribution (OOD) visual shifts during execution. While existing test-time methods attempt to suppress spurious visual cues, they fundamentally overlook the rich, uncorrupted kinematic priors embedded in the policy's own training experience. We introduce Retrieve to Act (R2A), a test-time scaling framework that formulates OOD robustness purely as a geometric motion primitive retrieval problem. R2A bypasses fragile visual features by structuring successful training demonstrations into an offline spatiotemporal motion primitive graph. During inference, a query-dependent Graph Neural Network dynamically localizes the robot's ongoing execution within this graph, retrieving structurally compatible future motion primitives. These retrieved geometric priors are then seamlessly harmonized with the frozen VLA's predicted actions via a learned, dimension-wise gating mechanism, adaptively rectifying erroneous spatial trajectories while preserving the policy's local reactivity. Extensive experiments across three state-of-the-art VLA architectures, GR00T N1.7, $π_{0.5}$, and StableVLA, demonstrate that R2A consistently and significantly enhances robustness against diverse contextual perturbations. R2A achieves absolute success rate improvements of up to +12% in simulation benchmarks (LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin2.0-Plus) and +40% in real-world deployments, without requiring any finetuning of the base policies. Project page: https://retrieve2act.github.io/r2a/

VLA-Stem: A Lightweight Safety Mechanism for Vision-Language-Action Models via Reinforcement Learning

2026-10-27 cs.RO 👤 Wei-Hao Yu (h=5)
Bojun Zou, Wenhao Yu, Jiaohao Li, Jiaxuan Lin, Guanting Ye
Core Contributions
  • Adds obstacle avoidance to VLAs without fine-tuning them, which would need costly dynamic-obstacle data and could erode original task skill.
  • Converts VLA action chunks into motion references and trains an RL "Stem" policy, conditioned on proprioception and local obstacle information, to adjust timing and path while preserving task intent; training combines teacher-student distillation with curriculum learning.
  • Reaches 52.8% collision-free task success across six constrained simulation settings versus 23.7% for the strongest safety baseline, with base performance preserved when no obstacles are present, and fewer collisions in real-world tests.
Show abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models integrate high-level reasoning with low-level action execution for end-to-end robotic control. However, most VLA models target static manipulation in unconstrained environments, overlooking spatial constraints and dynamic obstacles. Fine-tuning VLAs on obstacle-avoidance data is a straightforward solution, but collecting such data is costly, especially for dynamic scenarios, and may degrade their original task capabilities. To address these limitations, we propose VLA-Stem, a lightweight, reinforcement-learning-based safety mechanism for VLA systems. Without fine-tuning the underlying VLA policy, VLA-Stem converts its action chunks into motion references and uses trajectory-level guidance to preserve their spatial intent while allowing flexible motion and timing adjustments. Conditioned on these references, robot proprioception, and local obstacle information, the Stem policy adaptively modifies execution at inference time, preserving the original task intent while enabling responsive adaptation to spatial constraints and dynamic obstacles. For robust and sample-efficient training, we combine teacher-student distillation with curriculum learning. We evaluate VLA-Stem on reference following, static spatial constraints, and dynamic obstacle avoidance. Across six constrained simulation settings, VLA-Stem achieves 52.8% average collision-free task success versus 23.7% for the strongest safety baseline, while preserving base-policy performance in obstacle-free settings; real-world experiments further show consistent collision reductions under static and dynamic obstacles. These results demonstrate that VLA-Stem couples high-level VLA task intent with rapid, reflex-like responses to immediate physical risks, providing a brainstem-inspired mechanism for robust robot execution.

Tokens of Motion: Primitive Motion Learning for Human-Humanoid Skill Transfer

2026-10-27 cs.RO 👤 Zhong-Pai Gao (h=8)
Hanwen Ren, Meng Zheng, Wenzhe Cui, Benjamin Planche, Zhongpai Gao
Core Contributions
  • Note: the paper's listed title describes primitive-motion transfer, but the abstract describes AFIL, a failure-informed method for VLAs; these bullets follow the abstract.
  • Success-only behavioral cloning gives no corrective signal, so small errors compound; AFIL has the pretrained VLA generate its own failure rollouts online, avoiding handcrafted failure modes or human recovery data.
  • Trains dual action generators (success and failure) on a shared vision-language backbone, so the failure branch adds little parameter overhead.
  • During sampling the failure generator steers actions away from failure-prone regions, with strength set per diffusion step by the distance between the two distributions; reported to improve success on short- and long-horizon in- and out-of-domain tasks.
Show abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models provide a promising paradigm for scalable robotic manipulation, yet their reliance on success-only behavioral cloning leaves them brittle; lacking corrective training signals, minor execution errors rapidly compound into unrecoverable, out-of-distribution failures. To address this limitation, we propose Adaptive Failure-Informed Learning (AFIL), an end-to-end framework that leverages failure trajectories as adaptive negative guidance for diffusion- and flow-based VLA policies. AFIL uses a pretrained VLA to generate failure rollouts online, avoiding the need for handcrafted failure-mode design or human-in-the-loop recovery. It then jointly trains Dual Action Generators (DAGs) for successful and failed behaviors while sharing a common vision-language backbone, enabling efficient failure-aware policy learning with limited parameter overhead. During sampling, the failure generator adaptively steers action generation away from failure-prone regions and toward more reliable success modes, with guidance strength determined by the per-diffusion-step distance between success and failure distributions. Experiments across in-domain and out-of-domain robotic manipulation tasks, covering both short- and long-horizon settings, show that AFIL consistently improves task success rates and robustness over existing VLA baselines, demonstrating its effectiveness, efficiency, and generality.

CARD: Contact-Anchored Retargeting for Dexterous Manipulation

2026-10-27 cs.RO 👤 Jitendra Malik (h=18)
Cunxi Dai, Botao He, Francois R Hogan, Wei Pan, Rao Fu
Core Contributions
  • Existing retargeting copies kinematics and ignores force, so retargeted hands lose contact; CARD instead sets explicit contact-force targets alongside joint motion.
  • Unifies video, motion capture and tactile-glove data through one hand-contact representation, and adds Vid-HACO to infer contact from video that has no tactile signal.
  • Maintains contact on 43-78% of contact frames versus under 10% for kinematic and 22.5% for object-aware baselines; plugged into physics-based retargeting, strict success on GRAB rises from 12% to 32%.
  • A tactile tracking controller is shown to be necessary to reproduce demonstrated forces during open-loop hardware replay.
Show abstract
Humans are dexterous, but traditional data collection paradigms such as teleoperation, kinesthetic teaching, and wearable exoskeletons force humans to act through the robot embodiment, bottlenecking their dexterity. Retargeting can avoid this. However, existing methods purely translate kinematics and ignore the real-world contact dynamics essential for task success. We present CARD: Contact-Anchored Retargeting for Dexterous Manipulation, a simulator and object-free framework that retargets human demonstrations to robot actions by explicitly preserving contact force targets. CARD unifies human data sources including video, motion capture, and tactile gloves via a shared hand--contact representation and generates explicit contact force targets in addition to kinematics. To maintain these contact targets during hardware open-loop replay, CARD also introduces a tactile tracking controller. For video which does not contain an explicit tactile source, we present Vid-HACO, our video-to-contact model that sets a new state of the art. Across our video, glove, motion-capture data and the GRAB dataset, CARD maintains contact on $43$--$78\%$ of contact frames, while kinematic and object-aware retargeting methods maintain contact on $<10\%$ and $22.5\%$ of the frames respectively. Further, integrating our formulation into physics-based retargeting improves strict task success from 12\% to 32\% on GRAB dataset. Finally, we show that our tactile tracker is critical to reproduce demonstrated forces during hardware replay. These results establish that contact preservation via CARD is a scalable interface for transferring human dexterity to robots.

Pushing Dexterity and Safety for In-Hand Object Pose Reaching That Works Remarkably Well

2026-10-27 cs.ROcs.LGeess.SY 👤 Ian T. Chuang (h=5)
Ian Chuang, Hiya Shah, Vamsi Chilamkur, Iman Soltani, Claire Tomlin
Core Contributions
  • Sets a hard benchmark for in-hand manipulation: reaching arbitrary object poses at any wrist orientation through long goal sequences, not just spinning an object.
  • The sim-only recipe relies on diverse resets and goals from generated grasp datasets and an adaptive friction curriculum, and transfers zero-shot to a real LEAP hand using mocap or a single wrist depth camera.
  • Over 13.3 hours and 4,800 unseen goals the state-based policy reaches 95.2% of goals within 2 cm and 0.4 rad; a learned residual safety filter with tunable conservativeness cuts drops 75% while still reaching 91.5%.
  • Also demonstrates portable skills such as screwing in a lightbulb using only a wrist camera, and releases the full recipe.
Show abstract
Dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands is the next frontier of robotics, yet learned hand skills remain limited to simple grasps or basic repeated motions, such as rotating an object. As robots attempt more demanding in-hand maneuvers, safety, such as not dropping the object, becomes critical, yet it is rarely measured. Alex S. Miller, Jeffrey H. Lang
Core Contributions
  • Pursues electric-field motors for settings where magnetics fall short (minimal mass, vacuum, high temperature) by replacing continuum rotor materials with discrete circuit elements between conductive vanes.
  • The discrete circuit rotor widens the design space for resistivity, permittivity and circuit behavior that a single material cannot offer.
  • Emulates the magnetic "deep bar" rotor's frequency-dependent time constant in the electric domain, giving improved torque over a wide speed range in a macro-scale demonstration.
Show abstract
Electric-field-based actuators and machines lead the design space at the nanoscale and in biological systems, while having the potential to be used in favorable macroscale applications which prioritize minimal mass, performance in a vacuum, or performance in high temperatures. Electric-field induction motors consist of a set of stator electrodes which induce opposite charges onto a movable rotor surface. In earlier work, rotor surfaces for electric-field induction motors have been made of single continuum materials, which are limited in possible resistivity, permittivity and circuitry characteristics. To enhance design freedom, we develop a rotor for electric-field-based machines which may be constructed out of discrete circuit components between a finite number of conductive vanes. Magnetic-field induction motors, which are common in machinery, often utilize ``deep bar'' rotors, consisting of conducting bars which decrease the rotor time constant with increasing frequency using the skin depth effect. We propose, analyze, design and demonstrate a macro-scale electric-field induction motor which uses circuitry on the rotor surface to emulate the ``deep bar'' design, providing improved torque over a wide range of speeds.

Predict the Swing, Match the Speed: Robotic Pipe Stabbing With Learned Stereo Vision and Residual Reinforcement Learning

Show abstract
A joint of pipe run into the hole swings from the elevator over the well and strikes a fixed stabbing guide at whatever speed the swing has. StabBot, a robotic stabbing system, predicts the swing and meets the pipe at its own speed. A pendulum Kalman filter tuned from the pipe tally tracks the joint in stereo images. A six-axis arm with an open V-shaped roller head closes in at matched velocity, confirms the last millimetres by touch, aims the pin over the box when the elevator hangs off centre, and follows the block down with lateral compliance. A ResNet-18 keypoint network locates the pipe, pin nose and box mouth in 3-D, and a residual reinforcement-learning policy refines the capture. On 200 paired random jobs with nine sizes from 2-3/8-in. tubing to 20-in. casing, StabBot seated every joint with the pipe touching only rollers, against 74% for a conventional fixed guide. Median contact speed fell from 18.4 to 2.3 cm/s and median peak head-pipe force from 11.3 to 1.4 kN, and the force was lower in all 200 jobs (Wilcoxon $p < 10^{-30}$). The network locates the pipe with 0.4-mm median error in 2.3 ms per stereo pair. With the network in the loop, success stayed at 100% under 20 mm of added noise, 300-ms delay, 85% frame loss, 20-mm calibration offset, 20-cm elevator offset and 40-cm swings. All results come from a 1:4 Froude-scaled MuJoCo model and are reported at full scale.
