Research Landscape

Today's 30 papers are dominated by the machinery around vision-language-action models. Several look inside the VLA stack: the gradient-routing study (8) asks whether the action expert should train the VLM, Asynchronous Robot Policies (12) split slow reasoning from fast control, HiLo-WAM (24) splits long-horizon task evolution from local dynamics, and LoopQ (16) audits quantization for deployment. The common thread is that monolithic VLAs are being decomposed along time scales and training signals.

A second theme is where robot data comes from. DreamX (1), RoboTryOn (11), PyRUA-DataFarm (5) and GUILD (3) all attack teleoperation cost, but differently: video-model latent actions, human-to-robot video translation, an LLM agent that builds the simulation and renders real-looking video, and active choice of what to collect. Trusting Imagined Futures (29) adds a caution that generated or imagined supervision needs auditing, which echoes the independent judges in PyRUA-DataFarm. GSBR (7) and CRONOS (18) cover the next stage, improving pretrained policies with online RL.

A third cluster is safety and guarantees: nested FoV barriers (19), the Backup-Horizon CBF for MARL vehicles (28) and barrier-caged legged VLN (27) all give learned or human-driven systems formal constraints, with attention to feasibility. Papers 9 and 25 add that human perception, not only algorithmic correctness, decides whether a robot behaves acceptably. Overall the field is moving from bigger models to making them fast, cheap to feed, auditable and safe.

Should the Action Expert Train the VLM? Action-Token Supervision and Gradient Routing in Mixture-of-Transformer VLAs

2026-11-03cs.ROAsher James Hancock (h=n/a)
Asher James Hancock, Boyi Li, Yuxiao Chen, Shuhan Tan, Wenhao Ding
Core Contributions
  • Tests a design choice made by default in mixture-of-transformer VLAs: whether the flow-matching action expert's gradients should flow into the VLM (coupling) or be blocked (insulation).
  • Compares those against discrete action-token supervision of the VLM, which carries the same target-action information without gradients from the expert.
  • Questions whether insulation adds protection beyond vision-language co-training, which already aims to preserve VLM capability, and what it costs in task-relevant representations.
  • Gives practitioners a controlled comparison where most recipes just inherit one setting.
Show abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) models increasingly pair a pre-trained vision-language model (VLM) with a flow-matching expert that conditions its action predictions on the VLM's key-value representations. Those representations must carry information useful for control, yet the VLM was never trained for the action expert's flow-matching objective. Two signals can address this: gradients backpropagated from the expert's loss (coupling), or VLM supervision to predict discrete tokens encoding the same target actions. Prominent training recipes forgo the first, blocking expert-to-VLM gradients (insulation) to protect the VLM's existing capabilities, while also supervising it on vision-language data and action tokens. Because vision-language co-training also aims to preserve those capabilities, the additional protection provided by insulation is unclear. At the same time, insulation removes a direct signal about which features continuous action prediction requires. On the other hand, action-token supervision offers an alternative way to learn action-relevant representations, which may explain why insulation succeeds in practice. We disentangle these factors in autonomous driving and manipulation by independently varying action-token supervision and whether the expert's loss updates the VLM. In open-loop driving, insulation is competitive with coupling when action tokens are supervised; without them, coupling reduces trajectory-prediction error by roughly 25%. When both components are trained jointly from the outset, coupling increases closed-loop driving route progress by approximately 17% with action-token supervision and 40% without it, and similarly improves robot manipulation performance under both settings. These findings show that the value of gradient insulation depends on the VLM's action supervision and the evaluation setting, challenging insulation as a default VLA training recipe.

Asynchronous Robot Policies

2026-11-03cs.RO, cs.AISombit Dey (h=n/a)
Sombit Dey, Luc Van Gool, Danda Pani Paudel
Core Contributions
  • Targets the latency problem of large VLAs, where slow semantic reasoning sits in the main control loop and degrades reactivity.
  • A big VLM updates a semantic memory at low frequency, while a lightweight vision-action expert conditioned on that memory generates actions at a much higher rate.
  • Decoupling the two lets scale for reasoning and agility for control grow independently.
Show abstract

Scaling vision-language foundation models has significantly advanced generalist robot manipulation, but the increased model scale introduces computational latency that degrades real-time physical reactivity. Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architectures execute high-level semantic reasoning and action ex- ecution sequentially within the primary control loop, creating a fundamental tension between semantic-reasoning capability and robot agility. To address this, we propose Asynchronous Robot Policy (ARP), an architectural framework that decouples semantic reasoning from low-level action generation using asynchronous processes operating at independent frequencies. In ARP, a high-capacity Vision-Language Model (VLM) asynchronously updates a semantic memory at low fre- quency, while a lightweight Vision-Action Expert, conditioned on this semantic memory, generates robot actions at a much higher control frequency. Such a formulation, enables having a fast perception-action loop without compromising on the semantic capability. To overcome asynchronous memory staleness, ARP trains the vision-action expert on the semantic staleness and execution delay, with temporally mismatched observation across processes. On Franka manipulation tasks, ARP achieves an 18% higher success rate and 20% less time need for execution in quasi-static setups, while 3-5 times baseline performance in dynamic simulation environments. Furthermore, ARP enables operating the VLM at 10 times lower than its nominal frequency with no significant performance loss making it much more efficient than the baseline. These results highlight the architectural advantages of modeling robot policies as asynchronous processes, enabling real-world reactive deployment without sacrificing the scale of foundation models.

LoopQ: Fair, Closed-Loop Evaluation of Quantized Vision-Language-Action Models

2026-11-03cs.ROSyed Umer Bukhari (h=n/a)
Syed Umer Bukhari, Magnus Almgren, Ahmed Ali-Eldin
Core Contributions
  • Points out that VLA quantization papers are hard to compare because they differ in checkpoints, layers and engines; LoopQ fixes those and varies only method and kernels.
  • Evaluates RTN, AWQ, GPTQ, ActQuant and QuantVLA on four VLAs over more than 180,000 LIBERO and LIBERO-plus episodes, on an H100, L4 and Jetson AGX Thor.
  • Also tests whether four public VLA-quantization releases can be reproduced, so the benchmark doubles as a reproducibility audit.
Show abstract

Post-training quantization (PTQ) for vision-language-action (VLA) models promises smaller and faster VLA deployments. However, when analyzing existing VLA quantization works, we realised that it is hard to reason about the performance of the suggested quantization method compared to existing, similar, or in many cases, simpler PTQ methods. We present LoopQ, a closed-loop benchmark that compares multiple quantization policies on the same checkpoint, layers and engine, varying only the method and the kernels. We evaluate RTN, AWQ, GPTQ, ActQuant and QuantVLA on four VLAs with more than 180,000 episodes of LIBERO and LIBERO-plus, compare against QVLA's reported results on the same layers, and benchmark whether the four public VLA-quantization releases can be reproduced. We benchmark the methods on an NVIDIA H100, an L4, and a Jetson AGX Thor. Our results show that while quantization reliably saves memory, 2-3x for real low-bit models, VLA-specific PTQ methods underperform compared to simpler generic baselines. At 4 bits, RTN, AWQ and GPTQ match or exceed ActQuant and QuantVLA, plain RTN matches the calibrated methods, and plain AWQ matches the success QVLA reports for itself. Standard LIBERO hides the losses that do exist: configurations with no significant LIBERO loss lose 1.5-4.3 points under paired LIBERO-plus perturbations. Speed is a weaker story. At batch 1 on the H100, no quantized pi0.5 or OpenVLA-OFT configuration is faster than its 16-bit model (1.06-1.58x slower on library kernels); only the L4 with int8 kernels gains (4-14%), the Thor is slower on every 4-bit configuration, and the engine alone changes latency by 2.1x. Profiling explains why: pi0.5's quantized backbone is compute-bound, while its memory-bound action expert, 63% of GPU time and 13% of the parameters, stays in 16 bits.

HiLo-WAM: Decoupling Long-Horizon Task Evolution and Local Dynamics in World Action Models

2026-11-03cs.ROXingyu Wang (h=n/a)
Xingyu Wang, Yanjiang Guo, Zhijing Ding, Jiaxin Fang, Zeyu Lai
Core Contributions
  • Separates long-horizon task evolution from dense local interaction dynamics, which a single short-horizon world action model entangles.
  • A sparse long-horizon visual stream carries history and future task evolution, a dense stream models local robot-object dynamics, with both in a dual-DiT architecture tied to action generation.
  • Gets long-range context without the uncertainty and compute of densely extending the prediction horizon.
Show abstract

World Action Models (WAMs) improve robotic manipulation by jointly modeling visual dynamics and robot actions. However, most existing WAMs model dense short-horizon visual dynamics, capturing local interactions but providing limited long-range task context. Extending prediction horizons increases uncertainty and computation while entangling task-level evolution with fine-grained local dynamics, obscuring their dependencies and distinct roles in action generation. We propose HiLo-WAM, which decouples long-horizon task evolution from local visual dynamics while jointly modeling their interaction with robot actions. A sparse long-horizon visual stream captures historical task context and future evolution, while a dense local dynamics visual stream models short-horizon robot-object interactions. The two streams operate at different temporal scales within a unified dual-DiT architecture and guide actions through structured cross-stream attention. Progressive inference further reduces unnecessary computation through stream-wise partial denoising, joint denoising, and dynamic skipping. Experiments demonstrate strong long-horizon manipulation performance: HiLo-WAM achieves a 93.70% average success rate on RoboTwin 2.0 and a 17.43 progress score on RoboDojo long-horizon tasks, with robust in-domain and OOD performance on the Fourier GR-3 robot. Ablations confirm the complementary contributions of long-horizon task evolution and local interaction dynamics, while temporal-scale analyses highlight the importance of balancing temporal coverage and resolution. Progressive inference further achieves a 2.93x speedup while improving performance.

Trusting Imagined Futures: Auditing Imagined Rewards for VLA Post-Training

2026-11-03cs.RO, cs.CVZicheng Kong (h=n/a)
Zicheng Kong, Zhenbo Xu, Haoyan Zhang, Jinghan Yang, Zhenzhen Li
Core Contributions
  • Targets a failure of world-model-based reward: high predicted reward can pick trajectories with poor real execution quality.
  • A multidimensional reward model predicts a utility score plus five execution-quality dimensions; an auditor uses predictions and uncertainty to detect learned overestimation.
  • A candidate replaces the existing trajectory only if its advantage exceeds a margin and audit risk passes a threshold; otherwise real supervision is kept for behavior cloning.
Show abstract

Video world models enable candidate actions to be evaluated through imagined outcomes, but high predicted rewards can favor trajectories with poor execution quality. We introduce Trusting Imagined Futures (TIF), an offline supervision selection method for vision-language-action (VLA) post-training that audits predicted rewards using execution feedback. A multidimensional reward model predicts a utility score for candidate ranking and five dimensions of execution quality. An auditor uses reward predictions and uncertainty to identify overestimation patterns learned from paired imagined videos and real executions. TIF accepts the candidate with the highest predicted utility when its advantage over the existing trajectory exceeds a margin and its audit risk passes a threshold; otherwise, it retains the existing supervision. The retained real trajectories then supervise behavior cloning. Across four LIBERO suites and three training seeds, TIF achieves a mean success rate of 76.25%, improving over training on the existing trajectories by 3.00 percentage points with the same trajectory count and training budget. Adding audit gating yields 1.47 percentage points with the reward model and advantage margin held fixed. The gains persist against advantage ranking and logistic regression risk selection at matched replacement counts. On recorded videos from three real robot tasks, TIF increases clean completion among selected trajectories from 58.33% to 70.00% across 60 candidate pools.

DreamX: Video Diffusion Model is a Universal Latent Action Learner

2026-11-03cs.CV, cs.AI, cs.ROJisoo Kim (h=n/a)
Jisoo Kim, Chaehyun Kim, Junsu Kim, Jiho Park, Seongchan Kim
Core Contributions
  • Instead of training a latent action model from scratch on visual transitions, it reuses a pretrained video diffusion model's spatiotemporal features, so the generative prior helps separate action from appearance.
  • Each latent video frame is compressed 300x (1,200 to 4 tokens), which makes the latent action a cheap, shared interface across humans, robots and environments.
  • The shared space allows any-to-any video transfer and zero-shot action transfer, and makes video-diffusion policies cheaper by learning in the compact latent space.
  • Reported state of the art among latent action models on LARY-Bench.
Show abstract

Learning a universal action representation from video requires modeling dynamics that are consistent across different executions of the same action yet invariant to changes in embodiment and appearance. Existing latent action models infer actions from visual transitions, but their representations often entangle the underlying action with irrelevant visual variations, limiting transfer across embodiments. We introduce DreamX, which repurposes a pretrained video diffusion model as a universal latent action learner. Our approach extracts compact latent actions from the video model's rich spatiotemporal features, compressing each latent video frame by $\boldsymbol{300\times}$ ($\boldsymbol{1{,}200 \rightarrow 4}$ tokens). In addition, by leveraging the video model's generative prior, these latent actions capture action-relevant dynamics disentangled from visual appearance. The resulting latent space serves as a shared action interface across embodiments (human and robot), environments, and action parameterizations. On LARY-Bench, DreamX achieves state-of-the-art performance among latent action models. Moreover, actions encoded in one setting remain effective in another: the latent space supports any-to-any video transfer and zero-shot action transfer across diverse embodiments and environments. Furthermore, this shared interface also enables policy learning in a compact latent action space, reducing the computational cost of video-diffusion-based policies.

GUILD: Guided Iterative Data Collection for Imitation Learning

2026-11-03cs.RO, cs.LGShivin Dass (h=n/a)
Shivin Dass, Kevin Nguyen, Rohan Yelandur, Junhong Xu, Roberto Martín-Martín
Core Contributions
  • Moves data curation from after collection (filtering) to before it: the system decides which data to collect next, so uninformative demonstrations are never recorded.
  • Closes the loop by alternating between task proposal (guided by vision-language semantic priors) and evaluating how the new data affects the policy.
  • Matters because teleoperation time is the bottleneck in imitation learning; directing operators toward high-value tasks raises return per hour.
Show abstract

Imitation learning policies in robotics are trained on datasets collected through costly real-world teleoperation. Often, the data-collection process is unguided, relying on the teleoperator's intuition, resulting in data that contributes little to overall policy performance. Recent work has shown that curating such unguided datasets can substantially improve policy performance, but these approaches operate by filtering existing datasets after collection and therefore do not prevent the acquisition of uninformative data. In this work, we propose GUILD, a novel Guided Data Collection (GDC) method that actively reasons about which data should be collected to maximize downstream policy performance. We introduce an iterative framework that alternates between 1) task proposal and data collection and 2) data evaluation to close the loop. For task proposal, we leverage semantic priors from vision-language models to identify relevant environments, object configurations, and goals for data collection. However, since semantic relevance does not imply policy improvement, we perform data evaluation. By leveraging predictive data attribution we approximate how the collected trajectories influence policy performance on a target deployment setting. This signal is then used as feedback to guide subsequent data collection, closing the data collection loop. We evaluate our approach in simulation and real robot settings, demonstrating that guided data collection substantially improves sample efficiency and deployment performance compared to passive data collection strategies, multiple baselines and experienced roboticists. More information at https://robin-lab.cs.utexas.edu/guild2026/

PyRUA-DataFarm: LLM Agent as a Cost-Efficient Teleoperator for Real-Ready Robot Data

2026-11-03cs.RO, cs.AIKewei Zhang (h=n/a)
Kewei Zhang, Juncheng Ma, Guodong Lv, Duomin Wang, Feixiang Ruan
Core Contributions
  • Asks whether a coding agent can do the teleoperator's job: from one calibration episode it rebuilds the scene in simulation and writes an expert controller and success judge.
  • The simulator supplies exact actions, while a video model conditioned on real images renders multi-view photographic video consistently, giving data in the real robot's visual domain.
  • Independent judges certify the expert and every rendered episode before admission, guarding against silent bad data.
  • Evaluated on five real-robot tasks, with policies trained on the generated data deployed on the physical robot.
Show abstract

Teleoperation remains the default way to collect robot demonstrations, but every episode costs robot and operator time, and every new task starts from scratch. We ask whether a coding agent can take over the teleoperator's job. In our DataFarm, the agent receives a data request and one calibration episode of the robot in the target scene and rebuilds the task in simulation: it writes the scene, an expert controller, and a success judge. The simulator supplies exact actions, and a video model conditioned on real images renders each rollout as photographic video, generating all camera views together consistently. The output is real-ready data: synchronized camera views labelled with the robot's actions, on which a policy can be trained directly and deployed on the real robot. Independent judges certify the expert and every rendered episode before admission. On five real-robot tasks, policies trained only on farm data match policies trained on as many teleoperated episodes (93 vs. 84 successes in 100 trials), and under an unseen tablecloth and coloured light they keep 87 successes where the teleoperation-trained policies fall to 46. Adapting a task is fully automatic and costs \$21 of API usage on average, paid once per task, plus \$5 for a render contract; every further episode costs \$0.80 on average for simulation, rendering, and review, with no robot or operator. One hour of three-view data for a task costs \$107 on average, these costs included, slightly below a quote of \$117 for teleoperation in China and below \$290 in labour alone in North America.

RoboTryOn: Synthesizing Dexterous Robot Demonstrations from Human Videos via Virtual Try-On

2026-11-03cs.RO, cs.AISeungyong Lee (h=n/a)
Seungyong Lee, Suhyeok Jang, Minyoung Kim, Jinwoo Shin
Core Contributions
  • Frames human-to-robot translation as virtual try-on: keep the demonstrated motion, scene and objects, swap the human for the target robot given a reference image.
  • Translates keyframes autoregressively and uses them to guide video synthesis, avoiding the visual artifacts of overlaying rendered robots and the action gaps of pose retargeting for high-DoF hands.
  • Aims to turn egocentric human video into dexterous demonstrations, replacing teleoperation when adapting robot foundation models to new tasks.
Show abstract

Robot foundation models have shown remarkable performance in robot control, yet adapting them to a new task still relies heavily on teleoperated demonstrations. Egocentric human videos offer a scalable substitute, but overlaying rendered robots on human videos or retargeting human poses into robot actions leaves significant visual or action gaps for high-DoF robot embodiments. To address this limitation, we propose RoboTryOn, which treats human-to-robot translation as virtual try-on: try-on keeps the person and swaps the garment, RoboTryOn keeps the demonstrated motion and swaps the human for the robot. Specifically, RoboTryOn converts a human video into a video of the target robot specified by a reference image, while preserving the original task, objects, and background. To maintain visual consistency, we translate selected keyframes autoregressively and use them to guide video synthesis with motion conditioned on the human hand skeleton. Additionally, we use an inverse dynamics model (IDM) that incorporates depth features to infer action labels from synthesized videos for policy learning. Our experiments across multiple humanoid embodiments show that policies trained with our synthetic data improve the average success rate on novel tasks, including challenging bimanual manipulation, from 2% to 60%, without any real robot demonstration of those tasks. Moreover, training with our synthetic data matches the performance of policies trained solely on teleoperated demonstrations at approximately a third of the collection cost, demonstrating the cost efficiency of our approach.

PolicyIK: High-Precision Inverse Kinematics for Robot Arms with Reinforcement Learning

2026-11-03cs.RO, cs.LGYuhan Liu (h=n/a)
Yuhan Liu, Xinqi Liu, Zixuan Dong, Keith W. Ross, Shenji Wan
Core Contributions
  • Treats inverse kinematics as a goal-conditioned RL problem, targeting millimeter precision, which many VLA pipelines depend on when they hand off end-effector commands.
  • The policy observes its accumulated joint-reference offset relative to measured joints, so it keeps the steady-state offset a position controller needs at rest instead of fighting it.
  • Two-scale pose errors with an error-dependent action gain resolve small residuals without damping large motions; progress and dwell rewards teach it to reach tolerance and stay there.
  • Replaces hand-tuned IK controllers and avoids collecting costly physical-robot training data.
Show abstract

Inverse kinematics (IK) converts end-effector pose commands into joint motion, and many vision-language-action (VLA) policies rely on it to act on a given arm and to transfer across embodiments. Precise IK remains laborious: redundancy and singularities complicate the inverse mapping, execution depends on hand-tuned controllers, and training data on physical robots are costly to collect. We present PolicyIK, a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning policy for high-precision IK. It updates joint-position references from pose feedback and observes its accumulated reference relative to the measured joints, so it can retain the offset that the position controller needs at rest. Two-scale pose errors with an error-dependent action gain resolve millimeter residuals while preserving large motions. Progress and dwell rewards teach it to both reach the tolerance and stay within it. In simulation, a single attempt by the trained PPO policy keeps the mean errors over the final second within 1 mm and 0.2° on 93.5% of held-out tasks, and a deployment protocol raises success to 98.9%. When trained directly on a physical arm with an off-policy RL learner, PolicyIK achieves higher reaching and holding success than all four classical IK controllers under the same time budgets. In simulated pick-and-place, PolicyIK executes the end-effector commands of the $π_{0.5}$ VLA policy with 95.7% success. These results show that reinforcement learning can provide high-precision inverse kinematics for robot arms. Code is available at https://github.com/Tinaliu0123/PolicyIK.

PACT: Pose-Robust Actuation Co-Design for Tendon-Driven Robotic Hands

2026-11-03cs.ROPrashant Kumar (h=n/a)
Prashant Kumar, Rachael Granberry, Taylor Niehues, Andrew A. Stanley, Gabe Pirie
Core Contributions
  • Co-optimizes tendon routing, moment arms and capstan sizes together, avoiding the sequential design that satisfies force at the expense of speed (or the reverse).
  • A feasible-force formulation enforces actuator bounds and joint equilibrium at every pose, and a max-min objective maximizes force coverage at the weakest pose.
  • Pose robustness matters for humanoid hands, where a design tuned to one grasp can be too weak in another.
  • On a 4-DoF finger it yields an asymmetric design rather than the uniform default.
Show abstract

Humanoid robotic hands must provide sufficient force, speed, and backdrivability within a human-scale envelope. Tendon-driven transmissions address this challenge by relocating actuators to the palm and forearm, but routing paths, moment arms, and capstan dimensions jointly determine both force capability and joint speed, so selecting them sequentially satisfies one requirement at the expense of the other. This paper presents a pose-robust actuation co-design for tendon-driven robot hands (PACT) framework that optimizes tendon routing, moment arms, and capstan dimensions under pose-dependent force requirements and drivetrain-bandwidth constraints. A feasible-force formulation enforces actuator bounds and equilibrium at all joints, while a max-min objective maximizes task-force coverage at the weakest pose. Applied to a four-degree-of-freedom finger, the framework produces an asymmetric N + 1 tendon topology using quasi-direct-drive actuators. Across eight poses, the design achieves task-force coverage of 0.88-1.00 and a predicted 5 Hz minimum isolated-joint flexion/extension bandwidth within a 70th-percentile human-size envelope. Prototype measurements deviate from predictions by up to 24.5%, primarily due to unmodeled transmission losses. These results indicate that co-designing routing with the drivetrain can meet pose-dependent force requirements without relaxing the bandwidth constraint, paving the way for human-sized robotic hands with human-level performance.

DexHOT: Learning Hand-Object Configuration Transfer for Dexterous Manipulation

2026-11-03cs.ROZhewei Gui (h=n/a)
Zhewei Gui, Anthony Bolton, Jiyao Zhang, Zeyuan Chen, Qiyang Yan
Core Contributions
  • Specifies dexterous goals as hand-object configurations (hand articulation plus object pose relative to the wrist) instead of only object poses, so useful contacts are explicit.
  • Learns a reusable relation-conditioned transfer primitive from synthetic feasible configuration pairs, needing no human reference motions, and chains subgoals by closed-loop tracking.
  • A single policy reaches 80.5% transfer success on seen and 69.4% on unseen objects across 120 objects on DexHOTBench.
Show abstract

Dexterous manipulation involves not only moving objects, but also arranging the hand around them to maintain useful contacts and enable subsequent actions. Yet many learning-based systems specify goals primarily through object poses or trajectories, leaving hand-object configurations to emerge implicitly. We introduce DexHOT, which formulates dexterous manipulation as hand-object configuration transfer: moving between feasible configurations that jointly specify hand articulation and object pose relative to the wrist. DexHOT learns a reusable relation-conditioned transfer primitive from synthetic feasible configuration pairs, without human reference motions, and composes long-horizon behaviors through closed-loop tracking of configuration subgoals. On DexHOTBench with 120 objects, a single policy achieves 80.5% transfer success on seen objects and 69.4% on unseen objects; on a real dexterous hand, it achieves 61.5% success over 200 real-robot transfers and 90% task success across six demonstration-guided tasks. These results show that explicit hand-object goals provide an expressive and composable interface for dexterous manipulation. More details and videos are available at https://dex-hot.github.io/.

HLO-AMP: Hierarchical Loco-Manipulation from Object-Aware Motion Priors

2026-11-03cs.ROJean-Pierre Sleiman (h=n/a)
Jean-Pierre Sleiman, Bilal Hammoud, He Li, Jonathan Foster, Sylvain Bertrand
Core Contributions
  • Trains the whole-body controller with the manipulated object physically present, rather than approximating payload effects with random end-effector force disturbances.
  • Argues random forces miss the distributed multi-contact wrench a real payload applies across arms and torso, which matters most near payload limits.
  • Uses motion imitation of retargeted MoCap references as an object-aware motion prior, giving more natural movement than manual reward shaping.
Show abstract

Hierarchical pipelines for humanoid loco-manipulation typically pair a task-agnostic whole-body controller (WBC) with a downstream task policy. Such WBCs are often trained through manual reward shaping, which yields visibly machine-like motions; more consequentially, they are trained without the manipulated object in the scene. Object dynamics are instead approximated through randomized end-effector force disturbances, a proxy that fails to reproduce the distributed multi-contact wrench a real payload applies across the arms and torso, with the gap becoming critical near the platform's payload limit. In this work, we train the low-level (LL) controller itself as an object-aware motion prior: a single policy trained via motion imitation on retargeted motion-capture (MoCap) references that span the core motion primitives of the downstream task, with the manipulated object physically present in the scene. This single stage inherits natural human-like coordination from the references and absorbs the realistic contact wrench at its source, while exposing the same compact task-space command interface used by command-tracking WBCs. A reference-free high-level (HL) policy is then trained on top, with its action channel anchored to the empirical envelope of the low-level training data so that it generalizes the priors to a long-horizon multi-stage task while structurally preventing queries that would push the low level off-distribution. We instantiate the framework on a Unitree G1 for bimanual box pick-and-place: the same deployed policy generalizes across object masses and dimensions, initial and goal poses, and reactively recovers from intentional drops mid-carry. GSBR: Geometric Support Behavior Regularization for Online RL

2026-11-03cs.RO, cs.LGCharles Xu (h=n/a)
Charles Xu, Jacob Berg, Liyiming Ke, Sergey Levine
