🔭 Research Landscape

The sharpest thread in today's batch is that how a policy is executed now matters as much as the policy itself. The reactivity study (#22) shows that, for a pretrained pi-0.5, simply changing the execution horizon on a conveyor task gains as much as RL fine-tuning, and that keeping successive action chunks continuous beats merely cutting latency. ActionCodec2 (#23) attacks the same staleness problem from the representation side: its streaming tokenizer releases actions step by step so the policy can fold state feedback into a chunk. Bind-WAM (#29) asks the analogous question for world-action models (only imagine the future when language-object binding is uncertain), and MOSAIC (#7) and GraSLA (#14) both argue that VLAs should stop inferring geometry implicitly and instead be handed obstacle masks, depth tokens or grasp hypotheses.

A second, almost meta, theme is frontier models acting directly as robot engineers and operators. Robo-SWE (#1) finds that today's best coding agents resolve only about half of real robotics-repository issues (45.8-56.5%), while JustRUA (#3) shows that a stripped-down harness with full episode history, early motion stopping and scene geometry lifts a GPT-6 robot-use agent on RoboDojo from 29.7 to 50.3 with no learning at all. Embodied-Corrector (#16) supplies the missing piece of that loop, training a model to say why execution deviated and how to recover. Read together, these papers suggest the bottleneck for agentic robotics is increasingly the scaffolding, and the evaluation of that scaffolding, rather than raw model capability.

Third, contact remains the hard frontier and the community is building instruments to see it. DexRemo (#4) shows fingertip forces only help when expressed in a shared wrist frame; RUMI (#10) makes force-aware demonstrations possible for the nonlinear Robotiq gripper; the touch-latent study (#9) and granular scooping work (#30) are about choosing representations that preserve physical properties. Meanwhile, the humanoid e-stop paper (#8) is a sobering counterpoint to the push toward capability: on a full-size H1-2, every protective stop caused a fall, which is arguably a more dangerous outcome than the fault that triggered it. Uncertainty-aware search (#6, #13) closes the batch with a shared lesson: maintaining an explicit, never-overconfident belief about the target is what makes stopping decisions trustworthy.

MOSAIC: Modular Obstacle and Spatial-Aware Framework for Manipulation In Cluttered Scenes

2026-11-11 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Richard Shi (h=n/a)
Richard Shi, Mohammad Mahdavian, Mehrdad Momeni Zadeh, Mohammed Elmahgiubi, Amir Rasouli
Core Contributions
  • Argues that VLAs fail in clutter because obstacle relations are left implicit, and adds explicit role-specific masks for target, destination and obstacles as an auxiliary grounding task.
  • Converts target and destination masks plus reconstructed depth into geometric tokens injected straight into the action expert, rather than only into the vision encoder.
  • Adds attention supervision that emphasises obstacles near the robot body, then feeds those attention features to later action-expert layers, all without changing the pretrained architecture.
  • Improves success by 5.1% in clutter and 9.7% under visual perturbation on RoboPRO, with up to 25% gains in the hardest environment.
Show abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated strong performance in robotic manipulation, yet most rely primarily on 2D visual observations and language instructions, leaving scene geometry and obstacle relationships to be inferred implicitly. This limitation is particularly problematic in cluttered environments, where small spatial errors can lead to unintended contacts or collisions. We propose MOSAIC, a modular spatial- and obstacle-aware framework that can be integrated with pretrained VLA policies to improve object-level scene understanding and action generation. Our method augments the VLA backbone with an auxiliary semantic grounding task that predicts role-specific masks for the target object, destination region, and surrounding obstacles. We further combine the target and destination masks with depth estimated from 3D scene reconstruction to construct role-specific geometric tokens that are injected directly into the action expert. In addition, we propose obstacle-aware attention supervision that emphasizes obstacles near the robot body during task execution. The resulting attention features are subsequently injected into later action-expert layers to further guide action generation. Together, these components provide explicit semantic, geometric, and obstacle-aware cues while preserving the underlying pretrained VLA architecture. We evaluate our framework on pretrained VLA model using the RoboPRO benchmark, including visual perturbation tests, and further validate it on real-world manipulation tasks. MOSAIC improves the success rate by 5.1% in cluttered scenes, and by 9.7% under visual perturbations, with gains of up to 25.0% in the most challenging environment. We will release our code and dataset upon acceptance at the following Github page: https://mmahdavian.github.io/MOSAIC/

GraSLA: A Lightweight Spatial-Language-Action Model with Grasp Hypotheses as Geometric Priors

2026-11-11 cs.RO 👤 Jibao Yuan (h=n/a)
Jibao Yuan, Zixiang Zhao, Wensong Xu, Siqi Lu, Shaofei Chen
Core Contributions
  • Combines the behavioural diversity of 6-DoF grasp detectors with the closed-loop responsiveness of continuous-action policies, which normally trade off.
  • Grasp hypotheses act as geometric priors, fused with spatial and language foundation-model features through bidirectional cross-attention.
  • Remains lightweight while reaching 99.30% average success on LIBERO, suggesting explicit grasp structure can substitute for model scale.
Show abstract
This paper focuses on improving the behavioral diversity and effectiveness of language-conditioned manipulation policies trained through imitation learning. Recently, continuous-action policies have become mainstream in robotic manipulation, but the lack of diverse demonstrations tends to restrict their behavioral diversity. For grasping, a fundamental subtask of manipulation, 6-DoF grasp detection provides diverse grasp hypotheses, but open-loop execution constrains responsiveness in dynamic environments. This motivates us to combine the diversity of grasp hypotheses with the adaptability of continuous-action policies. To this end, we propose GraSLA, a lightweight and effective spatial-language-action model with grasp hypotheses as geometric priors. In particular, we employ spatial and language foundation models to encode scene geometry and task semantics, and integrate them with grasp hypotheses through bidirectional cross-attention for continuous action prediction. On LIBERO, GraSLA achieves an average success rate of 99.30%.

What Makes a VLA Reactive? A Study of Execution Strategies for Dynamic Manipulation

2026-11-11 cs.ROcs.CVcs.LG 👤 Zhuoyue Li (h=n/a)
Zhuoyue Li, Zhendong Han, Pankhuri Vanjani, Wenting Li, Rudolf Lioutikov
Core Contributions
  • A systematic study of why VLA action chunks go stale on moving objects, varying prediction and execution horizons, sync vs async inference and smoothing methods on pi-0.5.
  • Finds the best prediction horizon is about one second, while the best execution horizon is checkpoint-dependent, so it must be tuned per model.
  • Methods that keep consecutive chunks continuous beat methods that only reduce latency, and short horizons pay off mainly under asynchronous execution.
  • On a conveyor task, retuning the execution horizon matched latent-space RL adaptation, implying policy and execution pipeline must be evaluated together.
Show abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models predict chunks of future actions, and when objects move, those chunks go stale before they are executed. We study what makes a pretrained VLA reactive, using \pihalf{} on two dynamic simulation benchmarks and three real-robot tasks. We vary the prediction and execution horizons, compare synchronous and asynchronous inference, test methods that deliver actions sooner or keep successive chunks continuous, and adapt the policy with motion cues and latent-space reinforcement learning. The best prediction horizon is about one second, and the best execution horizon depends on the checkpoint. Short horizons help more with asynchronous execution, and methods that keep the trajectory continuous outperform those that only reduce latency. Adapting the policy repairs targeted failures, but on the conveyor task changing the execution horizon gained as much. Reactivity depends on the execution pipeline as much as on the policy, so both should be evaluated together.

ActionCodec2: A Streaming Action Tokenizer

2026-11-11 cs.RO 👤 Zibin Dong (h=n/a)
Zibin Dong, Yicheng Liu, Shiduo Zhang, Tianyuan Yuan, Baijun Ye
Core Contributions
  • Unlike chunk tokenizers that must decode a whole chunk before acting, ActionCodec2 releases actions incrementally while bounding reconstruction error.
  • Each step is quantized relative to the decoder's reconstructed state, so primitives correct accumulated residuals; the minimal primitive set is solved as a covering problem with exact translation bounds.
  • A universal codec fitted on 20k hours across 24 embodiments averages 8.8 tokens/s at 15 Hz, and outperforms other tokenizers with the same VLA backbone in sim and zero-shot real tests.
  • Streaming tokens enable intra-chunk feedback under disturbances and moving objects, and double as a control language from which VLM agents build reusable macros.
Show abstract
Most action tokenizers for autoregressive vision language action (VLA) models compress motion into chunks that must be decoded in full before their actions become executable. We introduce ActionCodec2, a non-neural streaming tokenizer that releases actions incrementally while keeping trajectory reconstruction error within a prescribed tolerance. Its encoder quantizes each step relative to the decoder's reconstructed state, so the selected motion primitive corrects residual error rather than allowing errors from independently quantized increments to accumulate. Finding the smallest such primitive set is a geometric covering problem; we derive the exact minimum for translation and constant-factor-tight bounds for rotation. Set-BPE then losslessly merges co-occurring primitive symbols across coordinates and neighboring steps, without a fixed coordinate ordering. A universal version fitted on 20k hours of data from 24 embodiments averages 8.8 tokens per second for 15 Hz end-effector motion on this corpus. With the same VLA backbone, ActionCodec2 outperforms the compared tokenizers on three simulation benchmarks and in zero-shot real-robot evaluation. Its streaming representation allows policies to incorporate state feedback within an action chunk, substantially improving performance over open-loop execution under randomized dynamics, external disturbances, and moving objects. The same tokens also can serve as a direct control interface for VLM agents, which compose successful action sequences into reusable macros. These agent-built macros reduce control decisions and thinking-token usage across attempts and transfer to a held-out task. More results are available at https://zibindong.github.io/actioncodec2-page/

Bind-WAM: Binding-Uncertainty-Guided Selective Future Imagination in World Action Models

2026-11-11 cs.RO 👤 Chenhao Qiu (h=n/a)
Chenhao Qiu, Shuyi Chen, Songen Gu, Shufeng Nan, Tianyu Wang
Core Contributions
  • Questions the default of imagining a visual future at every replanning step, which is costly for world action models and often unnecessary.
  • Introduces language-object binding uncertainty as the trigger: imagine only when the current view does not stably support task-relevant action.
  • Offline pseudo-labels flag reference mismatch, invisibility and identity drift, training a lightweight router that gates imagination at inference.
  • Gives an interpretable compute-allocation signal for WAMs, validated in simulation and on real robots.
Show abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) can improve robot learning by jointly predicting future visual states and actions, but generating a visual future at every replanning step is expensive and sometimes unnecessary. This raises a critical question: when should a robot policy imagine the future? In this paper, we propose Bind-WAM, a binding-uncertainty-guided WAM for selective future imagination. Bind-WAM introduces language-object binding uncertainty, selectively invoking joint future-action generation when the current observation does not support stable task-relevant action generation. An offline labeling pipeline uses complete demonstrations and action context to identify task-relevant objects and detect reference mismatch, invisibility, and identity drift. The resulting pseudo-labels supervise a lightweight router that predicts binding uncertainty from the current observation and language instruction. At inference, a shared WAM policy invokes selective future imagination according to the predicted uncertainty and its change between consecutive replanning steps. Simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate that Bind-WAM can establish binding uncertainty as a strong, interpretable signal for selective future imagination.

Robo-SWE: An Executable Issue-Resolution Dataset Mined from Robot Software Repositories

2026-11-11 cs.SEcs.RO 👤 Vahid Haratian (h=n/a)
Vahid Haratian, Vahid Ansari, Mohammadhossein Malmir, Alois Knoll
Core Contributions
  • Fills a gap left by SWE-bench-style suites: robotics code depends on ROS 2 build tooling, physics engines and timing, so general-purpose coding scores do not transfer; Robo-SWE gives 236 container-reproducible tasks from 28 robotics repos.
  • Every task is validated by execution (developer tests fail before and pass after the fix across repeated offline runs), and a provenance ledger records why each of 1,567 candidate PRs was rejected, making the curation itself auditable.
  • Frontier agents land in a narrow 43.9-56.5% band (Claude Opus 5.5 best, Kimi K3.1 Pro lowest), so roughly half of real robotics maintenance tasks remain out of reach for autonomous coding agents.
  • Ships 3,540 graded attempts with patches and trajectories, giving researchers failure data to study which robotics domains (simulation vs. ROS vs. learning frameworks) agents struggle with.
Show abstract
Coding agents are evaluated mostly on general-purpose software, but domain-specific systems require domain-aware evaluation. Robot software is a particularly challenging case: Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) packages have their own build tools, simulators and learning frameworks are built on physics engines, and behavior that depends on timing and hardware is hard to test. We present Robo-SWE, a dataset of 236 executable issue-resolution tasks mined from merged pull requests in 28 public robotics repositories in four domains: ROS 2 systems, simulation and physics engines, robotics algorithm libraries, and robot-learning frameworks. Each task contains the original issue text, a container recipe for the repository at the commit before the fix, the developers' fix, and the developers' tests, which fail before the fix and pass after it in repeated offline runs. The dataset also keeps a provenance ledger of all 1,567 candidate pull requests, with the stage each reached and the reason for every rejection, and, as a first use, 3,540 graded attempts by five coding-agent setups with recorded patches and 3,529 available trajectories. Across three runs per primary setup, resolve rates range from 45.8% for Claude Sonnet 5.5 to 56.5% for Claude Opus 5.5; GPT-6.1 Sol reaches 54.1% at maximum effort and 52.5% at medium effort, and GPT-6 Astra 51.4%. GLM-5.3 resolves 46.2%, and Kimi K3.1 Pro 43.9%. The tasks are validated by execution; verification of their fairness by human reviewers is future work. The dataset and public leaderboard are available online.

JustRUA: A Controlled Study of GPT-6 Robot-Use Agents

2026-11-11 cs.ROcs.AI 👤 Zefan Wang (h=n/a)
Zefan Wang, Yufan Dang, Xuanwei Shi, Mingqi Gao, Cheng Yin
Core Contributions
  • A controlled ablation that untangles why robot-use agent scores have been rising: prior systems changed harness, action interface, memory and test subset all at once.
  • Finds that the cheapest wins are structural: keep the full episode history in context and stop each motion early so the model can re-observe, rather than adding learned components.
  • Scene geometry is the single most helpful input, and it still helps when depth is predicted from RGB, while experience reuse changes behaviour but is not uniformly beneficial.
  • The resulting minimal harness lifts RoboDojo score from 29.7 to 50.3 without any learned policy or task tuning, a strong baseline that future agent papers will need to beat.
Show abstract
GPT-6 Astra can drive a robot arm directly through a fixed harness, and scores of such robot-use agents on RoboDojo have risen quickly since. Reported systems differ in harness and test subset and often change several designs at once, so it is hard to tell which choices produce the gains. We take these designs apart and test them in one framework, along the harness around the model, the action interface, the reuse of experience and the scene geometry given to the model. A lean harness that keeps the full episode history and stops each motion early goes a long way. Reusing experience changes how the model acts but is not a free lunch, and scene geometry helps most, even when depth is predicted from RGB alone. JustRUA starts from the official L3 harness and keeps only what matters most. Even with no learned policy, self-improvement loop or task-specific tuning, this simple harness raises the score on the RoboDojo simulation benchmark substantially, from 29.7 to 50.3.

Recover, Don't Just Continue: Learning from Feedback for Physical Failure Understanding and Correction

2026-11-11 cs.RO 👤 Miao Pan (h=n/a)
Miao Pan, Kaixiang Yao, Xiaohe Zhou, Xu Wang, Qinfeng Li
Core Contributions
  • Targets a gap in embodied MLLMs: they plan from instructions but cannot explain why execution deviated or how to recover from the current state.
  • Builds recovery data by injecting perturbations at subtask boundaries of successful trajectories and keeping only failures that are observable and recoverable.
  • Structured Judge-Cause-Sub-Fine supervision is trained evaluation-first, then an on-policy distillation step aligns planning under self-generated prefixes with planning under correct ones, fixing error propagation.
  • Improves composite task success by 6.1 points with a frozen VLA and by 13.9 points when steering fixed GPT-5.5 execution.
Show abstract
Multimodal large language models are regarded as the cognitive foundation of embodied intelligence, but planning from instructions alone cannot make them reliable. During execution, actions deviate from intended effects, constrained by the embodiment and environment. An embodied brain must understand why execution deviates, identify what can be reliably realized, and revise decisions using interaction experience. These correspond to causal failure localization, capability-constrained adaptation, and experience-driven revision. Current embodied foundation models largely lack these capabilities. To address this, we introduce Embodied-Corrector. We construct failure-recovery data by injecting perturbations at subtask boundaries of successful trajectories, replaying affected subtasks, and keeping failures that are observable from public evidence and recoverable from the current state. We align actions, consequences, failure causes, and recoveries into Judge-Cause-Sub-Fine supervision. Training uses evaluation-first SFT: the model learns Judge and Cause first, then Sub and Fine conditioned on accurate prefixes. Since deployment uses self-generated prefixes, evaluation errors propagate into recovery planning, and response-level rewards are too coarse to locate affected planning tokens. We therefore use OPD to internalize the planning distribution under accurate prefixes into that under self-generated ones: the model generates plans from self-generated prefixes, while a contrastive forward pass with accurate prefixes compares token probabilities to reinforce or suppress planning tokens. At deployment, this branch is removed. Experiments show stronger failure analysis and corrective planning: with a frozen VLA, composite task success improves by 6.1 percentage points; with fixed GPT-5.5 execution, manipulation and navigation tasks improve by 13.9 percentage points.

Imitating the Imperfect: Offline-to-Online Robust Imitation Learning from Heterogeneous Demonstrators

2026-11-11 cs.LGcs.RO 👤 Cheng Pan (h=n/a)
Cheng Pan, Nan Lu, Kai Arulkumaran, Josie Hughes
Core Contributions
  • Drops the usual assumption that all demonstrators are near-experts and instead jointly infers a per-demonstrator latent expertise level and a state-action optimality score from unlabeled mixed-quality data.
  • The score then becomes a reward for online RL, and online rollouts are fed back to refine the scorer, directly addressing the distribution shift that breaks purely offline filtering approaches.
  • Provides identifiability guarantees for both the optimality score and the recovered policy, which is rare for heterogeneous-demonstrator IL and clarifies when crowd-sourced data can be trusted.
  • Most useful for large teleoperation datasets where operator skill varies but is never labelled.
Show abstract
Imitation learning (IL) typically relies on large-scale offline demonstrations collected from multiple human or algorithmic demonstrators. However, most existing approaches assume these demonstrators are homogeneous or near-optimal experts, a convenient but unrealistic assumption in real-world applications. In practice, demonstrations are often collected from heterogeneous demonstrators with unknown and varying levels of expertise, resulting in highly mixed-quality data. In this work, we study a challenging yet practical setting of imitation learning from such heterogeneous imperfect demonstrators. We propose Latent Expertise and Optimality Scoring based Imitation Learning (LEOS-IL), a principled framework that jointly learns (i) a state-action optimality scoring model and (ii) latent expertise levels for each demonstrator from unlabeled suboptimal offline demonstrations. The learned scoring model is then integrated into an online policy optimization procedure, where the agent is trained to maximize estimated optimality scores while its online rollouts are iteratively leveraged to refine the scoring model, thereby mitigating distributional shift during online adaptation. We further provide theoretical guarantees for optimality score identification and conditional optimal occupancy and policy recovery under explicit sufficient conditions. Experiments on continuous control benchmarks demonstrate that our approach consistently outperforms existing baselines across highly heterogeneous and low-quality demonstration regimes.

Video-Action Models for Data-Efficient Industrial Manipulation

2026-11-11 cs.RO 👤 Eugenio Chisari (h=n/a)
Eugenio Chisari, Stefanos Charalambous, Oliver Sanchez, Thomas Umbach, Michael Gentner
Core Contributions
  • Tests whether video pretraining actually buys sample efficiency on the factory floor, where fixtures and parts limit how many action-labelled demos can be collected.
  • FLUX-mimic couples a video backbone pretrained on web plus industrial footage with an action expert, trained jointly on video and actions rather than using video only as a frozen encoder.
  • Across four automotive tasks on two bimanual platforms it beats all baselines on the dexterous robot and matches pi-0.5 on the gripper robot.
  • Reaches 77.5% success on deformable part kitting with only about two hours of task-specific demonstrations, a concrete data budget useful for industrial planning.
Show abstract
Industrial manipulation requires precise alignment and controlled handling of rigid, deformable, and flexible components. Yet, the benefits of pretrained visuomotor policies remain underexplored in settings where limited access to specialized hardware, components, and fixtures constrains demonstration collection and evaluation. Understanding task-specific data requirements is important for assessing their scalability across diverse processes on the factory floor. We investigate whether video pretraining can support reliable industrial manipulation with limited action data. We propose FLUX-mimic, a video-action model, motivated by the potential of video pretraining to capture motion and interaction patterns that enable sample-efficient training. FLUX-mimic is built on a backbone pretrained on large-scale web and industrial task data, and an action expert to generate robot trajectories. We train our policy by joint video-action training. Across four automotive manufacturing benchmark tasks and two bimanual robot platforms, FLUX-mimic outperforms all considered baselines on the dexterous robot platform and matches pi_{0.5}'s average success rate on the gripper-based robot platform. On a deformable part kitting task, FLUX-mimic reaches 77.5% success with as little as two hours of task-specific action-labeled demonstrations.

  • Benchmarks pi-0, pi-0.5, SmolVLA and GR00T on identical embodiment and long-horizon toilet-cleaning data (250 demos), a fair head-to-head rarely available.

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have shown promising capabilities for robot manipulation, but their performance on accessible mobile bimanual platforms remains underexplored. Reading the Table: Sim-to-Real Game State Detection for the Chef's Hat Card Game

2026-11-11 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Connor Gäde (h=n/a)
Connor Gäde, Matthias Kerzel, Hauke Bünning, Jan-Gerrit Habekost, Mariana Barros
Core Contributions
  • Replaces costly real-world annotation with a fully automated synthetic pipeline that reprojects known 3D coordinates to generate labels, plus domain randomization.
  • Produces 25,413 samples to fine-tune detection and segmentation models that assign cards to game zones.
  • Achieves 0.906 F1 for card-to-zone assignment on a real NICO humanoid, enough to support autonomous play of the Chef's Hat game for HRI studies.
Show abstract
Tabletop games offer a compelling scenario to study the interaction between artificial and human agents, combining social interaction with game state tracking and physical manipulation. To assess the game state, a robot needs to precisely detect closely related game objects, such as different card designs, requiring dedicated training data that is costly to annotate in the real world. Instead, we develop a fully automated synthetic data collection pipeline, using re-projection of known 3D coordinates to generate labels automatically and domain randomization to bridge the reality gap. This approach is used to collect a dataset of 25413 samples to finetune an object detection and a segmentation model, which are combined to assign detected cards to game-relevant zones and infer the game state. When evaluated on a real NICO humanoid robot, our pipeline achieves an F1-score of 0.906 for card-to-zone assignment, thus providing the perceptual foundation for a robot that can autonomously play Chef's Hat against human players.

2026-11-11 cs.RO 👤 Dezhi Sun (h=n/a)

Core Contributions

An integrated training strategy facilitates policy optimization under coupled constraints. Comparative, ablation, and uncertainty experiments validate the effectiveness of PCM-PPO. PCM-PPO provides a feasible and interpretable scheme for autonomous mission planning of Titan deep space probes.
