Research Landscape

The loudest theme today is don't retrain the foundation model — wrap it. ForceHarness (#6) gives an API-only frontier model force sensing by putting self-terminating force primitives beneath the model and translating force traces into physical quantities for it, lifting zero-shot real-arm success from 3.3% to 90%. ReRead (#29) argues the same point from the inside: a failing VLA often already encodes the right information, so a tiny image-free corrector that re-reads the frozen backbone's own caches adds +8 to +23 points on hard RoboTwin tasks. WiTac (#5) is the sensory version of the idea — a pretrained wrist-vision+touch encoder plugged into GR00T and π0.5 without changing their backbones. Together these papers suggest the field is converging on a "frozen core + thin adapters" architecture, and the Robotic Reward Models survey (#7) supplies the uncomfortable counterpoint: across 27 evaluators on 1,117 real executions, dense reward signals track elapsed time more than actual task state, so the evaluators we use to judge such adapters are themselves unreliable.

A second thread is where manipulation data comes from. WinDEX (#27) builds a 1,000-hour metric 3D hand dataset from cheap egocentric stereo rigs, LSA (#25) mines the dense intermediate features of 3D foundation models rather than only the sparse hand trajectories extracted from human video, AsymEEF (#24) re-targets a small demo set across 2/3/5-fingered hand pairs for a 16.6× expansion, and ARMFUL (#3) sidesteps teleoperation entirely by sampling 1.7M whole-arm grasps in simulation. The common bet is that the bottleneck is no longer models but embodiment-matched, physically grounded data — and that simulation or human video can supply it if the representation is right.

Third, there is a quiet but notable push toward guarantees that survive deployment. Making Koopman Guarantees Hold (#22) shows that all 40 standard Koopman controllers with stable latent loops were actually unstable on the real nonlinear plant, and fixes it with first-order tangent matching; BoBDeLaN (#20) bounds how measurement noise can perturb a learned dynamics context; the heavy-duty manipulator paper (#11) tightens CBFs by the measured inner-loop tracking error. The same theme of "stability at the right abstraction" appears in Multi-CONDOR's (#9) Lyapunov-certified multi-attractor primitives and SG-ODOM's (#4) gating of contact updates until the foot is actually stable. On the humanoid side, QTOP (#12) and Driving the Style (#21) disagree productively on how to escape reference-dependence in motion tracking — one by scaling with distributional critics, the other by removing the reference from the actor altogether.

ForceHarness: Turning Force into Action and Physics for Frozen Frontier Models

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 M. Shou (h=53)
Ke Sun, Kevin Yuchen Ma, Yike Zhang, Yanzhe Chen, Mike Zheng Shou
Core Contributions
  • Tackles a structural mismatch: contact dynamics evolve much faster than an API-based frontier model can respond, and such models cannot read raw force streams.
  • Self-terminating force primitives close the fast force loop beneath the model and return control only at contact events; a physics translation layer turns force traces into quantities (e.g., mass, friction, stiffness) with uncertainty that the model can reason about.
  • Requires no demos or fine-tuning and works even without a wrist F/T sensor (force calibrated from joint torques): zero-shot real-arm success rises from 3.3% to 90.0%.
  • Introduces HiddenPhys, a 7-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables, where it beats demo-trained force-aware policies and matches hand-written expert controllers; smaller models benefit most from physical-quantity feedback.
Show abstract
Frontier multimodal models can now control robots zero-shot from images, yet many manipulation tasks depend on contact forces that images cannot show. Bringing force to an API-only frozen model raises two challenges. First, contact evolves much faster than an API model can decide, so the system must close the loop on force beneath the model. Second, a frozen model cannot interpret raw force streams, so force must be returned in a form the model can reason about. Therefore, we present ForceHarness, a harness that lets frozen models use force without demonstrations or finetuning. After calibrating hand force from a wrist sensor or joint torques alone, ForceHarness addresses the first challenge with self-terminating force primitives that regulate force on their own and return at a contact event. To address the second challenge, a physics translation layer turns each force trace into physical quantities with uncertainty as the tool result. To evaluate ForceHarness against expert force controllers and learned policies under identical conditions, we also build HiddenPhys, a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables. On a real arm without a wrist force sensor, ForceHarness raises the zero-shot success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 3.3% to 90.0%. In simulation, it adds 17.5 percentage points on the public ForceBench suite and 34.3 percentage points on HiddenPhys, surpassing force-aware policies trained on demonstrations on both benchmarks and matching hand-written expert controllers on HiddenPhys. Ablations further show that smaller models make significantly better decisions when given physical quantities rather than raw force values.

ReRead: Wrong Predictions, Useful Representations - Correcting Frozen Robot Policies by Re-reading Their Own Representations

📅 2026-11-16cs.ROcs.AI👤 Qi Yi (h=7)
Xu Su, Shunlong Xiao, Qintao Yi, Mingkuan Zhao, Xinmiao Yu
Core Contributions
  • Core claim: wrong actions don't imply wrong representations — a frozen VLA often encodes what it needs but fails to decode it into the right action.
  • A lightweight, image-free corrector reads the backbone's current and two previous caches to predict an action residual, adding only 4.17% latency; external visual encoders add nothing, and swapping in foreign caches breaks it, confirming it relies on the backbone's own features.
  • On the 11 hardest RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, boosts LingBot-VLA, π0.5 and Fast-WAM by 23.5, 13.1 and 8.0 points; on a real Flexiv dual-arm robot lifts a phase-ambiguous task from 0% to 36%.
Show abstract
When a pretrained robot policy fails on challenging tasks, common remedies include retraining the backbone, adding an external visual encoder, or introducing a memory module. We argue that wrong predictions do not imply wrong representations: a policy can retain the required information yet fail to read it out as the correct action. To test this view, we propose ReRead: with the backbone frozen, a corrector re-reads the current and two previous caches to predict an action residual. The corrector sees no images, is trained solely on offline demonstrations from all 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, and adds only 4.17% latency. Ablations and interventions show that the correction relies on the backbone's own representations: none of four external visual encoders adds further gain, and swapping in past caches from other trajectories sharply increases action error. On the 11 hardest RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, ReRead improves the success rates of LingBot-VLA, $π_{0.5}$, and Fast-WAM by 23.45, 13.09, and 8.00 percentage points, respectively. In paired evaluation on all 50 tasks, it raises Fast-WAM's average success rate from 90.73% to 92.66%. On a Flexiv dual-arm robot, it raises success on a phase-ambiguous task from 0% to 36%.

Lift, See, Act: Hierarchical Robot Policy Pretraining with 3D Foundation Models

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 Yi-Yuan Ge (h=9)
Yiyuan Ge, Changxing Ding, Ziyu Hao, Zijie Zheng, Xiangmin Xu
Core Contributions
  • Observes that methods pretraining policies from human video keep only sparse 3D hand trajectories and throw away the rich dense features computed by 3D foundation models along the way.
  • Lift: aligns the policy's shallow layers with intermediate features from multiple 3D FMs; See: adds depth reconstruction and hand-region attention supervision for geometric and interaction awareness; Act: fine-tunes for control.
  • Reports significant gains over state-of-the-art video-pretraining approaches in diverse simulated and real tasks, suggesting dense 3D distillation is a better pretraining target than trajectories alone.
Show abstract
Robot policy pretraining based on human videos is crucial for improving the policy's generalization ability. One main challenge for this task is the lack of explicit action-relevant representations in such unlabeled data. Recent works tend to estimate 3D hand motion trajectories from videos using 3D foundation models (3D FMs). However, they keep solely the sparse trajectories as supervision, ignoring the rich, fine-grained information produced during the trajectory extraction process. To address this issue, we propose LSA, a hierarchical robot policy pretraining framework that includes three phases: Lift, See, and Act. Specifically, the Lift phase elevates 2D video observations into dense 3D representations by adaptively aligning features in the policy's shallow layers with the intermediate representations extracted from multiple 3D FMs. Building upon these lifted representations, the See phase equips the policy with explicit geometric and interactive awareness through dual guidance. It introduces depth-map reconstruction to help the model comprehend 3D spatial layouts and utilizes hand region cues to explicitly supervise the encoder's attention toward task-relevant interaction hotspots. Empowered by the dense 3D representation learning and precise spatial guidance, our policy achieves robust and accurate robotic manipulation in the Act phase. Extensive experiments on diverse simulation and real-world tasks demonstrate that LSA significantly outperforms current state-of-the-art approaches. The project page is available at: https://yiyuan00.github.io/Lift-See-Act.

Robotic Reward Models in the Foundation-Model Era

📅 2026-11-16cs.ROcs.CV👤 M. Shou (h=53)
Guoqiang Liang, Yiqi Lin, Shangchen Zhou, Wenzheng Zeng, Yanzhe Chen
Core Contributions
  • Organizes 114 robotic reward model methods by the question their output actually answers — Outcome, Absolute Progress, Relative, Temporal Value, Hybrid — clarifying that methods sharing a name are often not interchangeable.
  • Identifies an evaluation blind spot: agreement with demonstration ordering and downstream policy gains do not prove a reward tracks task fulfilment.
  • RRM-Bench-1K (1,117 real closed-loop videos with task-state and camera-evidence labels) reveals that dense credit tracks elapsed progression more than actual task state, and no evaluator flags when the decisive view is occluded.
  • Practical takeaway: current RRMs are good enough for ranking/filtering whole rollouts but risky as dense RL signals; RRM-Hub packages 14 evaluators for apples-to-apples testing.
Show abstract
Robotic reward models (RRMs) use foundation models to replace per-task reward engineering with reusable, task-conditioned evaluation. Yet under one name, their outputs answer different questions, from whether a task succeeded to which execution is better. This survey organizes 114 methods into five semantic families by the question their native outputs answer: Outcome, Absolute Progress, Relative, Temporal Value, and Hybrid. Within this taxonomy, we review how model design determines what an evaluator can judge, how raw experience becomes supervision, and how existing evaluation practice tests the result. The review exposes a gap: agreement with demonstration order and downstream policy gains do not show whether a reward tracks task fulfillment. We therefore introduce RRM-Bench-1K, 1,117 real-robot videos of closed-loop executions with task-state and camera-evidence labels, and four contrast suites that separate the credited condition from its usual cues. A unified study of 27 evaluators shows that the strongest final scores rank whole executions well enough for selection and filtering. Dense credit, however, follows the progression of an execution more closely than its task state, and no evaluator signals when the decisive view is missing. How much fidelity a reward model needs therefore depends on the decision it drives. We outline directions from real-failure supervision to metrics that separate task state from chronology, and open-source RRM-Hub, a toolbox in which 14 existing RRMs can be readily applied and new evaluators tested on the same capabilities.

WiTac: Wrist-Informed Tactile Representations for Contact-Rich Manipulation

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 E. Weinan (h=62)
Haotian He, Yiyang Chen, Congsheng Xu, Wenchao Yue, Mingze Ye
Core Contributions
  • Argues that identical tactile readings call for different corrections depending on hand/object/target configuration, so touch should be fused with wrist vision before entering the policy rather than supplied as a separate stream.
  • Pretrains this fused contact representation on ~300 hours of SharpaDex data, then plugs it into off-the-shelf VLAs (GR00T N1.7 and π0.5).
  • Raises success on four contact-rich tasks by +28.8 and +15.0 points over vision-only policies, and beats a wrist-free tactile encoder by 18.8 and 7.5 points — isolating the value of the visual grounding.
  • Offline probes show the fused features are more stable under tactile noise and more linearly predictive of action direction, offering an explanation rather than just a benchmark gain.
Show abstract
Similar tactile contacts can call for different corrections depending on the configuration of the hand, object and target. Rather than supplying touch and wrist vision separately, fusing them before policy injection better grounds tactile features in visual context, yielding more accurate contact representations. We introduce Wrist-Informed Tactile Representations (WiTac), a pretrained encoder that learns such representations before action generation. Pretrained on nearly 300 hours of SharpaDex data, WiTac deeply fuses tactile signals with hand motion and wrist views into a comprehensive contact representation. Conditioning downstream VLA policies (GR00T N1.7 and $π_{0.5}$) on WiTac substantially improves task success over vision-only base policies across four contact-rich manipulation tasks (+28.8% and +15.0% on average), and further outperforms a wrist-free tactile encoder by 18.8% and 7.5%, respectively. Offline analyses further show that incorporating wrist vision yields more accurate contact descriptors, stronger joint task-contact discrimination, greater feature stability under tactile noise, and clearer action-direction readout from similar contacts.Project page: https://wrist-informed-tactile-representations.github.io/.

ARMFUL: Learning Whole-Arm Grasp Configurations for Single-Arm Pickup of Large Objects

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 G. Sukhatme (h=98)
Xinyi Yu, Yunshuang Li, Daniel Seita, Gaurav S. Sukhatme
Core Contributions
  • Targets a largely ignored regime: picking up boxes, baskets and chairs too big for any gripper by wrapping the whole arm around them.
  • Introduces WAG, the first large-scale whole-arm grasp dataset (1.7M joint configurations, 793 objects), generated by sampling directly in joint space and stress-testing survivors with shaking and external forces — avoiding the near-impossible task of teleoperating wrap-around grasps.
  • A point-cloud-conditioned diffusion model proposes arm configurations that a motion planner reaches collision-free before a squeeze-and-lift primitive.
  • 85.4% lift success on training and 69.9% on held-out objects in sim; 70/100 successful lifts on a real Franka across 10 IKEA objects.
Show abstract
Many everyday objects, such as storage boxes, baskets, and chairs, are too large or heavy for a gripper to grasp, yet a single arm can still pick them up by wrapping around and supporting them. Such grasps are hard to learn: wrapping motions are difficult to teleoperate, and existing datasets contain little whole-arm pickup data. We introduce WAG (Whole-Arm Grasp Dataset), the first large-scale dataset for single-arm pickup of large objects, with 1.7~million joint configurations across 793 objects. We sample configurations directly in joint space, execute each through squeeze and lift primitives in simulation, and stress-test survivors under active shaking and external forces. To demonstrate the utility of WAG and accomplish the whole-arm pickup task, we develop ARMFUL, a whole-arm pickup pipeline that pairs a diffusion model trained on WAG, generating whole-arm grasps from the object's point cloud, with a motion planner that reaches them collision-free before the same squeeze-and-lift execution. In simulation, ARMFUL reaches 85.4\% lift success on training and 69.9\% on held-out objects, and it lifts the object in 70 of 100 trials on a physical Franka arm across 10 IKEA objects.

AsymEEF: Bimanual Manipulation with Asymmetric End Effectors

📅 2026-11-16cs.ROcs.AIcs.CVcs.LG👤 Xue-Yan Zou (h=9)
Xiaokang Sun, Ningyue Wang, Kunqi Xu, Guangqi Jiang, Jianglong Ye
Core Contributions
  • Generates executable demonstrations for heterogeneous bimanual hand pairs (two-, three- and five-fingered) from a small source dataset — no new teleoperation per hardware combination.
  • Kinematic teacher anchors provide geometric supervision while sparse real-world X-Calib anchors correct grasp-execution discrepancies; a shared encoder maps actuation into a morphology-agnostic latent.
  • Replay filtering keeps only task-executable trajectories, yielding a 16.6× mean expansion of valid demonstrations in simulation, and the same representation trains one unified bimanual policy across end-effector pairs.
Show abstract
Teleoperated demonstrations are costly to collect and rarely reusable across end-effectors with different kinematics and action spaces. This burden grows in bimanual manipulation, where each end-effector pair may require new data. We present AsymEEF, a framework for executable cross-end-effector demonstration generation and unified bimanual policy learning. Given a small source dataset, AsymEEF transfers demonstrations to heterogeneous combinations of two-, three-, and five-fingered end-effectors without additional teleoperation. Kinematic teacher anchors provide geometric supervision, while sparse real-world X-Calib anchors correct discrepancies in grasp execution. A shared encoder maps wrist poses and actuator states to a morphology-agnostic latent space, and a morphology-conditioned decoder reconstructs target commands. Generated trajectories undergo replay filtering for task-level executability. The representation also supports a unified bimanual visuomotor policy trained across multiple pairs. Across four simulated and six real-world tasks, AsymEEF improves replay success over adapted transfer baselines, achieves a mean replay-valid expansion gain of 16.6 times in simulation, and is evaluated through downstream policy training on real robots. Project website: https://asymeef.vercel.app/.

WinDEX: Metric 3D Hand Motion Reconstruction in the Wild for Dexterous Teleoperation and Policy Learning

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 Xin You (h=8)
Hongyu Ma, Hairong Qu, Shiqi Zhao, Yongsong Yang, Kai Shi
Core Contributions
  • Builds a low-cost pipeline for metric-scale 3D hand reconstruction from in-the-wild egocentric stereo, combining geometric–temporal optimization with visual-inertial SLAM for world-frame trajectories.
  • WinDEX-Net is fisheye-native and trained via a data–model flywheel, yielding a 1,000-hour annotated hand dataset from the inexpensive CyberCap rig.
  • Fast enough for deployment: 43.6 FPS offline and 171 FPS causal streaming; 37.3 mm world-frame joint error with VIO.
  • RL policies trained with WinDEX motion references reach 7.6× and 3.6× the success of those using WiLoR and HaWoR references, and it supports real-time arm-hand teleoperation.
Show abstract
Human manipulation data are widely used to train robots for human-like dexterity. However, low-cost, large-scale collection in the wild remains challenging. Existing methods remain limited in geometrically grounded metric-scale reconstruction, real-world generalization and inference speed. We present WinDEX, integrating (1) geometric-temporal optimization for metric 3D hand annotation from in-the-wild egocentric stereo videos, combined with visual-inertial SLAM for world-frame reconstruction; (2) WinDEX-Net, an end-to-end, fisheye-native network combining calibrated stereo, local detail and temporal context, with a data-model flywheel of model-assisted annotation and retraining; (3) distilled models for offline reconstruction and causal streaming, supporting reinforcement learning and teleoperation; and (4) low-cost CyberCap stereo-inertial capture, forming the WinDEX-Hand dataset with 1,000 hours of training annotations and held-out human-annotated in-the-wild 2D and independent optical motion-capture evaluation sets. Offline reconstruction runs at 43.61 FPS and causal streaming at 171 FPS. On held-out data, offline reconstruction achieves state-of-the-art accuracy: 14.5 pixels in-the-wild reprojection error and, with visual-inertial odometry, 37.29 mm world-frame joint error. We achieve 7.6 and 3.6 times the task success rates of reinforcement learning policies trained with WiLoR and HaWoR motion references, respectively, and demonstrate real-time arm-hand teleoperation. Project website: https://fruitrabbit.github.io/WinDEX/.

QTOP: Scaling Humanoid Motion Tracking with Quantile Critics

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 M. Peschl (h=22)
Markus Peschl, Dana Kianfar, Daniel Dijkman
Core Contributions
  • Hypothesizes that humanoid motion tracking is compute-bound because diverse motions interfere like tasks in multi-task RL.
  • QTOP uses distributional (quantile) critics conditioned on state–reference pairs to produce better advantage estimates, improving sample efficiency over both on- and off-policy baselines by up to 10%.
  • Establishes a scaling law for motion tracking and finds that, in moderate GPU-hour regimes, dataset size matters surprisingly little — a useful budgeting signal for labs training general trackers.
Show abstract
Learning human motion tracking policies through reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a core capability that underpins a majority of recent progress in humanoid whole-body control. However, achieving generalization to unseen motions at high fidelity requires jointly scaling training to thousands of GPU and dataset hours. This work hypothesizes that motion tracking is inherently compute-bound because training on a diverse set of motions introduces learning interference, similar to multi-task RL. To tackle this, we propose Quantile Tracking Optimization (QTOP), which improves sample efficiency by introducing a distributional advantage estimate conditioned on state and reference pairs. QTOP outperforms on and off-policy RL baselines both in simulation and real-world tracking scenarios on multiple datasets by up to 10\%. Furthermore, we establish a scaling law for QTOP and find that, aside from scaling predictably, dataset size has minimal impact on performance for moderately sized GPU hour regimes.

Driving the Style: Velocity-Conditioned Whole-Body Control from Motion Demonstrations

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 Nu-Tan Chen (h=12)
Murad Dawood, Oezge Drama Berkenkamp, Antonio Gonzales Marin, Nutan Chen
Core Contributions
  • Gets the best of both worlds between adversarial motion priors (stylish but hard to deploy) and whole-body tracking (deployable but needs a runtime reference): train with tracking rewards but give the actor only velocity commands and proprioception.
  • Reference motion supervises only the reward and privileged critic, so style gets baked into the policy without teacher–student distillation.
  • Trained on a single random-walk clip, it achieves ≥98% sim success on held-out velocity profiles and completes every trial on a Unitree G1, where whole-body tracking drops to 2% and distillation to 56% on slow walking.
  • Keeps joint error within 8% of full tracking while achieving higher style coverage than AMP, which fell on hardware.
Show abstract
Achieving human-like behavior on humanoid robots has advanced rapidly by leveraging motion-capture demonstrations rather than hand-crafted cost functions. Adversarial imitation supplies a style signal that lets a policy fulfill a task while adhering to a desired style, but is difficult to transfer to hardware. Whole-body tracking through reinforcement learning instead deploys reliably and imitates demonstrations faithfully, but the resulting policies are conditioned on the future reference, so they require a reference trajectory at runtime and can fail when the motion differs from the training data. We train a humanoid policy directly with whole-body tracking rewards while restricting its actor to compact locomotion commands and robot observations. Reference motion supervises the reward and privileged critic, without supplying reference joint trajectories to the actor or requiring teacher distillation. The demonstrated style is thereby encoded in the policy through the tracking reward, so the robot reproduces the whole-body style under commanded velocities. Trained on a single random-walk motion and commanded with velocity profiles from held-out motions, our policy reaches at least 98\% success in simulation and completes every trial on the Unitree G1. On held-out slow walking, whole-body tracking drops to 2\% success and command-conditioned distillation to 56\%, and both fail on the real robot. On the trained motions, the joint position error of our policy stays within 8\% of whole-body tracking, and it has a higher style coverage than adversarial motion priors (AMP), which fell on the real robot without a safety crane.

SG-ODOM: Event-Driven Proprioceptive State Estimation with Stability-Gated Contact Updates for Legged Robots

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 F. Dellaert (h=72)
Francesco Iotti, Chiyun Noh, Franco Angelini, Manolo Garabini, Ayoung Kim
Core Contributions
  • Challenges the standard assumption that a foot becomes a stationary reference at touchdown: impact and load transfer make early-stance kinematic corrections unreliable.
  • A stability gate delays accepting a contact until the foot is genuinely stable, firing one event-driven kinematic correction per stance rather than many redundant ones; timeouts bound pure-IMU propagation.
  • Works on both quadrupeds and humanoids and reduces Relative Pose Error by up to 25% on outdoor GrandTour sequences versus the baseline estimator.
  • Open-source and drop-in, making it a cheap upgrade for any proprioceptive legged estimator.
Show abstract
Reliable proprioceptive state estimation for legged robots fuses inertial sensing with kinematic sensing from leg encoders. The latter assumes a foot is stationary during the stance phase; however, physical foot touchdown does not necessarily provide a stationary reference for odometry, particularly during impact and load transfer. Repeatedly applying kinematic corrections throughout the same contact phase can introduce redundant or unreliable information. We present SG-ODOM, a proprioceptive odometry framework that uses stability-gated contact selection to schedule kinematic updates. Following physical touchdown, a stability gate accepts the contact for state estimation only after the foot reaches a sufficiently stable support condition. The resulting validated touchdown event triggers an estimator correction, while additional timeout updates bound the maximum duration of uninterrupted IMU propagation. The framework is evaluated on multiple legged platforms, both quadrupedal and humanoid robots, against representative state-of-the-art proprioceptive estimators. Across the evaluated sequences, SG-ODOM demonstrates competitive or superior estimation accuracy, reducing Relative Pose Error (RPE) by up to 25% compared to the baseline estimator on outdoor sequences from the GrandTour dataset. Source code: https://github.com/ori-drs/SG-ODOM. Video: https://youtu.be/-583_RyuhqQ

ROSE: A Shared Cross-Embodiment Facial-Motion Latent Space for Humanoid Robot Expression

📅 2026-11-16cs.RO👤 M. Hansen (h=19)
Yongji Fu, Man Cao, Yi Zhou, Fei Lin, Mark F. Hansen
Core Contributions
  • Packs 34 actuators into a 19.5 cm biomimetic head via tendon-sheath transmission, decoupling actuator placement from facial action points to enable full-face expressions.
  • Avoids the common render-and-invert pipeline (generate avatar video → retarget) that adds latency and artifacts: ROSE-VAE learns an identity-independent facial-motion latent shared across human, digital-human and robot data.
  • ROSE-Flow maps text and/or audio directly into this motion space, and ROSE-ACT converts trajectories into constrained actuator commands — an end-to-end executable representation for expressive HRI.
Show abstract
Natural, coherent full-face dynamic expressions are central to making humanoid robots socially approachable, yet existing systems commonly realize only limited lip motion or scripted expressions. The limitation is both physical and algorithmic. A compact head leaves little room for densely packed, independently controllable actuation; meanwhile, learned systems often generate an avatar video, landmarks, or facial parameters and then infer actuator commands through visual retargeting. This render-and-invert path adds latency and artifacts, and it does not reliably transfer generated behavior to a physical robot. We present ROSE (RObot facial expression through Shared motion Embeddings), a framework that integrates biomimetic hardware, cross-embodiment motion representation learning, and multimodal motion generation. On the hardware side, we build a flexible tendon-sheath transmission structure that integrates 34 actuators into a 19.5 cm biomimetic head. By decoupling actuator placement from facial action points, it enables coordinated full-face expression within a compact volume. On the algorithmic side, ROSE-VAE learns an identity-independent shared facial-motion latent space from human, digital-human, and robot observations. ROSE-Flow directly maps text, audio, or their combination to trajectories in this motion space without using video as a generator input. ROSE-ACT then converts these trajectories into constrained actuator commands. Our system demonstrates how high-density biomimetic hardware and a shared executable facial-motion representation can jointly unify cross-embodiment learning, multimodal generation, and physical robot expression.

Déjà-Events: Multi-view 3D Geometry with Event Cameras via Sparsity-Aware Adaptive Gating

📅 2026-11-16cs.CVcs.RO👤 M. Pollefeys (h=126)
Arda Cinar Demirtas, Pablo Varela Bernal, Marc Pollefeys, Sunghwan Hong
Core Contributions

Data-driven predictive control (DPC) predicts future outputs by combining recorded input-output trajectory data such that the combined trajectory approximately matches a measured initial trajectory and tracks a provided reference. This paper investigates to what extent and under which conditions DPC using data pooled across operating conditions retains the prediction accuracy of DPC using data from the current operating condition. In autonomous vehicle racing, the tire-road friction varies between racetrack sections and tire sets. If the initial trajectory and the recorded data originate from different frictions, prediction accuracy of DPC degrades. Ensuring the frictions match requires knowing the friction during recording and estimating the current friction explicitly. Instead, we pool data recorded at different frictions. We compare the prediction accuracy of DPC using data recorded under the current friction (matched), a different friction (mismatched) and data pooled from both frictions. We evaluate in simulation and small-scale vehicle experiments in the Cyber-Physical Mobility Lab (CPM Lab). Our results show that pooled data keep the multi-step root-mean-square prediction error within 1.5 times that of matched data, whereas mismatched data increase it by a factor of 2.2 to 3.4. To assess how this prediction accuracy carries over to control, we evaluate the tracking error from the raceline in closed loop. In simulation, DPC with pooled data tracks comparably to nonlinear model predictive control (13.7 cm versus 13.3 cm) and close to DPC with matched data in the CPM Lab, differing by 0.1 cm on the low-friction tires (1.6 cm versus 1.5 cm) and by 0.2 cm on the high-friction tires (0.9 cm versus 1.1 cm). DPC thus provides partial adaptation to varying friction, without a dedicated adaptation method.

Inverse kinematics are critical in many robotic applications, yet standard MoveIt-based solvers struggle in confined scenarios. This paper provides a benchmark of the available solvers and compares multiple practical modifications for improving the performance of the solvers. Splitting the IK problem into two parts results in the best performance, leading to a 100% success rate under 100 ms in an endoscopic tank scenario.
