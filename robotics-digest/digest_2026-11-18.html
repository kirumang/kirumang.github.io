🔭 Research Landscape

The dominant story today is the rapid convergence of VLA policies and world models into a single network that predicts the future in a latent space rather than in pixels. Three independent groups land on the same design choice. LaST1.0 (#1) predicts compact, geometry-aware latents derived from future wrist views instead of full videos; Z-sight (#3) forecasts the future directly in the visual-token space its own VLM perceives, reaching 81.9% on the SIMPLE benchmark and +20 points on a Unitree G1 at 110 ms per chunk; and JEPA Dots (#24) gets 84.9% on 40 LIBERO tasks with only 39M parameters by separating instruction-free dynamics latents from language-conditioned task latents. Doppel (#21) attacks the same problem from the other side: instead of asking a video model to infer body motion from numeric actions, it renders the robot's intended configuration and lets the model spend capacity on how the world responds, which is what makes training across eight robot and human embodiments possible. The shared conviction is that appearance reconstruction is wasted capacity for control.

A second, more skeptical thread asks whether these learned models actually mean what we assume they mean. Balestriero and LeCun's counterfactual JEPA paper (#25) proves that a world model trained only on factual transitions cannot answer 'what if I had acted differently' on its own, then shows that a smoothness argument recovers counterfactuals with no extra data, lifting counterfactual R² from roughly 40% to 86–96%. The driving probe (#27) finds that action-conditioned forecasters barely use the action (at most +2.5 mIoU from the right action, but up to −23.2 from a wrong one), and #11 shows that attention weights in a Transformer planner are correlational, not causal, indicators of which objects matter. #30 gives a provable lower bound on how conservative a latent safety filter must be given its latent, and TARS (#5) finds that LLM-to-action-model pipelines in four commercial robots execute physical actions users never asked for. Read together, these papers say that the latents everyone is now building on need explicit tests of causality, action dependence and safety before they can be trusted.

The rest of the batch shows learning moving closer to deployment. Several papers reuse experience the robot already has: SeRA (#14) relabels autonomous rollouts with later policy checkpoints and doubles tomato-picking success to 80%, the driving work (#26) turns late or unnecessary human takeovers into earlier, proactive targets, and RecallFlow (#19) uses episodic memory as the starting distribution of a flow policy rather than as an extra condition. Robustness to the physical setup is another theme, with WACO (#4) using the robot's own arm as a calibration target to recover from a bumped camera (+36.7 points on a real UR5e) and ROMUX (#22) navigating from wall cameras with no calibration at all. In hardware, two papers push quasi-direct-drive actuation in different directions, as compact tendon-driven muscle modules (#10) and as a stiffer load-sharing compound planetary gearbox (#29).

LaST1.0: A Unified Latent World-Action Model with Control-Centric SpatioTemporal Reasoning

2026-11-18 cs.RO 👤 Pheng-Ann Heng (h=121)
Hao Chen, Xuheng Zhang, Zhuangzhe Wu, Jiaming Liu, Shaobo Zhang
Core Contributions
  • Argues that reconstruction-style world models spend most of their capacity on appearance that is irrelevant to control; LaST1.0 predicts geometry-aware latents taken from future wrist views, so supervision concentrates on the changing robot-object contact region.
  • Drops the pretrained vision encoder entirely in favor of a light patch embedder, which keeps fine-grained visual detail in front of the backbone instead of compressing it through a CLIP/SigLIP-style bottleneck.
  • Introduces LaST-Attention, a structured attention pattern for space, time and modality, to make joint latent prediction and action generation work inside one unified model.
  • Evaluated on two real embodiments with five tasks and on RLBench, LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, reporting state-of-the-art success and generalization to novel scenes, which supports the claim that control-centric targets beat full video futures.
Show abstract
World-Action Models couple action learning with future prediction, enabling robotic policies to capture environmental dynamics for robust control. However, prevailing methods take a reconstruction-oriented approach to model future videos, allocating substantial capacity to appearance details that are incidental to control. In this work, we reformulate world modeling for manipulation as control-centric future prediction in latent space, and present LaST1.0, a unified latent World-Action Model. Rather than modeling an observation-complete video future, LaST1.0 predicts compact, geometry-aware latent targets derived from future wrist views, focusing the supervision on the evolving robot-object interactions required for fine-grained manipulation. To better support this control-centric paradigm, LaST1.0 revisits the visual input interface and attention mechanism of its unified architecture. It adopts a vision-encoder-free design with a lightweight patch embedder, exposing fine-grained visual cues directly to the backbone, and introduces LaST-Attention for structured spatiotemporal interactions across modalities. Evaluations across two robot embodiments, five challenging manipulation tasks, and three simulation benchmarks---RLBench, LIBERO, and LIBERO-Plus---demonstrate that LaST1.0 achieves state-of-the-art success rates and generalizes to novel scenarios. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of control-centric latent world modeling for robust manipulation.
#3 h=98

$Z$-sight: Unifying Vision-Language-Action and Latent World Modeling

2026-11-18 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 G. Sukhatme (h=98)
Junjie Ye, Yiqi Zhao, Muchen Xu, Celina Shiyu Wang, Hao Dong
Core Contributions
  • Unlike latent policies that align to a frozen external encoder, Z-sight predicts future visual tokens in the same space its VLM uses to perceive the present, so foresight and understanding share one representation.
  • A mixture-of-transformers couples a foresight expert and a continuous action expert with the VLM, jointly denoising future tokens and action chunks with no pixel decoder.
  • Trained on both human video and robot trajectories, it reaches 81.9% on the SIMPLE benchmark (+9.6 points over the best baseline) and 73.3% on six Unitree G1 humanoid tasks (+20.0 points).
  • Runs at 110 ms per chunk, showing that world-model benefits can be obtained without the cost of video generation.
Show abstract
We introduce $Z$-sight, a unified robot foundation model that couples vision-language-action (VLA) and generative latent world modeling. Video-based world action models (WAMs) show the value of joint future-and-action generation for control, yet inherit the heavy cost of video generators. Recent latent-space policies avoid pixel generation, yet typically align intermediate features or rely on frozen target encoders, decoupling future prediction from the VLM's native feature space. $Z$-sight instead predicts the future directly in the visual token space in which its VLM perceives the present. Coupling a foresight expert and a continuous action expert with the VLM in a mixture-of-transformers, $Z$-sight jointly denoises future spatial tokens and actions at inference without pixel reconstruction or external encoders. Trained on human video and robot trajectories, $Z$-sight achieves 81.9% success on SIMPLE (+9.6 points over the strongest baseline) and 73.3% across six Unitree G1 tasks (+20.0 points) at 110 ms per chunk, while remaining competitive on tabletop benchmarks.

JEPA Dots: Dual-Latent World Model for Language-Guided Robot Control

2026-11-18 cs.RO 👤 Jiankai Sun (h=25)
Xianhao Pan, Chi Li, Hanchao Li, Yunxin Liu, Jiankai Sun
Core Contributions
  • Addresses a conditioning flaw in language-guided world models: the instruction should change which action is chosen, not the predicted outcome of a fixed action.
  • Keeps separate dynamics and task latents; masked attention limits world prediction to instruction-free history and actions, while task latents drive a flow-matching policy.
  • Gets 84.9% on 40 LIBERO tasks with about 39M parameters (vs 78.7% reported for 161M NanoVLA-S under a different protocol) and raises LIBERO-Goal from 67.1% to 88.5% over its own task-ID baseline.
  • Instruction-switching tests confirm the intended invariance, a property most VLA world models do not check.
Show abstract
Predictive world models for language-guided robot control face a fundamental conditioning challenge: instructions should influence action selection without altering the predicted consequences of fixed actions under identical observations. To address this challenge, we introduce JEPA Dots (Dynamics-Oriented Task Separation), a compact dual-latent framework that jointly learns visual dynamics and language-guided control through separate dynamics and task representations. Language-conditioned task features guide a flow-matching policy, while masked attention restricts world prediction to instruction-independent dynamics history and supplied actions. In closed-loop evaluation on 40 trained LIBERO tasks, JEPA Dots achieves 84.9% overall success with approximately 39.1M parameters, compared with the published 78.7% of NanoVLA-S with 161.0M parameters under a different evaluation protocol. In the reference experiment, JEPA Dots also improves overall success from 76.0% for our task-ID-conditioned adaptation baseline to 84.9%, with LIBERO-Goal success increasing from 67.1% to 88.5%. Instruction-switching experiments further confirm that task representations and policy outputs respond to language changes, while dynamics representations and world predictions remain invariant under fixed observations and supplied actions.

Render the Robot, Generate the World: One Video World Model Across Embodiments

2026-11-18 cs.RO 👤 Homanga Bharadhwaj (h=26)
Yash Jangir, Karan Mirakhor, Renos Zabounidis, Muhammad Zubair Irshad, Sergey Zakharov
Core Contributions
  • Conditions the video world model on camera-aligned renderings of the robot's future body configuration, so it does not have to infer body motion from raw action numbers and can focus on the environment's response.
  • Rendered motion gives a shared visual interface across action spaces, enabling one backbone trained on about 600 hours from eight robot and human embodiments with no embodiment labels.
  • Recovers camera poses for datasets without extrinsics so renderings can be aligned with recorded video.
  • Improves PSNR by 1.00 dB on held-out and 1.20 dB on out-of-distribution trajectories over action-only baselines; training on all eight embodiments helps robot predictions on four of six sets.
Show abstract
Predicting the consequences of an agent's actions requires modeling both the body's motion and the environment's response. Most video world models attempt to learn both from numerical actions, even though geometry and kinematics already specify the intended motion. We introduce Doppel, an embodiment-agnostic video world model conditioned on camera-aligned renderings of future body configurations and numerical action trajectories. By explicitly providing the intended motion, Doppel can focus on predicting object motion and interaction-driven changes in the environment. Rendered motion also provides a common visual interface across different embodiments and action spaces. To train on robot datasets without camera extrinsics, we recover camera poses and align rendered trajectories with recorded observations. We jointly train a single shared video backbone and conditioning adapter on eight datasets spanning eight robot and human embodiments, from single- and bimanual robot arms to a dexterous humanoid and human hands and bodies, using about 600 hours of video without embodiment labels. Across three robot-arm datasets, Doppel improves PSNR over action-only baselines by 1.00 dB on held-out trajectories and 1.20 dB under out-of-distribution evaluation, while predicting more consistent object motion. Training on all eight embodiments (Doppel-8) improves robot predictions over the three-robot model (Doppel-3) on four of six evaluation sets. Project page: https://doppelwm.github.io/.

Towards Pose-Robust Few-Shot Vision-Language-Action Models

2026-11-18 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CV 👤 Li-Qiang Nie (h=87)
Wenhao Li, Zhongyi Han, Yuhao Wei, Liang Han, Yilong Yin
Core Contributions
  • Reframes two common deployment failures, a bumped camera tripod and a re-docked mobile base, as the same problem: a change in the camera-to-base extrinsic that the few-shot policy has memorized.
  • Uses the robot arm, posed by forward kinematics, as a metric calibration target and the static scene as a world anchor, which lets one RGB-D frame distinguish a moved camera from a moved robot without ground-truth extrinsics.
  • Canonicalizes image, state, action and start pose back to the calibration frame and fine-tunes the unmodified VLA on its own estimator's artifacts, so it works with existing policies.
  • Raises shifted success by 13.2 points in simulation and 36.7 points on a real UR5e, recovering about 90% (sim) and 87% (real) of the gap to an oracle; also releases LIBERO-PoseShift as a benchmark.
Show abstract
Fine-tuning a vision-language-action (VLA) model on a few dozen demonstrations is the standard way to put a manipulation policy on a robot, yet the resulting policy silently assumes that the camera and the robot base stay exactly where they were during data collection: a bumped tripod or a base re-docked a few centimetres away makes it reach for the wrong place. We show that both failures are one phenomenon, a change of the camera-to-base extrinsic that the policy has memorised, and that the robot carries everything needed to undo it. We introduce WACO, a world-anchored canonicalization framework for VLA models: the arm, placed by forward kinematics at the measured joint angles, serves as a metric calibration target, and the static scene serves as the world anchor that tells a moved camera from a moved robot. From a single RGB-D frame, WACO estimates both shifts without ground-truth extrinsics or extra data, canonicalises the image to the calibration viewpoint and the state, action and start pose to the calibration base frame, and fine-tunes the unmodified policy under the artefacts of its own estimator. We also introduce LIBERO-PoseShift, the first benchmark for RGB VLAs with graded camera, base and compound shifts. Across LIBERO-PoseShift, the LIBERO-Plus camera track and four real-world tasks on a UR5e robot, WACO consistently outperforms the standard few-shot recipe and prior viewpoint-robust methods, raising shifted success by 13.2 points in simulation and by 36.7 points on the real robot, recovering 90% of the simulation gap to a ground-truth oracle and 87% of the real-robot gap, and establishing a new state of the art among methods that use no perturbed training data, with no test-time calibration. Project page: https://peacelwh.github.io/WACO/.

Lost Between Mind, Model Collaboration, and Motion: Understanding and Measuring Cross-Model Action Misalignment in Commercial Robots

2026-11-18 cs.RO 👤 Xiu-Zhen Cheng (h=71)
Yuhang Huang, Junchao Li, Boyang Ma, Minghui Xu, Kaidi Xu
Core Contributions
  • Identifies a new safety boundary in commercial robots: an LLM interprets intent and a smaller action model translates it, and meaning can drift between the two so the robot moves when the user never issued a motion command.
  • TARS is a black-box testing framework that explores the command-reasoning-action pipeline and automatically verifies unintended physical behaviors, without access to model internals.
  • Runs 133,400 executions across four commercial robots and 44 settings, finding misalignment is widespread and reaching up to 94% verified action coverage.
  • Matters because it treats multi-model robot stacks as an attack surface, which most VLA safety work, focused on a single model, overlooks.
Show abstract
Embodied robots increasingly rely on large language models (LLMs) to interpret user intent and coordinate with robot-specific action models for physical interaction. This multi-model pipeline creates a critical semantic boundary across mind, model collaboration, and motion: semantic consistency may break between what the large model understands, how the small action model translates that understanding into executable commands, and what physical actions the robot ultimately performs. As a security consequence, commercial robots may execute unintended physical actions even when user inputs contain no explicit action commands. To measure this problem, we develop TARS, a black-box framework that explores the command-reasoning-action pipeline and automatically discovers and validates unintended physical behaviors. We evaluate TARS on four commercial robots across 44 experimental settings, conducting 133,400 executions. TARS uncovers widespread cross-model action misalignment and achieves up to 94% verified action coverage.

Attention Is Not Relevance: Challenging the Causality of Attention in Autonomous Driving

2026-11-18 cs.AIcs.RO 👤 J. Gozálvez (h=41)
Luca Lusvarghi, Javier Gozalvez
Core Contributions
  • Brings the NLP 'attention is not explanation' debate into autonomous driving by comparing attention-based relevance against a counterfactual ground truth: remove each object and see whether the plan changes.
  • Uses more than 80,000 synthetic scenes with the Transformer planner PlanT 2.0, providing a large-scale causal reference rather than visual inspection of heatmaps.
  • Finds attention misses relevant objects and flags irrelevant ones; it is reasonable on single-lane roads but degrades at multi-lane roads and intersections where explanations matter most.
  • Matters for safety cases and regulators that treat attention maps as interpretability evidence.
Show abstract
Attention has become one of the most widely adopted solutions for estimating the relevance of surrounding objects and interpreting an autonomous vehicle's driving decisions. However, its ability to capture the causal impact of individual input elements on an AI model's output has long been discussed in the literature, particularly in the Natural Language Processing (NLP) domain. This work brings this discussion into the autonomous driving domain by analyzing the accuracy of attention in estimating the impact of an input object on an autonomous vehicle's driving, i.e., its relevance. To this end, we compare different attention-based relevance estimation methods against a causal reference of object relevance obtained through counterfactual analysis. We perform our analysis on more than 80,000 synthetic driving scenes using an autonomous vehicle based on PlanT 2.0, a state-of-the-art Transformer-based model for object-level planning. Results show that attention provides a correlational rather than causal indication of object relevance. Attention-based relevance estimation methods do not consistently identify all relevant objects and tend to incorrectly classify as relevant objects with little or no impact on the vehicle's driving. In particular, results show that attention can effectively identify relevant objects in simple single-lane, straight-road scenarios, but its accuracy degrades in more complex settings, such as multi-lane roads and intersections. These findings highlight the limitations of attention as a proxy for object relevance, calling for novel methods able to accurately capture the causal impact of individual objects on the vehicle's driving and distinguish it from correlational associations.

Counterfactual World Models: JEPAs That Reason About "What If"

2026-11-18 cs.LGcs.AIcs.CVcs.ROstat.ML 👤 Randall Balestriero (h=24)
Randall Balestriero, Yann LeCun, Kerda Varaku
Core Contributions
  • Proves that a world model trained only on factual transitions cannot answer 'what would have happened under another action, with the same hidden disturbance', regardless of data or capacity.
  • Shows that for physical systems where actions and disturbances act as separate forces, the counterfactual is approximately the observed outcome plus the model's prediction difference between the two actions, with no new data, parameters or retraining.
  • Gives an O(h²) error bound with a matching minimax lower bound and explains failure cases such as discontinuities and multiplicative disturbances.
  • With JEPA models on frozen encoders, counterfactual R² rises from 38–46% to 86–96%; in hindsight planning it finds the right action in 35–69% of events vs 14–23% for an oracle planner.
Show abstract
World models operate in a world they cannot fully observe. When a hidden disturbance, such as a current pushing a boat, changes the outcome of an action, a counterfactual question follows: what would have happened under a different action, subject to the same disturbance? Its answer separates bad decisions from bad luck. We prove that world models trained on factual transitions cannot answer it on their own, whatever their data or capacity. For many physical systems, the observed outcome and a simple property of the dynamics suffice: natura non facit saltus, nature makes no leaps. When actions and disturbances act as separate forces, their interaction is second-order in time, so the counterfactual outcome is, to first order, the observed outcome plus the difference between the world model's predictions under the alternative and factual actions. This yields a counterfactual world model with no counterfactual data, extra parameters, or retraining. For transitions of duration $h$, we prove an $O(h^2)$ bound on the counterfactual error relative to the disturbance variance, establish a matching minimax lower bound from unpaired factual data, and characterize failures at discontinuities, coordinate folds, and disturbances that scale the command. We validate these predictions from pixels with JEPA world models on frozen DINOv2 and masked autoencoder (MAE) encoders. On four rendered simulators, our approach improves counterfactual $R^2$ from $38$--$46\%$ to $86$--$96\%$ with the same world models; on a fifth, an underwater vehicle, it reaches $90$--$94\%$ with sixteen times more factual data. Four further frozen encoders, DINOv3, SigLIP~2, CLIP and EVA-02, give $81$--$95\%$ on the same simulators. In hindsight planning, it finds the action that would have reached the goal in $35$--$69\%$ of events, against $14$--$23\%$ for an oracle planner that knows the simulator but not the outcome.

Does the Action Matter? Probing Action-Conditioned Semantic Forecasting as a World Model for Autonomous Driving

2026-11-18 cs.CVcs.AIcs.LGcs.RO 👤 N. Lobo (h=23)
Binay Kumar Singh, Niels Da Vitoria Lobo
Core Contributions
  • Questions an untested assumption of driving world models: that their forecasts actually depend on the action they are conditioned on.
  • Measures action dependence by intervening on the action at test time and comparing against an identical model with no action input, validated in a synthetic world with known action effects.
  • The correct action helps mid-term mIoU by at most 2.5 (Cityscapes) and 0.6 (BDD100K) points, while a wrong action hurts by up to 23.2 and 7.0, falling below the no-action model.
  • Feature-wise conditioning relies on actions 1.6x more than input-level conditioning on real data, showing accuracy gains alone do not reveal how strongly a model uses its action.
Show abstract
World models are increasingly used in autonomous driving to predict how a scene evolves under the ego vehicle's actions, supporting counterfactual reasoning and model-based planning. However, existing evaluations focus on prediction fidelity and planning performance, and rarely verify that the predictions depend on the conditioning action. In this paper, we study action dependence in action-conditioned semantic forecasting, which predicts future semantic segmentation from past segmentations and the ego action. We compare a forecaster with feature-wise and input-level action conditioning against an identical model without action input, and measure action dependence with test-time action interventions scored by standard segmentation metrics. The evaluation is validated in a synthetic environment with known action-dependent dynamics and applied to Cityscapes and BDD100K, both within each dataset and across the two vehicle platforms. Experiments show that the executed action improves mid-term mIoU by at most 2.5 points on Cityscapes and 0.6 points on BDD100K, whereas a wrong action reduces it by up to 23.2 and 7.0 points, below the model without action input. On real data, feature-wise conditioning depends 1.6 times more on the action than input-level conditioning at similar accuracy, whereas both depend on it equally in the synthetic environment, where the history does not determine the action. These results show that an accuracy gain indicates that a driving world model uses its action, but not how strongly its forecasts depend on it. Code is available at https://github.com/binayksingh/does-action-matter.

Measuring the Conservatism of Latent Safety Filters for World Models

2026-11-18 cs.ROcs.LGeess.SY 👤 Reza Akhavian (h=21)
Johannes Mootz, Mani Amani, Reza Akhavian
Core Contributions
  • Shows that conformal calibration can bound a latent safety filter's false acceptance of unsafe actions, but says nothing about how many safe actions it must refuse.
  • Defines 'overlap', the share of safe transitions in latent-action cells that also contain unsafe ones, and proves it is the minimum refusal rate of any cell-wise filter that rejects all unsafe data.
  • Applies to any latent, enabling comparison of three designs; in visual navigation a separate safety encoder has the lowest overlap, refuses the fewest safe actions and lets the planner reach the most goals.
Show abstract
A latent safety filter decides from the latent of a world model whether a planned action is safe. Conformal calibration sets the threshold of the filter so that it accepts an unsafe action only with a small probability. However, the filter can still be conservative and refuse many safe actions, depending on the latent. To measure the conservatism that a latent forces on the filter, we group similar latents and actions into cells. A filter that gives one decision per cell can refuse the unsafe transitions of a cell only by refusing the safe ones in it as well. We call the share of safe transitions that lie in such cells the overlap. We prove that the overlap is the minimum share of safe transitions that such a filter has to refuse in order to accept no unsafe transition in the data. Both the overlap and the conformal calibration can be applied to any latent. We use this to compare three existing designs, in which the safety labels train a classifier on top of the world model, the world model itself, or a separate encoder beside it. In a visual navigation task, the separate encoder has the lowest overlap, and at the same conformal level its filter refuses the fewest safe actions and lets the planner reach the most goals of the three.

Grana: Compositional Reward Modeling for Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation

2026-11-18 cs.RO 👤 R. Poovendran (h=62)
Zi-ang Cao, Kaiyuan Zheng, Yinzhen Xu, Haonan Duan, Jiasheng Gu
Core Contributions
  • Builds a reward model that tracks progress in long tasks by naming the current unfinished subtask in open vocabulary and estimating its continuous progress, instead of relying on fixed subtask classifiers.
  • Composes whole-task progress as credit for completed subtasks plus the progress of the active one, so knowledge learned on short tasks transfers to multi-minute tasks.
  • Introduces an annotation protocol that turns sparse subtask boundaries and action-quality labels into dense progress targets, which avoids the known flaw of elapsed-time proxies when failures reverse progress.
  • Pretrained on 200 mostly short tasks, it predicts rewards zero-shot on unseen tasks, adapts to five long-horizon tasks and improves downstream policies.
Show abstract
Robot reward modeling faces two challenges: generalizing across tasks and accurately tracking progress in long-horizon manipulation. General-purpose reward models demonstrate broad task transfer, but transfer from short-task pretraining to long-horizon tasks remains underexplored. Long-horizon progress models often rely on task-specific subtask classifiers or fixed action vocabularies, limiting the reuse of learned progress knowledge when adapting to new tasks. To address these challenges, we introduce Grana, a vision-language robot reward model that learns to identify unfinished subtasks and estimate their continuous progress using open-vocabulary subtask descriptions. We train a language-conditioned model to estimate progress in short-horizon tasks and individual subtasks of long-horizon tasks. For long-horizon tasks, we combine the selected subtask's predicted progress with credit for preceding completed subtasks to estimate whole-task progress. General progress prediction requires training the model across diverse tasks with progress supervision that accounts for imperfect execution. Elapsed-time proxies can misrepresent progress when relative subtask durations vary, or failures reverse local progress. We therefore introduce an annotation protocol that converts sparse subtask boundaries and action-quality annotations into dense progress targets, without requiring annotators to assign numerical progress scores. Pretraining with this supervision on 200 diverse, mostly short-horizon manipulation tasks enables zero-shot reward prediction on unseen tasks and improves progress prediction after adaptation to multi-minute, long-horizon tasks. Experiments demonstrate (i) generalizable reward prediction, including adaptation to five long-horizon tasks, (ii) benefits from pretraining and composing subtask progress estimates, and (iii) improved downstream policy performance.

Quasi-Direct Drive (QDD) actuators are widely used in legged robots due to their low reflected inertia, high backdrivability, and high torque density, commonly employing planetary or cycloidal gearboxes. Single-stage planetary gearboxes offer high efficiency but limited torque density; multi-stage designs improve torque density at the cost of efficiency; and cycloidal gearboxes provide high stiffness but suffer from torque ripple. Compound Planetary Gearboxes (CPG) offer a compromise, but conventional designs result in increased actuator width, mass, and reduced torsional stiffness in 3D-printed actuators. This paper proposes the BiPath Compound Planetary Gearbox (BiPathCPG), a novel architecture that introduces an idle second-stage sun gear and load-sharing second-stage planets within the otherwise unused space of a conventional CPG to simultaneously improve stiffness, reduce actuator width, and reduce mass. An optimization framework computes BiPathCPG parameters for a given motor and target gear ratio. The architecture is validated through a 3D-printed proof-of-concept actuator design, benchmarked against a conventional CPG using custom-built test rigs. The BiPathCPG achieves a torsional stiffness of 1394 Nm/rad, a 1.70x improvement over the conventional CPG, with a 32% reduction in backlash, 25 g lower mass, and approximately 6.4 mm reduction in axial width, while achieving a peak stall torque of 36.49 Nm.
