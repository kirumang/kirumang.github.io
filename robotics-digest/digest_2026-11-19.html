Research Landscape

Today's 30 papers are dominated by a single question asked from many angles: what is actually missing between a capable VLA or frontier agent and reliable physical execution? Three diagnostic papers answer it bluntly. PhyDEX (#5) finds that GR00T N1.7, DynamicVLA and ForceVLA all succeed on fewer than 3% of throws, and that simply giving them ground-truth mass and friction roughly doubles success, which points to "physical blindness" rather than poor motor skill. Mind2Act World (#9) shows that the strongest agent scores 52.7% on reasoning-only tasks and 39.0% on execution-only tasks but just 12.0% when it has to combine them, and AgentRoboCritic (#2) traces frontier-agent failures to grasping and alignment rather than to semantic grounding. Taken together, they suggest that today's systems can each reason or act, but break down when one has to feed the other.

The method papers split into two camps that answer this gap differently. One camp keeps a learned low-level policy and adds structured scaffolding around it. PGX (#4) uses physical-predicate state machines and world-coordinate locking, RoC (#17) calls the reasoning agent only when a progress monitor detects trouble and cuts token use by 65%, and FastRoverVLA (#21) reuses VLA plans through a motion-corrected action buffer at a 16–21× lower inference cost. The other camp turns the VLM itself into the controller. EmbodyAgent (#23) uses RL to take an open 9B VLM from 0/100 to 84/100 held-out LIBERO successes through raw code-as-action, and UniCraft (#15) refines a shared skill library across six embodiments without any weight updates. ACE-Brain-0.7 (#6) sits between the two camps and supplies the execution-state awareness both need. Its 9B model beats GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 on task-progress reasoning.

A third thread is a careful look at what supervision VLAs learn from. TacInno (#12) keeps only the part of the tactile signal that vision and actions cannot predict, and PhysVAM (#28) predicts future task value instead of future pixels. UniVEP (#29) shows that velocity and endpoint targets suit different noise levels in flow matching, while OVOA-VLA (#25) adds supervision in each camera's frame. "Beyond the Sink" (#14) adds a needed interpretability check by showing that high attention on endpoint tokens does not mean they function as sinks. On the data side, Awaken-HI (#11, 50K hours of whole-body human data with a measured scaling law) and HumanCOIN (#10, 12.5K hours with gaze, EMG and EEG tiers) both bet that human experience, recorded richly enough, is the scalable route to humanoid skill. O²LaF (#1) makes a related bet that pretrained video generators already contain the inverse and forward dynamics needed to extract latent actions. One caveat for this batch: Semantic Scholar returned no author h-index data for any paper, so ranks follow arXiv listing order rather than author influence.

TacInno: Teaching Vision-Language-Action Models the Contact Dynamics Beyond Vision and Action

2026-11-19 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Weile Li (h=n/a)
Weile Li, Yunfan Lou, Muqing Shi, Zihao Zhang, Zhenbiao Huang
Core Contributions
  • Explains why adding touch to VLAs often helps little: most tactile change, such as gel deformation during gripper closure, is already predictable from vision and the executed action, so models learn to ignore it.
  • Defines tactile innovation as the residual contact dynamics that vision and action cannot explain, and compresses it into a discrete code with a tokenizer conditioned on both.
  • Uses that code only as an auxiliary training target. The deployed policy has no extra module, so the gain comes at no inference cost.
  • Reaches 87.5% in simulation (6 tasks) and 70.5% in the real world (4 tasks), beating the strongest visuo-tactile baseline by 10.7 and 9.0 points.
Show abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently been equipped with tactile sensing to perceive visually subtle contact events in contact-rich manipulation. However, adding tactile input yields inconsistent gains, and some visuo-tactile VLA models rely little on tactile input, because much of the tactile change, such as the gel deformation during gripper closure, is predictable from visual observations and executed actions. To address this problem, we introduce TacInno, which isolates the contact dynamics that vision and executed actions cannot explain, termed tactile innovation, and uses tactile innovation only as a training target. Specifically, the TacInno tokenizer, conditioned on visual observations and executed actions, compresses the future tactile change into a discrete code, and the policy, TacInno-VLA, learns to predict the code, adding no module for the code at deployment. TacInno-VLA achieves average success rates of 87.5% across six simulation tasks and 70.5% across four real-world tasks, outperforming the strongest visuo-tactile baseline by 10.7 and 9.0 percentage points, respectively. Ablation studies validate the contributions of visual and action conditioning, multi-frame encoding, and auxiliary code prediction. Together, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of tactile innovation as a supervision signal for contact-rich manipulation.

Beyond the Sink Interpretation: Radial Attention in Flow-Based VLA Policies

2026-11-19 cs.RO 👤 Yuxi Zheng (h=n/a)
Yuxi Zheng, Yueqian Lin, Yudong Liu, Zijia Tang, Junyu Pan
Core Contributions
  • Tests the popular 'radial action sink' reading of π0.5's attention using the language-model definition of a sink: high incoming attention, a small value norm and little effect on the output.
  • The endpoint tokens fail that definition. Their extra attention is only 1.4 points above uniform and comes from RoPE, their value norms are average, and masking them changes success by less than 4 points, whereas the same value-swap on two mid-chunk tokens drops success by 28.4 points.
  • The real sink is the prefix BOS token. Zeroing its value actually raises success by +13.2 and +6.0 points in two paired runs, which suggests a free inference-time intervention.
  • A relative-offset routing profile keeps closed-loop success on held-out tasks (42.7% vs a 38.7% baseline), while shuffled routing collapses it to 3–4%. The broader lesson is that attention mass alone says little about a token's function.
Show abstract
Prior work calls the high attention on the first and last action tokens of a flow-based VLA policy a radial action sink, and uses these tokens to set the execution horizon. In language models, a sink is a token with high incoming attention, a small value, and little effect on the output. We test whether that profile holds, not the horizon rule, in one $π_{0.5}$ checkpoint on five RoboCasa tasks. The two endpoints receive 5.4% of action-key mass, 1.4 percentage points above their uniform share, and rotary positional encoding produces this excess: directly in shallow layers and through upstream action-token RoPE deeper in the network. The endpoints do not fit the sink profile. Their value norms stay near the action-token mean in all 18 layers. On 250 paired episodes, masking them or zeroing their values changes success by less than 4 points, and replacing their value directions with those of another observation lowers it by 3.2 to 7.2 points across three donors, whereas the same replacement on two non-endpoint tokens in the executed prefix lowers it by 28.4 points. The token that does fit the sink profile on mass and value norm is the prefix BOS; on this checkpoint, zeroing its value raises success in two paired runs, by +13.2 points (95% CI [+7.0, +19.4]) and +6.0 points ([+0.2, +11.8]). Inside the action chunk, a relative-offset routing profile estimated on other tasks keeps 75% of action-token spread and keeps closed-loop success on three held-out tasks (42.7% against a 38.7% baseline), while uniform or shuffled routing drops success to 3-4%. High attention mass alone does not tell what a token does.

Teaching Vision-Language-Action Models to Stop Safely

2026-11-19 cs.RO 👤 Zheyuan Liu (h=n/a)
Zheyuan Liu, Chuhan Wang, Jinduo Guo, Yue Huang, Xiangchi Yuan
Core Contributions
  • Shows that refusal at the start of a task is not enough. Policies that refuse a prohibited task from its initial state still complete it when taking over partway, and 'not finishing' can still mean dropping a held object.
  • SAFI builds fallback targets (a stationary arm and consistent gripper hold) throughout prohibited-task demonstrations, which gives the policy an explicit safe behaviour to switch to at any intermediate state.
  • Trains these targets jointly with the original actions of the allowed tasks, so safety does not erode the robot's other skills.
  • With OpenVLA-OFT on LIBERO/BadLIBERO, prohibited-task completion after takeover drops to 1.3% with zero recorded physical incidents and high success on the retained tasks.
Show abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models enable robots to perform diverse tasks from visual observations and language instructions. While prior work has explored task suppression and refusal, stopping prohibited behavior once execution is underway and controlling the resulting physical response remain challenging. Our evaluation reveals that policies which suppress a task from its initial state can still complete it when taking over partway through execution. Even when the task remains unfinished, the robot may drop a held object, making non-completion an incomplete measure of safety. To address these challenges, we introduce State-Aware Fallback Imitation (SAFI) for tasks where holding provides an appropriate alternative response. SAFI comprises two stages, (1) fallback target construction, which assigns stationary arm commands and consistent gripper targets throughout prohibited-task demonstrations, and (2) joint policy adaptation, which learns these targets alongside the original actions of retained tasks. This supervision teaches an explicit alternative response at intermediate execution states while maintaining the robot's other skills. Extensive experiments with OpenVLA-OFT across LIBERO and BadLIBERO tasks evaluate prohibited completion, physical incidents, and retained-task performance. On the evaluated LIBERO tasks, SAFI reduces aggregate target success after takeover to 1.3\%, with no recorded physical incidents or action saturation, while preserving high retained-task success.

One View, One Action: Spatially Generalizable VLAs through Observation-Aligned Experts and Cross Graph Consistency

2026-11-19 cs.RO 👤 Jinxi Liu (h=n/a)
Jinxi Liu, Ruifeng Zhai, Hao Liu, Guangrun Wang
Core Contributions
  • Multi-view VLAs only receive actions in the robot base frame and must learn implicitly how each camera view relates to them. OVOA-VLA also supervises actions in each camera's frame, so one motion yields several observation-aligned targets.
  • A base-frame expert and view-specific experts interact through two directed attention graphs, and Cross Graph Consistency makes both graphs agree on the base-frame prediction.
  • Can run in full or base-only mode at inference (88.1% / 85.7% on LIBERO-Plus), so the view experts are needed only for training.
  • Under real camera and initialization shifts, base-only inference raises success from π0.5's 28.3% to 58.3% across three tasks.
Show abstract
Multi-view vision-language-action (VLA) models still face challenges in spatial generalization, particularly under changes in camera viewpoints and robot initial states. These models typically receive action supervision only in the robot base frame, requiring them to implicitly learn the spatial correspondence between observations from each view and base-frame actions. We propose One View, One Action (OVOA-VLA), which learns to predict actions in each view's camera frame, allowing the same physical motion to provide diverse, observation-aligned supervision across views. OVOA-VLA jointly trains a base-frame expert and view-specific action experts, whose robot-state inputs and action targets are expressed in their respective camera frames. Two complementary directed attention graphs model both directions of interaction between the base and view-specific experts, jointly leveraging view-specific action supervision to improve base-frame prediction. Cross Graph Consistency further encourages agreement between the two graphs' base-frame predictions for the same sample. With the same multi-view inputs, the model supports both full and base-only inference. Experiments across multiple simulation benchmarks demonstrate improved manipulation performance and robustness to camera-viewpoint and robot-initialization perturbations. Full and base-only inference achieve average success rates of 88.1% and 85.7% on LIBERO-Plus, respectively. In real-world evaluations, base-only inference raises the average success rates from $π_{0.5}$'s 28.3% to 58.3% across three tasks under spatial distribution shifts.

PhysVAM: Physics-oriented Future Value Action Model for Dynamic Object Manipulation

2026-11-19 cs.RO 👤 Yuxiang Yang (h=n/a)
Yuxiang Yang, Jingbo Zhang, Jiaru Zhong, Junliang Chen, Yirui Zhou
Core Contributions
  • Argues that predicting future states does not show whether an interaction will help the task. PhysVAM predicts future task value at several horizons instead of reconstructing future frames.
  • The value branch learns horizon-specific latents from both successful and failed trajectories, conditioned on the history of object and robot motion, which is key when objects keep moving during execution.
  • Gated cross-attention feeds these value latents into action generation, steering the policy toward interactions that move the task forward.
  • Improves success by +27.0 points in simulation and +27.5 in the real world over the strongest baselines on dynamic-object manipulation.
Show abstract
Predicting future scene or object states does not necessarily reveal how robot-object interactions will advance or hinder a task. This challenge is particularly pronounced in dynamic robotic manipulation, where objects continue moving during perception and execution. Effective action generation therefore requires not only modeling object dynamics but also anticipating how future interactions will shape task progress. To this end, we propose \textbf{PhysVAM}, a Physics-oriented Future Value Action Model that represents anticipated interactions through their effects on future task progress. This value-based formulation provides task-directed foresight without reconstructing future visual states. Conditioned on current observations and historical object and robot motion, PhysVAM jointly learns action generation and multi-horizon future-value prediction. The value branch learns horizon-specific latent representations through future task-value prediction from successful and failed trajectories. These future-value representations are integrated into the action policy through gated cross-attention, guiding action generation toward interactions that facilitate task success. Extensive simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate superior task success, with absolute success-rate improvements of 27.0 and 27.5 percentage points over the strongest baselines in simulation and real-world tasks, respectively. Project page: https://metarobo.github.io/physvam.github.io/.

UniVEP: Unifying Velocity and Endpoint Prediction for Robot Manipulation

2026-11-19 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Zhengtu Liang (h=n/a)
Zhengtu Liang, Qiwei Liang, Tao Lin, Shihang Weng, Boyang Cai
Core Contributions
  • Flow-matching policies usually commit to a single prediction target. This paper systematically compares velocity (V), endpoint (X) and velocity-weighted endpoint (X–V) prediction across policies, benchmarks and step budgets.
  • Finds complementary regimes: endpoint prediction is more stable under high noise, and velocity gives more accurate corrections near clean actions, consistent with the concentrated covariance spectra of clean actions.
  • UniVEP trains both heads with consistency supervision and switches from endpoint recovery to velocity refinement as noise falls, still using one network pass per step.
  • On RoboTwin a single StarVLA pass reaches 61.36%, beating the best single-target baseline at 16 passes (59.96%). With LiLa-WAM, two passes beat 16 and cut latency by 77.1%.
Show abstract
Flow-matching robot policies usually use one prediction target throughout action generation, even though recovering actions from noise and polishing nearly clean actions bring different challenges. We systematically compare velocity prediction (V), endpoint prediction (X), and velocity-weighted endpoint prediction (X--V) across multiple robot policies, benchmarks, and inference budgets. On StarVLA and LiLa-WAM, X--V works best for few-step generation, while V gains more from iterative refinement. We further uncover their complementary strengths across noise levels: clean actions have concentrated covariance spectra, endpoint predictions stay more stable under high noise, and velocity predictions offer more accurate corrections near clean actions. Inspired by these findings, we introduce UniVEP, which learns both prediction targets together with consistency supervision and switches from endpoint recovery to velocity refinement as noise drops. Each step only needs one network run, and joint training also boosts single-step endpoint generation. Tests on simulated and real-world manipulation show better success rates and efficiency. On RoboTwin, UniVEP hits 61.36% success with one StarVLA run, beating the best single-target baseline at sixteen runs (59.96%). With LiLa-WAM, two runs outperform the best sixteen-step single-target baseline and cut inference latency by 77.1%.

AgentRoboCritic: Understanding the Capability of Frontier Agents on Direct Robot Control

2026-11-19 cs.ROcs.CLcs.CV 👤 Yiyang Du (h=n/a)
Yiyang Du, Yuxuan Kuang, Qitao Zhao, Yixuan Wang, Zhanqiu Guo
Core Contributions
  • Moves frontier-agent evaluation from episode success rates to action-level analysis. The agent states a local intent before each low-level numeric action, and a critic judges whether the outcome matched that intent.
  • Across 12 manipulation benchmarks the analysis separates strengths (closed-loop recovery, semantic grounding, zero-shot manipulation) from the real bottleneck, which is physical execution such as grasping and fine alignment, with uneven recovery across failure types.
  • Releases 12,844 annotated actions and an open 30B critic (Muse-Glimmer-30B) whose failure-mode accuracy rises from 32.58% to 74.24% after fine-tuning. This makes fine-grained failure analysis cheap enough to run routinely.
  • Its finding that agents do well at what to do but poorly at how to make contact lines up with today's PhyDEX and Mind2Act results.
Show abstract
Frontier multimodal language agents such as GPT-6 Astra have demonstrated strong capabilities in controlling robots directly via low-level numerical actions without high-level primitives. However, existing benchmarks report only episode-level success rates, which show neither how these agents succeed nor where they fail. In this paper, we present a systematic action-level analysis of frontier agents on direct robot control across 12 manipulation benchmarks. We introduce AgentRoboCritic, an intent-grounded critique framework in which the policy agent states a local intent alongside each action, and a critic evaluates the action's outcome and failure mode against this intent. Our action-level analysis demonstrates the promising capabilities of frontier agents on direct robot control, including closed-loop recovery from local execution failures, reliable semantic grounding across diverse scenes, and strong zero-shot manipulation without task-specific adaptation. It also identifies their current limitations, including task-relevant physical execution bottlenecks such as grasping and alignment, and uneven recovery across failure modes. Finally, we validate our critic on a 400-action human-annotated benchmark and release a dataset of 12,844 annotated actions alongside a fine-tuned open-source critic, Muse-Glimmer-30B, whose failure-mode accuracy rises from 32.58% to 74.24%, enabling low-cost action-level analysis.

ACE-Brain-0.7: Learning Execution-Grounded Reasoning for Embodied Agents

2026-11-19 cs.RO 👤 Yinhe Wang (h=n/a)
Yinhe Wang, Shijun Shi, Feng Pan, Tao Tao, Wei Mao
Core Contributions
  • Treats progress estimation and execution-state judgment as core VLM skills. The 9B model is trained on 3.7M QA pairs drawn from egocentric human video, robot episodes and simulation, spanning every stage of execution.
  • Averages 76.5 across six task-reasoning benchmarks, ahead of GPT-6 Astra (72.7) and Claude Opus 5.5 (73.5). A fine-tuned open 9B model thus matches or beats frontier models on embodied progress reasoning.
  • Also leads on spatial grounding (75.90 average over seven benchmarks; 91.25 Acc@0.5 on RefCOCOg), so one model covers both the 'where' and the 'how far along' questions.
  • Controlled ablations show that it makes better use of visual history to tell execution states apart. This is the capability that monitors such as RoC (#17) and critics such as AgentRoboCritic (#2) depend on.
Show abstract
Reliable reasoning for long-horizon embodied tasks requires predictions grounded in the observed execution state. We present ACE-Brain-0.7, a 9B vision--language model that combines spatial grounding, task understanding, and progress estimation. To train the model, we curate 3.7 million question--answer pairs, drawing execution supervision from egocentric human videos, robot episodes, and simulation. These questions span different stages of an execution and connect task-level judgments with observed progress and outcomes. We then fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B on this collection together with additional task, spatial-grounding, and progress-estimation data. Across six public task-reasoning benchmarks, ACE-Brain-0.7 achieves an average score of 76.5, ahead of GPT-6 Astra at 72.7 and Claude Opus 5.5 at 73.5. Our model also leads the evaluated models in spatial grounding, averaging 75.90 across seven benchmarks and reaching 91.25 Acc@0.5 on RefCOCOg. Controlled evaluations further indicate that our task-only model makes more effective use of visual history and more accurately distinguishes execution states. Together, these results show that ACE-Brain-0.7 effectively combines spatial perception with temporal understanding, delivering strong execution-grounded reasoning and comparing favorably with closed-source models.

JARVIS: An Agent That Designs, Builds, and Tests Circuit Boards on Its Own

2026-11-19 cs.ROeess.SY 👤 Shibo Zhou (h=n/a)
Shibo Zhou, Tianqi Hong, Zheng Yan, He Yang
Core Contributions
  • Extends embodied agents from manipulation to autonomous hardware R&D. One text instruction starts a full design–fabricate–test–redesign loop for power-electronics PCBs on unmodified desktop equipment.
  • The agent scans its workspace into an executable digital twin and rehearses robot motions, fabrication and circuit behaviour there before using materials. The rehearsal caught five issues, including a test criterion that would have wrongly rejected a working board.
  • Coordinates a robot arm, an ink-and-paste PCB printer and a pick-and-place machine, then measures the board with an OPAL-RT real-time simulator and an oscilloscope and revises the design in KiCad based on what deviated.
  • First run on a 60 Hz notch filter: 2 h 39 min with no human intervention, 50.6 dB attenuation at 60 Hz, and faults resolved autonomously along the way.
Show abstract
Power electronics is central to the energy transition, but developing new hardware still relies on human-led design, fabrication, testing, and iteration. We present JARVIS, an embodied AI agent that autonomously closes this loop for circuit boards. JARVIS maintains machine-specific capabilities and constraints, scans its workspace using a robot-mounted 3D scanner, and constructs an executable digital twin in which it rehearses robot motions, fabrication steps, and circuit behavior before consuming materials. A single text instruction initiates the physical experiment. Using unmodified desktop equipment, the agent coordinates a robot arm, a PCB printer that deposits conductive silver ink and solder paste, and a pick-and-place machine. It verifies progress using cameras and sensors, then tests the fabricated circuit with an OPAL-RT real-time simulator and an oscilloscope. Based on the measurements, JARVIS diagnoses deviations from the design and generates a revised design in KiCad for subsequent rehearsal. In a demonstration involving a 60 Hz notch filter for grid-connected converter sensing, JARVIS completed a first-run experiment in 2 h 39 min without human intervention, achieving 50.6 dB attenuation at 60 Hz. Its digital-twin rehearsal identified five issues before fabrication, including a test criterion that would have incorrectly rejected a functioning board. During execution, the agent also resolved encountered faults autonomously. These results demonstrate an integrated agent-driven workflow for physical design, fabrication, measurement, and iterative improvement of power-electronic circuitry. To our knowledge, JARVIS is the first system in power electronics in which an agent closes the physical design-build-test-learn loop for circuit boards.

Alignment Drives Reuse: Learning Generalizable Robot-Use Agents from Heterogeneous Experience

2026-11-19 cs.RO 👤 Ziyue Wang (h=n/a)
Ziyue Wang, Yaopeng Yang, Liyi Zhou
Core Contributions
  • Asks whether heterogeneous experience across benchmarks and embodiments can jointly improve one shared skill library. The bet is that alignment, not more data, is what unlocks reuse.
  • UniCraft makes coordinate conventions consistent, aligns sensing and control interfaces across robots and puts tasks in a canonical format, so feedback from any setting refines the same skills and the orchestration policy.
  • Learns without demonstrations or weight updates. Over 2,700 rollouts per agent model it raises success on unseen tasks by 9–28 points on LIBERO-PRO, 10.8–13.3 on RoboTwin 2.0 and 3.1–14.4 on RoboCasa365.
  • The frozen skills then transfer zero-shot to 4 new embodiments and 5 other agent models, which suggests a reusable skill layer that sits apart from any one VLM.
Show abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) demonstrate broad generalization across tasks and domains. However, comparable generalization in robot manipulation remains challenging. Heterogeneity in robot embodiments, task formulations, geometric conventions, and action interfaces fragments interaction experience across benchmarks and hardware. We rethink robot skill learning through alignment: Can heterogeneous interactions across benchmarks and embodiments jointly improve one shared skillset for robot manipulation? We propose UniCraft, a framework for learning robot manipulation skills by aligning heterogeneous settings. Specifically, UniCraft represents observed scene geometry using consistent coordinate conventions and aligns sensing and control interfaces across robots. It organizes tasks from different benchmarks in a canonical format, allowing interaction feedback to jointly refine shared skills and the orchestration policy that guides their selection and composition, without any demonstrations or model weight updates. We interleave tasks from 3 benchmarks and 6 embodiments, collecting 2,700 learning rollouts per agent model. During training, UniCraft improves pooled task success on unseen scenarios and tasks by 9-28 percentage points on LIBERO-PRO, 10.8-13.3 on RoboTwin 2.0, and 3.1-14.4 on RoboCasa365 (Epochs 1-3). After training, with the learned skills frozen, UniCraft demonstrates zero-shot transfer across 4 new robot embodiments and 5 additional agent models on robosuite and RoboDojo.

Reasoning on Call: Efficient Agentic Robot Control for Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation

2026-11-19 cs.RO 👤 Zilang Cen (h=n/a)
Zilang Cen, Chao Tang, Fangcheng Liu, Weishi Mi, Wei Hao
Core Contributions
  • Instead of calling an expensive reasoning agent at every step, RoC calls it only when an online task-state monitor flags that progress or the predicted next subtask looks wrong.
  • The monitor combines temporal visual observations with ordered interaction memory and decides whether to repair execution, revise the plan or both. Only verified outcomes update memory.
  • On RMBench it improves success by 24.0 points and cuts token use by 65.3% compared with the strongest agent baseline, and beats the best pure-policy baseline by 15.1 points.
  • Shows that efficiency and reliability need not trade off: routine execution stays with the fast policy, and reasoning is reserved for real decision points. Validated on three real long-horizon tasks.
Show abstract
Large multimodal models can support long-horizon robot manipulation, but frequent model calls incur substantial latency and token costs. The central challenge is determining when agent assistance or intervention is needed and whether errors require correcting robot execution, subtask planning, or both. To address this problem, we propose RoC (Reasoning on Call), an efficient framework that coordinates a low-level policy and a high-level reasoning agent through an online task-state monitor. The monitor combines temporal visual observations with ordered interaction memory to jointly estimate progress within the active subtask and predict its successor. These predictions guide event-triggered agent review for coordinated execution repair and plan revision. Verified outcomes govern memory updates and subtask transitions, allowing routine execution to remain with the policy while reasoning focuses on decisions requiring additional assessment. On RMBench, RoC improves task success rate by 24.0 percentage points and reduces token consumption by 65.3% compared with the strongest agent-based baseline. It also outperforms the strongest policy-based baseline by 15.1 percentage points in task success rate. Real-world experiments further validate its effectiveness across three long-horizon manipulation tasks. Overall, RoC supports efficient agent intervention and coordinated recovery across execution and planning for long-horizon robot manipulation.

EmbodyAgent: Post-Training Embodied VLM Agents via Distillation, SFT, and RL

2026-11-19 cs.RO 👤 Wei Dai (h=n/a)
Wei Dai, Jinkun Liu, Jiayi Chen, Han Liu, Wenbo Ding
Core Contributions
  • A full post-training recipe for turning an open 9B VLM into a robot-use agent that writes raw control code. The base model starts at 0/100 because it cannot follow interactive tool contracts.
  • Step one distills a black-box frontier teacher (no logits) through a forced think-then-code format with success filtering. Step two is SFT with both encoders trainable. Step three is online group-relative RL with a runtime-error-penalized reward.
  • Held-out LIBERO Spatial success rises from 0 → 14 (SFT) → 84/100 (RL), and median model calls per episode fall from 36 to 11, which shows that RL, not imitation, does most of the work.
  • Identifies a self-reinforcing runtime-error collapse specific to outcome-RL on executable code and provides a monitoring and curation protocol to prevent it, a practical lesson for anyone training code-as-action agents.
Show abstract
