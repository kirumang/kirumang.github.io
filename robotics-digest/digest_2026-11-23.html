Research Landscape

The loudest signal in this batch is that frontier models used as robot controllers are now a research object in their own right, with their own design science, benchmarks and safety audits. Embo-Harness (#3) and RUIBench (#7) reach the same conclusion from different directions: the interface around the model matters as much as the model. Changing only the robot-use interface moves a frontier agent from 45% to 81% success in RUIBench, and Embo-Harness gains 26 points on RoboLab-120 mostly by leaning on the model's native visual reasoning instead of handcrafted skills. ACAP (#2), ReWeave (#12), EmboEagle (#5) and SurgeCount (#13) all treat the harness as the thing to optimize, whether by splitting one robot into asynchronous coding agents, evolving a task topology without touching weights, gating expensive VLM calls, or composing skills under a written clinical protocol. RoboUniverse-10K (#14) and Robo-MOPD (#15) then try to close the gap between closed and open models through executable environments and multi-teacher distillation. RoboUniverse reports a striking 85.8% versus at most 10% split between frontier and open models.

The second thread is evaluation that does not trust proxies. SayDo (#18) shows that text-only safety tests do not predict embodied behavior: hazardous-action rates rise from 14% in chat to 47% in closed-loop simulation. WorldLeague (#16) finds that VLMs which answer every question correctly still make zero physical progress in 71.8% of those episodes. RUIBench documents a "verification gap" in which agents declare success despite visible failure, and HIVE-Bench (#27) concludes that no representation statistic can replace a rollout. Even the classic SLAM ground truth is not exempt: Place-Revisit (#30) finds metre-scale inconsistencies in KITTI's reference poses. Read together, these papers argue that the field's cheap metrics (text QA, pooled-feature probes, published ground truth) systematically overstate what robots can do.

The third thread is making VLAs and world-action models (WAMs) fast enough and geometrically aware enough to deploy. Three compression papers reach a shared insight: the parts that look important by naive measures are not the parts the action depends on. MoOP (#1) must preserve reasoning and trajectory importance separately, and NoveltyGate (#8) shows that input novelty, not approximation error, picks the tokens that matter, favoring the wrist camera. VITA-WAM (#4) gets 1-step generation by aligning observation and action manifolds. On the representation side, WeaveVLA (#6), Foresight Forcing (#17) and DexLatentWorld (#21) inject explicit 3D relational or geometric structure, while Native LAM/PAWlicy (#23) and Wrist Video Policy (#10) mine pretrained video models for dynamics without generating video at test time. The direction is clear: future VLAs will be distilled from large video and 3D models, but will run as lean, geometry-grounded policies wrapped in agentic harnesses that decide when to call something bigger. ACAP: Asynchronous Coding Agents for Efficient Robot Control

2026-11-23 cs.ROcs.MA 👤 Zexin He (h=n/a)
Zexin He, Rui Li, Yushi Lan, Chengkai Jin, Zhi Hou
Core Contributions
  • Proposes running one robot as a team of persistent coding agents, one per control group (arm and gripper, torso and chassis). Unlike prior single-agent code-as-policy systems, independent sub-tasks no longer wait on each other.
  • A hyper-agent assigns work, tracks physical dependencies and can temporarily take control of several groups for tightly coupled bimanual moves. This is the key difference from a fixed delegation scheme.
  • Across 72 real rollouts in 25 task and platform settings, it cuts task-balanced time cost by 28.2% versus a single-agent Direct Policy, and it beats Fixed Delegation on all 8 collaborative settings.
  • Releases a real-world dataset of observations, generated programs, actions and feedback, a scarce resource for studying how coding agents behave physically.
Show abstract
Coding agents can construct and revise robot behavior during execution, but using one agent for the whole robot can delay otherwise independent work. We introduce Asynchronous Coding Agents as Policy (ACAP), an asynchronous framework that organizes one robot as a team of persistent agents. Each member reasons and acts for a configurable control group, such as an arm with its gripper or the torso and chassis, enabling asynchronous reasoning and execution. However, different robot components often have physical dependencies that require coordination. To address this challenge, ACAP introduces a hyper-agent that assigns tasks, coordinates dependencies, and dynamically allocates control between the lead and member agents. This allows independent work to progress concurrently while supporting tightly coupled manipulation when necessary. Across 72 real-world rollouts covering 25 task/platform settings, ACAP reduces task-balanced time cost by 28.2% compared with a single-agent Direct Policy, with improvements across Fully Parallel, Parallel-Sequential, and Collaborative tasks. Across all eight Collaborative settings, ACAP also achieves lower mean time cost than Fixed Delegation, where only member agents can control their assigned groups. An additional 90-rollout study shows lower mean 30-minute-capped time cost than Direct on all six tasks. We further demonstrate mobile manipulation using the same member interface for chassis and torso control. We present the framework and a real-world rollout dataset containing observations, generated programs, actions, and feedback to support research on how coding agents reason and act in the physical world.

Embo-Harness: Towards Understanding Frontier-Model-as-Policy Harness

2026-11-23 cs.RO 👤 Xiao-Ming Wu (h=n/a)
Xiao-Ming Wu, Kang Liao, Yuexiao Ma, Taolin Zhang, Mingqian Zhou
Core Contributions
  • Treats "frontier model as policy" as a systems-design question. It separately ablates prompting, planning, action execution and tool use, then distills six findings into a recipe.
  • The central lesson runs against much prior work: interfaces that exploit the model's native visual reasoning and tool use beat handcrafted plans, skill libraries and action priors.
  • Reaches 83.3% on RoboLab-120 (+26.2 points over the strongest baseline) and 15/20 zero-shot real-world trials across 4 tasks.
  • Controlled interventions show that the model's success depends mostly on accurate spatial target estimation, not on producing low-level joints or trajectories. This tells future work where to invest.
Show abstract
Frontier multimodal models exhibit extraordinary generalization in generating robot actions from observations and instructions, enabling a new paradigm in which they serve directly as policies within closed-loop robotic harnesses. However, the design principles of such harnesses, and the capabilities underlying their strong performance, remain unclear. We present Embo-Harness, a framework for systematically studying frontier-model-as-policy harnesses. We investigate four components of the robotic agentic loop, namely, prompting, planning, action execution, and tool use, and distill six empirical findings into a practical recipe. This recipe prioritizes interfaces that exploit the model's native visual reasoning and tool-use capabilities over handcrafted plans, skills, or action priors, and identifies effective choices for action representation, chunking, reasoning effort, visual observations, short multi-action sequences, and policy refinement. On RoboLab-120, Embo-Harness achieves an 83.3\% success rate, outperforming the strongest reported baseline by 26.2 percentage points. It also succeeds in 15 of 20 zero-shot trials across four real-world manipulation tasks. Beyond performance, controlled interventions indicate that the studied model depends more strongly on accurate spatial target estimation than on explicit low-level joint or trajectory generation. These results provide both a practical recipe for building strong frontier-model-as-policy harnesses and a clearer account of the capabilities that drive their performance.

EmboEagle: An Efficient Agentic Embodied Framework for Long-horizon Robotic Manipulation

2026-11-23 cs.RO 👤 Fan Xu (h=n/a)
Fan Xu, Zhongtao Wang, Hao Wu, Yitong Zhu, Wenzhuo Ma
Core Contributions
  • Targets the two failure modes of long-horizon agents: WAMs alone compound physical errors, and calling a VLM every chunk is too slow and has no 3D memory.
  • A lightweight routing gate decides when to call the VLM, using only the action expert's latent transitions and subtask embeddings. It is distilled from teacher interventions and needs no extra vision encoder.
  • When invoked, the VLM runs parameterized recovery grounded in structured memory (initial references, grasp bindings, tool traces) and must verify the result afterwards. Prior agentic loops often skip this verification step.
  • Achieves 91.3% on RMBench and 90.6% on LIBERO-Mem (+8.0 and +9.8 points). Adaptive gating removes 74.2% of VLM calls for only a 2.4-point success cost versus calling the VLM every chunk.
Show abstract
Long-horizon robotic manipulation demands reliable execution across interdependent stages, yet standalone World-Action Models (WAMs) suffer from compounding physical errors, while stateless Vision-Language Model (VLM) supervision incurs prohibitive chunkwise latency and lacks 3D spatial persistence. We present EmboEagle, a closed-loop agentic framework that unifies subtask-conditioned WAM control, latent-space adaptive supervision, and memory-guided geometric recovery. First, we adapt sequential WAMs via decoupled two-stage flow matching that preserves cross-stage visual history while calibrating truncated action supervision at subtask boundaries. Second, without an auxiliary vision encoder, we distill teacher intervention decisions into a lightweight routing gate driven solely by the WAM Action Expert's pre- and post-chunk latent transitions and subtask embeddings. Third, upon invocation, the VLM decouples diagnostic status from discrete routing actions and executes parameterized recovery primitives grounded in structured memory (initial references, active grasp bindings, and tool traces), followed by mandatory post-repair verification. Across RMBench and LIBERO-Mem, EmboEagle achieves 91.3\% and 90.6\% average success, surpassing the strongest baselines by 8.0 and 9.8 percentage points, while boosting real-world success over VoLoAgent under identical executor weights. Moreover, adaptive gating cuts VLM calls by 74.2\% versus chunkwise monitoring (91.3\% vs. 93.7\% success).

ReWeave: Emergent Physical Task Topologies for Embodied Harness Optimization and Cross-Task Meta-Distillation

2026-11-23 cs.RO 👤 Yicheng Xu (h=n/a)
Yicheng Xu, Wencan Jiang, Zihao Zhu, Keru Zhou, Chang Zhou
Core Contributions
  • Improves an embodied agent with frozen policies and perception by co-optimizing its harness (context, workflows, control loops) together with an emergent physical task topology of preconditions learned from interaction.
  • When the topology changes, past trials are re-projected onto it. Failures that coarse progress signals called 0% often turn out to contain retained progress (89% of 159 failed RoboDojo trials).
  • Merges 72 specialist units into 44 reusable groups (0.34× the size of simple concatenation), giving one compact shared harness that supports continual learning.
  • Held-out success rises from 89.0% to 98.75% on LIBERO-PRO and from 25.5% to 58.25% on RoboDojo. On 8 unseen tasks it reaches 75.0% after continual learning, versus 41.3% for direct distillation.
Show abstract
Long-horizon embodied tasks involve physical dependencies that may only become clear through interaction. As task understanding deepens, past trials can reveal new information for adaptation and transfer. However, coarse progress feedback cannot identify which physical preconditions held, leaving search unguided, while task-specific optimization easily overfits to individual scenes, making cross-task transfer brittle. We introduce ReWeave, a framework that jointly optimizes an embodied agent harness and an emergent physical task topology from interaction without updating policy weights. Keeping execution policies and perception frozen, ReWeave co-optimizes coupled context, workflows, and control loops. As new evidence revises the topology, ReWeave reprojects past trials onto the updated task understanding, exposing retained progress, regressions, and bottlenecks that guide parent selection and search width. Across tasks, matching verified local mechanisms to unmet preconditions consolidates 72 specialist units into 44 reusable groups ($0.34\times$ full concatenation) within a compact shared harness capable of continual learning. ReWeave improves held-out success from 89.00% to 98.75% on LIBERO-PRO and from 25.50% to 58.25% on RoboDojo, while uncovering retained progress in 89% of 159 failed RoboDojo development trials and translating stronger offline reasoning into higher online performance. On eight unseen LIBERO-PRO tasks, the shared harness reaches 51.3% success versus 41.3% for direct distillation, rising to 75.0% after continual learning.

SurgeCount: A Protocol-Guided Composition Harness for Bimanual Surgical Sponge Counting

2026-11-23 cs.RO 👤 Sung Eun Kim (h=n/a)
Sung Eun Kim, Xiaoman Zhang, Xucheng Wang, Pranav Rajpurkar
Core Contributions
  • Applies robotics to a real clinical safety ritual: counting retained surgical sponges. This requires isolating deformable, tangled sponges one at a time and placing them in strict slot order.
  • One subtask-conditioned policy learns eleven short skills and when each ends. A stateful VLM picks the next skill from an operator-written protocol that specifies order, visual checks and recovery.
  • Recovery comes from recomposing existing skills, not from a separate recovery policy, and every decision traces back to the written protocol. This auditability matters in the operating room.
  • Fills 47/60 slots and completes 12/20 procedures, versus 27/60 and 3/20 for the best end-to-end policy on the same data. A minimal prompt drops results to 29/60, so the protocol itself carries much of the value.
Show abstract
We present SurgeCount, a system for auditable robotic surgical sponge counting that composes learned skills under a written procedural protocol. Retained sponges cause preventable harm, and surgical teams count every sponge before closure to reduce that risk. The robot repeatedly isolates one deformable sponge from a tangled pile and places it in the next numbered counting-tray slot, requiring handling of occluded grasp threads while preserving step order. We train one subtask-conditioned policy on eleven short skills and learn when each skill ends. At each learned boundary, a stateful vision-language model selects the next skill from an operator-authored specification of step order, visual verification, and recovery. This creates temporal commitment to each subtask and enables failures to be recovered by recomposing existing skills rather than using a dedicated recovery policy. We evaluate five methods across 100 real-robot trials using 20 matched layouts. SurgeCount fills 47 of 60 ordered slots and completes 12 of 20 procedures, compared with 27 of 60 and 3 of 20 for the strongest of four end-to-end policies trained on the same data. Replacing the detailed protocol prompt with a minimal prompt reduces performance to 29 of 60 slots and 5 of 20 completed procedures. These results show that long-horizon performance depends not only on visuomotor capacity, but also on how learned skills are committed, verified, and composed. This tabletop proof of concept motivates further evaluation of protocol-guided robotic sponge counting in clinically representative settings.

Generalizing Agentic Robotics via Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation

2026-11-23 cs.RO 👤 Xinxin Liu (h=n/a)
Xinxin Liu, Anh Dao, Wenrui Bao, Rafid Ahmed, Bingxin Xu
Core Contributions
  • Asks whether multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD), proven for consolidating LLM specialists, can produce one generalist robot planner instead of one RL specialist per domain with incompatible tools.
  • The student is trained on its own closed-loop rollouts in simulation with token-level supervision from the matching frozen specialist. This avoids the distribution shift of off-policy SFT.
  • Compares the approach head-to-head with weight merging, specialist SFT and multi-domain online RL. A single 9B planner reaches 72.9% on LIBERO-PRO and 44.8% on RoboCasa365, beating both specialists on their own benchmarks.
  • Shows its biggest edge on long-horizon tasks (+13.0 points over multi-domain RL on LIBERO-10), where dense token-level teacher signal beats sparse episode reward.
Show abstract
Building general-purpose robots is a central goal in robotics. Agentic robotics pursues this goal with planners that control robots through tool calls. However, existing methods either rely on proprietary models accessed through APIs, which incur high costs and latency, or post-train a planner with reinforcement learning (RL) in a single domain, requiring a separate specialist for each domain with incompatible tools. In large language model (LLM) post-training, multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) has been used to consolidate domain specialists into a single generalist. Yet whether MOPD can build a generalist planner for agentic robotics, how well it performs, and how it compares with other consolidation methods remain unexplored. In this paper, we systematically study these questions: we introduce Robo-MOPD, which trains a single student on closed-loop rollouts in simulation under token-level supervision from matching frozen specialists, and compare it with weight merging, off-policy specialist supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and multi-domain online RL. Experiments on LIBERO-PRO and RoboCasa365 show that a single 9B Robo-MOPD planner reaches 72.9% and 44.8% success (58.9% average), surpassing all consolidated baselines in average success and both 9B specialists on their respective benchmarks. Robo-MOPD excels on long-horizon tasks, outperforming multi-domain online RL by 13.0 percentage points on LIBERO-10.

RUIBench: What Makes a Good Robot-Use Interface?

2026-11-23 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CVcs.MM 👤 Yanzhe Chen (h=n/a)
Yanzhe Chen, Zhijun Cao, Zechen Bai, Wenzheng Zeng, Kevin Yuchen Ma
Core Contributions
  • Elevates the robot-use interface (RUI) to a first-class variable. It is the layer that decides how an agent expresses actions, and most papers treat it as fixed.
  • RUIKit crosses 6 action abstractions with 2 interaction forms (13 interfaces) under shared perception and control, so the interface effect is cleanly isolated across 30 tasks in 5 suites.
  • Changing only the interface moves a frontier agent from 45% to 81% and a fine-tuned 4B model from 13% to 47%. Interface choice can matter more than model choice.
  • Identifies a "verification gap": agents often claim success despite visible failures. This argues for task-level verification, the same point EmboEagle (#5) builds into its design.
Show abstract
Foundation models can reason about complex physical tasks, yet translating this intelligence into reliable robot execution remains challenging. We argue that a critical, often overlooked factor is the robot-use interface (RUI), which determines how agents express and execute physical actions. To systematically study its impact, we introduce RUIBench, comprising 30 manipulation tasks across five suites spanning world knowledge, in-context learning, discovery, dynamic interaction, and contact-rich manipulation. We further develop RUIKit, a unified framework that crosses six action abstractions with two interaction forms, yielding 13 interfaces under shared perception and control. In simulation, changing only the RUI shifts a frontier agent's success rate from 45% to 81%, and that of a fine-tuned 4B model from 13% to 47%. Our analysis reveals that effective interfaces reduce unnecessary decisions while preserving essential feedback, with their benefits shaped by agent behavior and physical uncertainty. We further uncover a verification gap, where agents frequently declare success despite observable failures, revealing the need for task-level verification beyond individual actions. Together, these findings establish RUI design as a critical factor in embodied agent reliability.

RoboUniverse-10K: Toward the Scaling Era of Agentic Robotics via Real-to-Sim Environments

2026-11-23 cs.ROcs.CL 👤 Jinkun Liu (h=n/a)
Jinkun Liu, Yingze Wang, Situ Wang, Deyao Hong, Mingju Gao
Core Contributions
  • Applies the software-engineering playbook (massive executable, verifiable environments) to agentic robotics: 10,807 resettable physics environments built automatically from real-world videos.
  • Environments hide privileged state and grading logic, as in Harbor-style harnesses, so agents must act from realistic sensing. A held-out set of 500 expert-verified tasks forms a zero-shot benchmark.
  • Measures a huge gap: frontier models reach 85.8% while open-source models score 2.6–10%. The bottlenecks are low-level grasping, deformables and post-contact control.
  • SFT on about 17K multi-turn trajectories (0.79B tokens) lifts a Qwen3.6-35B-A3B model from 0.8% to 21.2%, with zero-shot transfer to LIBERO-MAX, RoboCasa, RoboTwin 2.0 and a real Franka. The environments are released ready for RL.
Show abstract
Agentic Robotics, in which foundation models act as coding agents to control robots through executable programs, has emerged as a compelling paradigm for embodied intelligence. Yet unlike software engineering, where massive executable environments power post-training, robotics remains constrained by small, manual benchmarks and in-distribution evaluation. To bridge this gap, we introduce RoboUniverse-10K, 10,807 interactive, resettable, and verifiable physics environments built from real-world videos via an automated Real2Sim and quality assurance pipeline. Each runs in an isolated, Harbor-compatible harness that hides privileged states and grading logic to mirror real-world sensing. From the same pipeline, we hold out 500 unseen tasks to establish RoboUniverse-Verified, an expert-verified zero-shot benchmark. Evaluating 11 closed- and open-source models reveals a huge gap: frontier models reach 85.8\% success, while open-source baselines lag at 2.6\% to 10.0\%, bottlenecked by low-level grasping, deformable-object manipulation, and post-contact physical control. Finally, we collect about 17K multi-turn trajectories totaling 0.79B tokens with GPT-6 Astra and perform full-parameter supervised fine-tuning on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, yielding RoboUniverse-35B-A3B. Our model achieves substantial zero-shot gains on RoboUniverse-Verified, lifting the success rate from 0.8\% to 21.2\%, while achieving zero-shot task completions across LIBERO-MAX, RoboCasa, RoboTwin 2.0, and a real Franka arm. We open-source the RL-ready environments and model to lay the foundation for large-scale reinforcement learning in Agentic Robotics.

WorldLeague: Benchmarking Physical Agency

2026-11-23 cs.RO 👤 Xinyang Zhou (h=n/a)
Xinyang Zhou, Qi Li, Xinchao Wang
Core Contributions
  • Gives one common yardstick for VLMs, VLAs and WAMs on 60 simulated tasks. Each system uses its native control interface, but all are scored by progress toward the same physical goal.
  • Three suites probe different abilities: exploration to gather evidence (WorldSeek), rearrangement (WorldShape) and adapting to change (WorldShift). Results become a six-dimension "Physical Agency Profile".
  • Separates partial progress (Interactive Score) from full completion. The best system, Claude Fable 5.2, achieves only 13/60 goals with a mean score of 28.58/100.
  • VLMs that answer every final question correctly still make zero physical progress in 71.8% of those episodes. This is strong evidence that knowing and doing are separate capabilities.
Show abstract
AI systems can now operate Blender, follow instructions in complex video games, and manipulate objects with robot arms. What range of physical goals can today's AI systems achieve autonomously? Answering this question requires testing systems on tasks that demand different combinations of perception, memory, planning, and action. We introduce \textbf{WorldLeague}, a benchmark that compares AI systems on a common set of \textbf{60 simulated robot tasks} by measuring progress toward each task's physical goal from the robot's trajectory. Robots explore scenes to gather evidence in \textbf{WorldSeek}, rearrange objects in \textbf{WorldShape}, and adapt to changes in \textbf{WorldShift}. Tying these goals to a single control interface limits which AI systems can be compared. In WorldLeague, vision-language models (VLMs) choose targets and motions, while vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs) use their native controls. For each of six capability dimensions, we measure how much progress a system makes toward its goals (Interactive Score) and how often it achieves them (physical success rate). Together, these measures form the system's \emph{Physical Agency Profile}. Among 13 representative AI systems, Claude Fable 5.2 achieves 13 of the 60 goals and earns the highest mean Interactive Score (28.58/100). Fable leads in four dimensions, while GPT-6.1-Sol and GPT-6-Astra tie for first on rule-following tasks. Even when VLMs answer every final question correctly, they receive an Interactive Score of zero in 71.8\% of those episodes. WorldLeague gives Embodied AI, Physical AI, and Agentic AI a shared basis for comparing what AI systems achieve autonomously, from partial progress toward a physical goal to full goal completion. SayDo: Benchmarking the Safety of Frontier Models from Chat to Closed-Loop Robot Control

2026-11-23 cs.ROcs.AIcs.LG 👤 Benjamin Alt (h=n/a)
Benjamin Alt
Core Contributions
  • The first benchmark to track one model's safety along an embodiment gradient: from chat questions about an imagined robot to closed-loop control of a simulated arm, using the same 11 hazard-laden tasks.
  • Hazardous-action rates climb from 14% in chat to 47% in simulation, and from 2% to 80% for the worst model. Text-based safety evaluations therefore do not transfer to robot control.
  • Models flag the hazard before acting in 73% of unsafe episodes, so recognition is not the problem. The failure lies in turning that recognition into restraint during action.
  • The rise is driven mostly by open-weight models, and the safest models show overcaution on benign controls. This frames a real tradeoff for deployers choosing a controller model.
Show abstract
Frontier vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly used as high-level robot controllers, yet their safety is evaluated almost entirely in text. We introduce SayDo, a benchmark that assesses the propensity of frontier models to cause harm in embodied contexts. SayDo comprises eleven laboratory and household tasks that pair a benign instruction with a hazard latent in the scene. Tasks are implemented in six settings along an embodiment gradient, from chat questions about an imagined robot to closed-loop control of a simulated robot arm. Benign controls, safe-alternative variants and scripted operator pushback complement each task. Across six open- and closed-weight VLMs and 11,480 episodes, hazardous-action rates rise from 14% in chat to 47% in the simulator, and from 2% to 80% for the most affected model. The increase is largely driven by open-weight models. We find that models flag hazards before acting in 73% of unsafe episodes and give way to pushback about twice as often when controlling robots as in chat. The safest models also show signs of overcaution on benign controls. Safety evaluations run in text therefore do not predict the safety behavior of the same models when used as robot controllers.

HIVE-Bench: Evaluating Patch-Level Visual Representations for Egocentric Robot Manipulation

2026-11-23 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Qiwei Liang (h=n/a)
Qiwei Liang, Zhengtu Liang, Minghao Lai, Yikeng Chen, Yiming Wang
Core Contributions
  • Shows that the standard way of scoring visual encoders for robotics (one pooled vector from a third-person image) is mismatched with how bimanual policies actually read dense patch tokens from on-robot cameras.
  • Runs one shared flow-matching policy over 20 encoders from 7 families on 24 egocentric bimanual tasks, sweeping adaptation, data, size and layer.
  • Averaging patches to one token removes most of the success. Adaptation helps every encoder–task pair, and a single shared policy keeps most of the per-task success at far fewer updates.
  • An inverse-dynamics readout is the best pre-training predictor, while recognition, depth and correspondence scores do not transfer. Still, no statistic is stable enough to replace a rollout.
Show abstract
Pretrained vision is a common start for robot learning, but most comparisons still score one pooled vector from a third-person image. Bimanual policies instead read dense patch tokens from cameras on the robot. We introduce HIVE-Bench: one shared flow-matching policy reads 20 encoders from seven families on 24 egocentric bimanual tasks, across adaptation, task sharing, and sweeps of data, model size, and layer. Keeping the patches matters. Averaging each view to one token removes most of the success, adaptation helps every pair we test, and one shared policy keeps most of the success of separate policies at a fraction of the updates. The same tokens are then scored with statistics, state and object readouts, published vision scores, and an inverse-dynamics readout. That readout of the commanded motion is one of the strongest measurements available before a policy is trained. Recognition, depth, and correspondence do not transfer reliably. Fine-tuning can improve the action readout even when other measurements move the other way, and no statistic is stable enough to replace a rollout before any deployment.

WeaveVLA: 3D Relational Grounding for Vision-Language-Action Models

2026-11-23 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Zien Wang (h=n/a)
Zien Wang, Yuhao Pan, Yunfeng Fan, Nan Cheng, Xin Xia
Core Contributions
  • Argues that VLAs only implicitly know the 3D relations (object to gripper, object to reference) that decide manipulation success. WeaveVLA makes them explicit with a task-conditioned sparse graph.
