Research Landscape

The clearest theme in this batch is learning without (robot) demonstrations. One route replaces demos with code-writing agents: PyRUA-Dex (#4) has coding agents synthesize dexterous bimanual policies that beat every trained policy on Bench2Dex and DexJoCo and learn a piano piece in about 30 minutes. EmbodiedStudio (#21) uses the same closed-loop debugging to author the environments themselves, RoboRecast (#5) uses an agent to repair demonstrations that break under new layouts or embodiments, and RoboHarness-RL (#28) and Atomic Policy (#24) push VLMs to drive skills through tool calls. The other route mines human data: EgoLoAF (#23) trains a humanoid VLA from egocentric video alone and nearly matches teleoperation (73.1 vs 74.4), VidForce (#2) recovers force profiles from third-person video, and Any2Act (#26) treats humans, simulation and other robots as interchangeable demonstrators once their demos are re-expressed in the executor's action space.

A second thread is embodiment-aware generalization. OmniAct (#15) and Any2Act (#26) make opposite but complementary bets. OmniAct conditions on the robot's kinematic graph and trains across a million procedurally generated bodies, reaching 63.0% on held-out embodiments versus 17.8% for π0.5. Any2Act instead translates every demonstration into the executor's embodiment. Ada-BFM (#20) brings the same foundation-model-plus-adapter recipe to humanoid whole-body control, and iWAM (#12) makes video-pretrained world-action models cheap enough to deploy by replacing future-frame generation with a single forward pass. On the hardware side, Rus's soft-rigid hand (#1), the self-sensing hand (#18) and MRCT's confidence-blended impedance (#6) all treat compliance as something to exploit, not merely tolerate.

The third thread, and perhaps the most useful, is diagnosis over leaderboard gains. SimPWM (#3) checks whether simulator-conditioned video models actually follow the simulator and finds that some (LTX-2.3) largely ignore object state. LaWanle (#13) shows that driving models near human level on NAVSIM fail simple 90° turns in closed loop. MPQ (#19) attributes skill-policy failures to placement and correction rather than skill choice and finds offline metrics predict none of it. MRBench (#7) asks which robots are actually needed, and the Pi-depth paper (#25) measures exactly why int8 attention breaks. Read together, these papers say that open-loop or offline metrics keep overstating capability, and the field is building tools that attribute failure to specific components. That pairs naturally with the agentic thread, since closed-loop verification is what lets coding agents and harnesses self-correct.

OmniAct: One Policy, One Million Embodiments

2026-11-24 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CVcs.LG 👤 Ranjay Krishna (h=31)
Xiang Fan, Yuheng Wang, Jiayuan Yan, Bohan Fang, Wisdom Oluchi Ikezogwo
Core Contributions
  • Conditions a single policy on each robot's kinematic graph (joint positions, axes, connectivity) projected into the visual scene, so behavior adapts to morphology instead of being squeezed through a shared action space.
  • A flow-matching head predicts per-node relative joint motion, letting one model control robots with any number of DoF or kinematic layouts.
  • Trained on one million procedurally generated, physically filtered embodiments with simulated trajectories, an unusually large scale for cross-embodiment learning.
  • Reaches 63.0% on held-out embodiment-scene pairs vs 17.8% for π0.5, 64.7% vs 41.1% for GPT-6 Astra, and nearly 2× MolmoAct2 on an unseen real robot, while learning strategies that exploit each morphology.
Show abstract
A single policy that controls diverse robots must not only transfer task knowledge across embodiments, but also adapt its behavior to each robot's capabilities. Existing approaches facilitate transfer through shared action representations, but these representations do not necessarily capture how differences in robot morphology should shape manipulation strategies. We introduce OmniAct, a cross-embodiment manipulation policy that conditions directly on each robot's kinematics in a specific scene to produce embodiment-specific behavior. This kinematic graph represents the robot's joint positions, motion axes, and connectivity to its visual observation, allowing the policy to account for how its embodiment related to objects and surfaces in the scene. A flow-matching policy predicts relative joint motions for each node, enabling control across robots with different degrees of freedom and kinematic configurations. To learn how physical structure shapes behavior at scale, we procedurally design and filter one million physically feasible robot embodiments; next, we develop algorithms to generate robot trajectories in simulation for all embodiments; finally, we train across diverse simulated manipulation tasks. On 2,000 embodiment-scene-held-out evaluation episodes, OmniAct achieves 63.0% success, compared to 17.8% for pi 0.5 and 57.9% for the strongest baseline; on the task families GPT-6 Astra was evaluated on, it achieves 64.7% against GPT-6 Astra's 41.1%. On a previously unseen real-world robot, it achieves nearly twice the success rate of the next best method, MolmoAct2. Beyond task success, we observe that OmniAct learns distinct manipulation strategies that exploit the capabilities and accommodate the limitations of different morphologies. Project: https://omniact.github.io/
#26 h=21

Any2Act: Scaling In-Context Robot Learning from Heterogeneous Demonstrations

2026-11-24 cs.RO 👤 Yan-Chao Yang (h=21)
Ziang Ren, Zifan Wang, Zhuoyang Li, Benhao Qin, Haoyang Li
Core Contributions
  • Expresses every demonstration in the executor robot's camera layout and action space, regardless of who demonstrated it. This separates learning to follow demos from adding new demo sources.
  • Pretrains once on 1.2M real robot-to-robot trajectories across 10K tasks, then adds human video, simulation, UMI or other robots via small source-specific adapters trained on a few hundred pairs while the base model stays frozen.
  • A training-free tracker feeds only the relevant window of a long demonstration and can roll back to redo failed steps, keeping context bounded.
  • From one demo, reaches 57.5% on unseen tasks without fine-tuning, beating VLAs fine-tuned with 50 demos per task. Non-robot demonstrators retain 74–91% of that performance.
Show abstract
Robot in-context learning (ICL) enables a policy to perform unseen tasks by following contextual demonstrations, without task-specific fine-tuning. This is increasingly valuable as large language models reasoning at low frequency can specify tasks through demonstrations and delegate high-frequency execution to such a policy. However, methods conditioned on human videos are bounded by scarce human-robot pairs, while those conditioned on robot demonstrations accept only one or two source types at limited scale. We present Any2Act, a foundation model for robot ICL. Our key insight is that a demonstration should be expressed in the camera layout and action space of the robot that performs it (the executor), not of whoever provided it (the demonstrator). This unified embodiment-grounded representation decouples learning to follow from admitting a new source. Any2Act learns to follow once, from robot-to-robot pairs that already take this form in 1.2M real trajectories spanning 10K tasks. Human videos, simulation, UMI, and other robots are then translated into this representation, and source-routed adapters trained on only a few hundred pairs each absorb the residual gap while the pretrained model stays frozen. For long horizons, a training-free tracker feeds only the demonstration window around the executor's progress, bounding the context and rolling back to redo failed steps. From a single demonstration, Any2Act achieves 57.5% success on unseen tasks without fine-tuning, exceeding VLAs fine-tuned on 50 demonstrations per task; other demonstrators retain 74-91% of the success achieved with the executor's own demonstrations.
#12 h=37

iWAM: Efficient Action Prediction from World Models without Explicit Future Prediction

2026-11-24 cs.ROcs.CVcs.LG 👤 M. Simchowitz (h=37)
Vansh Kapoor, Max Sobol Mark, Sreyas Venkataraman, Max Simchowitz, Aviral Kumar
Core Contributions
  • Questions a core assumption of world-action models: does a policy need a fully generated future frame, or only features that tell possible futures apart?
  • Uses the reversibility of flow-matching to invert training futures into latent noise, then learns a bidirectional flow map so that a single video-backbone forward pass yields informative features.
  • At inference it samples noise and runs one forward pass, removing the multi-step denoising that makes WAMs too slow for real-time control.
  • Recovers the generalization benefit of WAMs in simulation and on real robots at a fraction of the inference cost, a practical route to deployable video-pretrained policies.
Show abstract
World-action models (WAMs) build policies on pretrained video-generation models, on the premise that predicting how a scene evolves helps determine how to act. However, generating future scenes is expensive for real-time control. This motivates efficient proxies for future imagination, but how to train such proxies without sacrificing action-prediction quality remains unclear. An effective proxy need not generate a high-resolution frame; it only needs to provide enough information to distinguish between possible futures. This leads to the design principle behind iWAM: rather than denoising an entire future frame, can we condition the action policy on intermediate features from a single video-model evaluation? We show that the answer is yes. Exploiting the reversible dynamics of flow-matching models, we invert each training future into the corresponding latent noise vector. However, a single velocity evaluation at this latent does not necessarily yield informative features for action prediction. We therefore learn a bidirectional flow map that amortizes future-generation computation, enabling a single model evaluation to provide effective conditioning features. At inference, we sample a latent noise vector from the prior and condition the policy using just one video-backbone forward pass. Across simulated benchmarks and real robots, iWAM recovers the generalization benefits of WAMs while substantially reducing its inference cost.
#3 h=126

Simulator-as-Proxy World Model for Robot Manipulation

2026-11-24 cs.ROcs.CVcs.LG 👤 E. Xing (h=126)
Tingting Liao, Mingjia Huo, Hao Li, Zhengzhong Liu, Eric P. Xing
Core Contributions
  • Asks a question most simulator-conditioned video world models skip: does the generated video actually obey the proxy it is conditioned on? SimPWM makes the proxy explicit (robot CAD model plus object points moved by a parameter-free rule-based simulator) so it can be perturbed.
  • The arm follows the proxy well (robot IoU 0.75–0.78 vs ≤0.69 for end-to-end models), but rendering the robot alone already gives this. The simulated objects are what reduce missed pick-ups, from 54% to 38%.
  • The key diagnostic is freezing simulated objects while the arm moves. MiniMax-H3's lift rate drops from 42% to 10%, so it respects object state. LTX-2.3 drops only from 57% to 49%, meaning it largely infers grasps from arm motion and ignores the proxy.
  • Practical takeaway: because SimPWM returns the simulated state alongside the video, disagreement between the two becomes a built-in detector of when a world model is hallucinating.
Show abstract
Video world models for robots are increasingly conditioned on a simulated or rendered proxy of the scene, on the assumption that the video will show what the proxy shows. Testing this assumption requires a proxy whose state is explicit and can be perturbed. We present SimPWM (Simulator-as-Proxy World Model): given a calibrated camera, it represents, from the first frame alone, the robot by its 3D model and the objects the gripper may touch as 3D points; a rule-based simulator with no trained parameters moves them under the actions, and a video model renders the result. SimPWM returns the realistic video together with the simulated state. We then freeze the simulated objects while the arm moves as recorded and check whether the video still shows the object lifted. On DROID episodes from unseen setups, the answer differs for the arm and for the objects. With each of three base video models, the arm follows the proxy (robot IoU 0.75-0.78, against at most 0.69 for end-to-end models); rendering the robot alone already gives this. What the simulated objects add is the object state: against a robot-only proxy, they cut missed pick-ups from 54% to 38%, and the video follows them only with some base models: on rigid objects, freezing them lowers the lift rate of MiniMax-H3, fine-tuned on episodes whose simulation agrees with the real video, from 42% to 10%, but that of LTX-2.3, trained on the same episodes, only from 57% to 49%, as if it inferred the grasp from the arm motion; more than half of the remaining lifts of MiniMax-H3 are of cloth and rope, which the simulator carries rigidly. With any base model, the simulated state records what the simulation did; under perturbed actions, a video that disagrees with it reveals that the video ignores the simulated state.

Exploratory Midtraining for Robot Policies

2026-11-24 cs.ROcs.LG 👤 Dhruv Shah (h=34)
Kshitiz, Zheyuan Hu, Robyn Wu, Yizhou Li, Yunchu Zhang
Core Contributions
  • Proposes a “midtraining” stage that gives policies connected behavioral coverage: low-level skills that get from hard states back to states where progress is possible, which expert-only demos rarely contain.
  • Collects β-Data via adversarial teleoperation: one human reversibly perturbs the scene while the operator continues without resetting, producing many natural recoveries.
  • Segments are relabeled with local outcomes in language and the policy is conditioned on recent action history, so it learns to keep making progress after setbacks.
  • Gives better data-scaling laws, zero-shot generalization and use of extra rollout time on three real platforms, and needs fewer rollouts during online RL. Midtraining here acts as preparation for autonomous improvement.
Show abstract
Robot policies predominantly learn from human-collected data emphasizing competent behavior, limiting coverage of the low-level behaviors needed to support autonomous improvement. We study how a form of midtraining can be used to build coverage over diverse low-level behaviors that connect challenging states of the robot and its environment to states from which further progress is possible ("connected behavioral coverage"). We also examine how this coverage supports downstream adaptation and online improvement. To this end, we introduce $β$-Data, a dataset collected via adversarial teleoperation, where a human adversary reversibly perturbs the environment while a human operator continues toward completion without resetting. We relabel trajectory segments with local outcomes in natural language and finetune a pretrained policy conditioned on recent action history. The resulting policy exhibits sustained use of more low-level behaviors. Across manipulation tasks in simulation and on three real-robot platforms, our approach yields more favorable data scaling laws, better zero-shot generalization, and more effective use of additional rollout time than training on standard demonstrations. During online finetuning, these policies need fewer rollouts to collect successful experience and reach higher success rates, suggesting that midtraining can prepare robots for both more robust task execution and continued autonomous improvement. Our project page and robot videos are available at https://kshitiz-1225.github.io/exploratory-midtraining-robot-policies/.

KeyMemVLA: Subtask-Guided Key Memory for Long-Horizon Robotic Manipulation

2026-11-24 cs.RO 👤 Zhi-Kun She (h=21)
Yongwei Zhang, Tao Chen, Sicheng Ma, Yuanzhe Xing, Pengwei Zhang
Core Contributions
  • Addresses long-horizon tasks where needed evidence is no longer visible by deciding what to remember, rather than storing a fixed history window.
  • Supervises memory writes with keyframes from a two-level subtask annotation: subtasks that signal progress or hold decision-relevant evidence.
  • One shared VLM backbone predicts the current subtask, the action and future memory-write positions; the subtask prediction then conditions the action.
  • Introduces KMBench and outperforms baselines on RMBench, RoboTwin-MeM, KMBench and real tasks, with ablations showing both key memory and explicit progress semantics are needed.
Show abstract
Memory-dependent long-horizon manipulation requires vision-language-action (VLA) policies to infer task progress and recall evidence absent from current observations. Under a limited memory budget, the challenge is to retain history that resolves stage ambiguity and supports subsequent decisions. We present KeyMemVLA, a subtask-guided key memory framework based on progress indication and decision-evidence retention. A two-level annotation process selects subtasks satisfying either criterion and labels their final frames as keyframes to supervise online memory writes. A shared vision-language backbone jointly learns subtask, action, and keyframe prediction. At inference, the policy first predicts the current subtask from the instruction, visual context, and memory, then uses this semantic condition to predict actions and future memory-write positions. Actual observations are retained at the selected times during execution. We introduce KMBench to evaluate active memory retention for decisions across subtasks. Evaluations on RMBench-$M(n)$, RoboTwin-MeM, KMBench, and real-world manipulation tasks show higher average success rates than the compared baselines. Ablation studies and representation analysis support the roles of key memory and explicit task-progress semantics. These results highlight the value of coupling sparse key memory with online progress inference for history-dependent control.

What Does a Skill Vocabulary Cost? A Closed-Loop Decomposition over Demonstrated Motions

2026-11-24 cs.ROcs.LG 👤 Rudolf Lioutikov (h=26)
Maximilian Xiling Li, Nils Blank, Paul Mattes, Pankhuri Vanjani, Rudolf Lioutikov
Core Contributions
  • Moves the skill vocabulary outside the policy: Movement Prototype Quantization snaps each predicted action chunk to the nearest demonstrated motion and applies a bounded correction, with no trained parameters.
  • Across six architectures, including a 7B VLA, the wrapped policy is statistically equivalent to the original, so quantization to a finite library costs nothing.
  • This yields a reference baseline for attribution. On LIBERO-90, choosing the skill costs at most 2 points, while selectors' own placement and correction explain up to 12 points of gap.
  • Offline reconstruction metrics fail to predict these closed-loop differences and misjudge library size, a warning about how skill-based methods are usually evaluated.
Show abstract
Discrete skill policies execute motions drawn from a finite vocabulary. In most current methods, this vocabulary is a latent codebook learned jointly with a selector and a decoder. When such a policy fails in closed loop, the failure cannot be traced to the vocabulary, to the choice of skill or to its execution. We present Movement Prototype Quantization (MPQ), which places a library of demonstrated motions after a trained policy instead of inside it. MPQ replaces each predicted action chunk with its nearest library element, corrects that element toward the prediction within a bound, and trains no parameters. Across six architectures, including a 7B vision-language-action model, the wrapped policy is statistically equivalent to the original. A reference policy executed through the library is therefore a baseline for any selector over the same library. Substituting a selector's components one at a time attributes its shortfall to them. On LIBERO-90, with selectors on three backbones, quantization costs nothing and the choice of skill costs at most 2 points under the reference's placement and correction. The selectors' own placement and correction account for nearly all of the remaining gap, up to 12 points. Offline reconstruction predicts none of these differences and misjudges library size, design and transfer across benchmark suites. Project page: https://intuitive-robots.github.io/mpq.

PyRUA-Dex: Learning Programmatic Policies for Dexterous Bimanual Manipulation with Coding Agents

2026-11-24 cs.RO 👤 En-Ze Xie (h=66)
Feixiang Ruan, Guodong Lv, Kewei Zhang, Wenbo Huang, Yintianrun Zhang
Core Contributions
  • Removes demonstrations from dexterous bimanual learning entirely: coding agents write the policy as a Python program, avoiding imprecise video retargeting and force-blind teleoperation.
  • Two-stage process: parallel agents explore skills and compositions, a distilling agent condenses successes into a reusable task library and single program, and then refinement under randomized layouts adds robustness.
  • Beats every trained policy on two benchmarks: 72.9 on Bench2Dex and 81.0% success on DexJoCo under full randomization.
  • On real 22-DoF bimanual hands it plays piano and writes calligraphy; learning a new piano piece from sheet music takes about 30 minutes, which suggests program synthesis can be a fast path to new dexterous skills.
Show abstract
Learning for dexterous manipulation is limited by the scarcity of high-quality demonstrations: retargeting from human video is imprecise, teleoperation gives no force feedback to the operator, and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale up. We present PyRUA-Dex, a framework that enables coding agents to develop dexterous manipulation through programmatic policy, without the need of demonstrations. The programmatic policy is developed in two stages: during exploration, multiple agents explore in parallel for task skills and ways to compose them, and a distilling agent condenses the successful attempts into a task library and one program. During refinement, this program is improved through randomized layouts for enhanced robustness and execution efficiency. In simulated environments, PyRUA-Dex achieves SOTA on Bench2Dex with a score of 72.9 and reaches 81.0% success on DexJoCo under full randomization, ahead of every trained policy on both benchmarks. In real world experiments on a bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, PyRUA-Dex learns to play piano, write calligraphy, and other tasks efficiently: learning a new piece from sheet music takes as little as 30 minutes. These results suggest that PyRUA-Dex is an effective framework for developing dexterous manipulation policies with coding agents in the absence of high quality dexterous manipulation demonstrations.

RoboHarness-RL: Agentic Reinforcement Learning for VLM-Based Robot Manipulation

2026-11-24 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Chun-Hua Shen (h=21)
Liyang Li, Mingyu Liu, Xiaoman Li, Zhiyue Zhao, Jin-Chuan Shi
Core Contributions
  • Shows that compact open-weight VLMs can be trained with multi-turn RL to drive robots through tool calls (perception, planning, control), narrowing the gap to frontier-model harnesses without a VLA or world model.
  • LiteHarness keeps multi-turn contexts compact with hierarchical memory, which is what makes RL over long tool-use episodes affordable.
  • With Qwen3.5-9B, RL lifts success from 31.2% to 44.0% on RoboTwin 2.0, from 57.8% to 73.3% on LIBERO-Pro, and from 26.4% to 38.0% on unseen randomized scenes.
  • Behaviorally, RL cuts repetitive tool calls by up to 4× and improves recovery from negative feedback, so it fixes how the agent uses tools, not just its outcomes.
Show abstract
Agentic harnesses enable frontier vision-language models (VLMs) to manipulate robots through perception, planning, and control tools, yet achieving comparable performance with compact open-weight VLMs remains challenging. We present RoboHarness-RL, an agentic reinforcement learning framework for improving VLM-based robot manipulation through tool calls. Starting from supervised fine-tuning on successful teacher demonstrations, the VLM further improves through multi-turn reinforcement learning with task-outcome rewards, without relying on vision-language-action models or world action models. To support efficient multi-turn training, we develop LiteHarness, which uses hierarchical memory to keep interaction contexts compact while preserving task performance. With Qwen3.5-9B, RL raises success from 31.2% to 44.0% on RoboTwin 2.0 and from 57.8% to 73.3% on LIBERO-Pro; these gains extend to randomized RoboTwin scenes unseen during training (26.4% to 38.0%). Behavioral analysis further shows that RL reduces repetitive-call rates by up to $4\times$, shortens successful executions, and improves recovery from negative feedback. Project page: https://aim-uofa.github.io/RoboHarness-RL/

Atomic Policy: Composing Atomic Manipulation Skills with Agentic Reasoning

2026-11-24 cs.RO 👤 Yu She (h=22)

Core Contributions
  • Bridges VLM reasoning and visuomotor control: a VLM estimates symbolic state and goal, a PDDL solver plans over learned atomic skills, and an agentic harness steers and verifies each one.

Long-horizon manipulation requires robots to translate task intent into physical interactions as the scene changes. RoboRecast: Intent-Preserving Demonstration Augmentation via Diagnosis-Driven Optimization

2026-11-24 cs.ROcs.AI 👤 Xiao-Wei Zhou (h=62)
Yushi Long, Juzhan Xu, Haibin Huang, Chi Zhang, Yin Chen
Core Contributions
  • Targets a known failure of simulation-based demo augmentation: replaying a source motion in a new layout, object or robot often produces actions that are physically infeasible or no longer accomplish the task.
  • Rather than retargeting trajectories blindly, an agent preserves the interactions the task requires and jointly edits the scene and robot motion so those interactions remain executable.
  • When an interaction fails in simulation, the agent diagnoses the cause, revises the relevant parameter, and tests the fix. This diagnose-and-repair loop is what makes the augmentation robust across embodiments.
  • Works from both human and robot source demos, improves real single-arm and simulated bimanual policies, and supports new objects or embodiments even with zero target-configuration demos.
Show abstract
Collecting diverse real-world manipulation demonstrations is costly, motivating the use of simulation to augment existing demonstrations for robot policy learning. When objects, layouts, or robots change, transferring motions from a source demonstration can produce behavior that is semantically implausible or physically infeasible in the new setting. We introduce RoboRecast, an agentic optimization framework that preserves the interactions required by the source task while jointly optimizing the simulated scene and robot motion to make those interactions executable in the target setting. When an interaction fails, the agent uses simulation feedback to diagnose the cause, revise the relevant scene or motion parameters, and test whether the revision resolves the failure. This flexibility allows RoboRecast to generate synthetic data from both human and robot demonstrations and to adapt them to new layouts, objects, and robot embodiments. When target demonstrations are available, adding RoboRecast data improves policy success on both real-world single-arm and simulated bimanual tasks. Even without demonstrations in the target configuration, RoboRecast adapts source demonstrations to support policy learning with new objects or robot embodiments.

EmbodiedStudio: A Task-Driven Simulation Generation Platform for Embodied Learning and Evaluation

2026-11-24 cs.RO 👤 Xu-Bo Yang (h=25)
Yang Zhao, Xubo Yang
Core Contributions
  • Generates simulation environments for a given task by jointly writing scene, task, robot-behavior and scoring-rubric programs, then debugging them under closed-loop simulation and independent review.
  • Tackles two specific gaps: scenes that look right but are physically unstable or do not support the task, and generated scoring rubrics that do not faithfully implement the intended success condition.
  • The debugging trace becomes training data for both environment construction and policy learning, and environments are frozen per revision for reproducible evaluation.
  • Improves task readiness, rubric faithfulness and physical stability over prior generators.
Show abstract
Training and evaluating robots across diverse tasks requires a supply of simulation environments suited to those tasks. While recent scene and task generators offer a promising way to expand this supply, delivering a usable task environment still faces two practical gaps: generated scenes that look plausible in appearance often fail to support the intended task interactions or remain physically stable in simulation, and generated scoring programs frequently attempt task conditions without implementing them faithfully. We present EmbodiedStudio, a task-driven simulation generation platform that bridges these gaps by jointly authoring scene, task, robot-behavior, and rubric programs under closed-loop simulation feedback and independent review. Crucially, as the coding agent iteratively debugs scenes, behaviors, and stateful rubrics in simulation, this closed-loop process natively yields both forward construction experience for learning to build environments and physical execution experience for policy learning, all bound to frozen environment revisions for repeatable evaluation. Across scene generation, rubric evaluation, ablations, and learning studies, EmbodiedStudio substantially improves task readiness and rubric faithfulness while improving physical stability and retaining actionable supervision signals for layout authoring and policy adaptation.

Design, Control, and Teleoperation of a Dexterous Robotic Hand with Soft-Rigid Hybrid Fingers

2026-11-24 cs.RO 👤 D. Rus (h=139)
Chao Liu, Wil J. Norton, Brandon Romero, Daniela Rus
Core Contributions
  • Scales the soft-rigid hybrid finger (compliant flexures around a rigid skeleton) to a full 12-DoF tendon-driven five-finger hand. The rigid core is what lets simple linear controllers work, sidestepping the modelling burden that usually makes soft hands hard to control.
  • Covers 28 of 33 (84.8%) human grasp postures from the standard grasp taxonomy, so compliance does not come at the cost of grasp coverage.
  • Joint extension reaches 1432°/s, roughly twice the ~700°/s of the equivalent human joint, showing that compliant hands need not be slow.
  • Teleoperated demos on thin deformables (picking up a dollar bill, folding cloth) highlight where passive compliance beats rigid hands: tasks where contact must be gentle and conform to the surface.
