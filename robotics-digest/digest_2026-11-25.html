Research Landscape

The strongest current in this batch is making agentic and self-improving loops trustworthy about their own evidence. Skeptic (#5) finds that LLM skill-discovery agents abandon solvable tasks by misreading failed programs as proof of impossibility, and fixes it by grounding diagnosis in measured action effects. Memory Is Not Ground Truth (#22) reaches the mirror-image conclusion for navigation: repeated VLM misses on a Spot robot are correlated and should demote a memory without declaring the object absent. SealedLoop (#29) and TRIAGE (#30), from the same group, carry the idea up to the training loop and deployment decision — an in-loop judge accepted 65 of 196 failures, and gates on latency removed the two best-scoring checkpoints, neither of which could finish a closed-loop trip. Meanwhile the loops themselves are getting stronger: RoboEvo² (#17) lifts LIBERO-PRO standalone success from 11.0% to 84.1% by co-evolving a coding-agent harness and a VLA, WISER-RL (#14) reaches 100% from one demonstration, and SOLVE (#23) shows sparse keyposes make LLM agents 12× cheaper. The shared lesson is that verified promotion, sealed judges and reversible beliefs are becoming as important as the learner.

A second theme is the gap between semantic and metric understanding. BOP-Refer (#4) shows frontier VLMs reaching 72.6% in 2D grounding but 12.4% in 3D, failing mostly on depth scale; QK-Bench (#26) finds the best MLLM at 41.2% on quantitative kinematics; DTU-Bench (#18) shows VLAs drop from 95.8% to 1.6% when the target object changes mid-task. The most robust systems in the batch route around this weakness by giving language a narrow role: Ask to Land (#16) passes only a segmentation mask to its controller, MERLoc (#3) retrieves references by pose rather than appearance, SOLVE grounds keyposes with explicit depth queries, and Aegis-VLA (#25) gates world-model corrections by predicted uncertainty. Image-goal quadrotor navigation (#1) takes the opposite route, learning metric state implicitly through recurrence and distillation to fly from pixels to thrust at 84% real-world success.

Third, feasibility and safety by construction links the planning papers with the data-generation ones. DFLAT (#19) diffuses in differentially flat space so every sample is dynamically feasible, GAO (#10) turns acceleration obstacles into exact affine QP constraints that scale to 1,000 agents, and recoverability editing (#7) intervenes only where a driving policy can still return to safety (48% fewer incidents). On the data side, DemoFoundry (#11), DataForgeVLA (#28) and Lab2Sim (#27) all use agents or generative video for appearance while keeping physics or verification in charge of actions, and DataForgeVLA's 80% vs 90% real-data comparison suggests synthetic demonstrations are nearing parity for simple tasks. Read together, the batch suggests the field is converging on hybrid systems: learned components for breadth, with structured checks — physics, geometry, sealed evaluation — deciding what is actually kept.

SOLVE: Sparse, Ordered Keyposes Make LLM Agents Versatile and Efficient Robot Manipulator

2026-11-25 cs.ROcs.CV 👤 Yali Li (h=33)
Xiang Li, Hongyu Zhou, Ya-Li Li, Tao Li, Rui Zhang
Core Contributions
  • Has a multimodal LLM write a sparse, ordered list of 6-DoF end-effector keyposes per call (with a motion planner filling in between), instead of dense step-by-step actions or fixed tool APIs.
  • Motivated by data: most DROID teleoperation demos compress to a few keyposes, so this granularity matches how humans actually manipulate.
  • Grounds keyposes with sparse depth queries from noisy sources (triangulation, stereo, mono depth) and reflects on rollouts to distill a few per-task rules, improving without training.
  • State of the art on RoboDojo (74.5%) and RoboLab (84.5%) at $1.7 per episode — 12× cheaper than dense-control agents — and 88% (75/85) on 16 zero-shot real tasks.
Show abstract
LLM agents have made remarkable progress in robot manipulation, yet they suffer from expensive inference, dense step-by-step control, and tool bottlenecks. We introduce SOLVE, a training-free, versatile and efficient agent policy: a multimodal LLM agent writes a sparse, ordered sequence of 6-DoF end-effector keyposes per call, observes the result, and writes the next, while a motion planner interpolates between them. The agent decides the number and spacing of keyposes for both fast long-range motion and fine-grained manipulation. This granularity matches the sparsity of teleoperated manipulation: most demonstrations in DROID can be compressed into a few keyposes instead of dense actions. SOLVE grounds keyposes with sparse depth queries answered by noisy sources, such as multi-view triangulation, stereo, or monocular depth, and copes with that noise itself. It also improves from execution feedback, reflecting on its own rollouts and distilling a few rules per task. SOLVE reaches state-of-the-art performance of 74.5% on RoboDojo and 84.5% on RoboLab, with only $1.7 per episode on average, 12x less than previous dense-control agent policies. On a real robot, the same agent achieves an 88% success rate (75 of 85 episodes) over 16 challenging zero-shot tasks. Our project page is at https://serenos.github.io/solve-website.
#17 h=49

RoboEvo$^2$: Recursive Robot Self-Improvement via Harness-Policy Co-Evolution

2026-11-25 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CV 👤 K. Kosuge (h=49)
Chenzhi Liu, Jiehong Lin, Wei Huang, Xuzhao Huang, Haoyuan Li
Core Contributions
  • Co-evolves an agentic 'harness' (coding agent + skills + memory) and a VLA policy: policy failures drive the harness to find solutions, and verified harness trajectories train the policy.
  • Policy-to-Harness search mixes Code, Code+ICL and Code+Policy routes, consolidating what works into reusable skills and routing strategies, so expensive deliberation is spent only where the policy fails.
  • Counterfactual Preference Distillation trains the policy to prefer successful corrections over failures from the same start state — a sharper signal than plain imitation of successes.
  • With no extra human demos, standalone success on LIBERO-PRO rises from 11.0% to 84.1% (hybrid 30.4% → 90.5%), and hybrid success reaches 95.0% on a Unitree G1-D.
Show abstract
Robots should adapt to unfamiliar environments and acquire new skills through interaction, then execute those skills efficiently with practice. Vision-language-action policies provide efficient control, but often need additional human demonstrations to handle unfamiliar settings; reasoning agents can discover solutions using broad prior knowledge and robot-control APIs, but repeated deliberation and repair are costly. Inspired by human learning through reasoning and practice, we propose RoboEvo$^2$, a framework for recursive harness-policy co-evolution. In Policy-to-Harness (P2H) evolution, policy failures guide a coding agent to search complementary Code, Code+ICL, and Code+Policy routes, consolidating verified solutions into reusable skills, routing strategies, and experience memory. In Harness-to-Policy (H2P) evolution, verified trajectories provide supervised training, while Counterfactual Preference Distillation (CPD) teaches the policy to favor successful corrections over failures from the same starting state. The improved policy provides stronger motor skills for subsequent search, while its remaining failures redirect harness evolution, closing the reciprocal loop. Through recursive co-evolution without additional human demonstrations, RoboEvo$^2$ improves standalone success from 11.0% to 84.1% and hybrid success from 30.4% to 90.5% on LIBERO-PRO, with similar gains and reduced agentic costs on RoboTwin C2R. Further experiments demonstrate autonomous task acquisition, cross-embodiment harness transfer, and 95.0% hybrid success on the Unitree G1-D. Our models and code will be publicly released.
#5 h=79

Skeptic: Grounding Failure Diagnosis for Robot Skill Discovery

2026-11-25 cs.RO 👤 Cees G. M. Snoek (h=79)
Wenfang Sun, Jianghao Deng, Wenzhe Yin, Yingjun Du, Cees G. M. Snoek
Core Contributions
  • Identifies a specific failure of LLM skill-discovery agents: concluding a task is 'physically impossible' after a failed program and stopping with revision budget left.
  • Replaces the agent's free-form diagnosis with a grounded event log built from depth and proprioception that reports measured action effects and object end states, separating what was observed from what is inferred.
  • Tightens skill-library admission with 'verified promotion': a repair must both reproduce independently and come from a program that actually succeeded in development, preventing lucky or wrong lessons from polluting shared skills.
  • Training-free: false impossibility judgments drop from 19.5 to 8.5 per campaign, and held-out success rises by 14.9 pp on 80 LIBERO-Pro tasks and 11.7 pp on Robosuite with the same budget and frozen Qwen3.8-27B.
Show abstract
Robot agents discover reusable skills by revising programs from execution feedback and retaining experience for later tasks. This process can fail when an agent treats an unsuccessful program as evidence that the task is physically impossible, ending exploration with revision budget remaining. We study this failure in Qwen3.8-27B and examine two decisions that shape skill discovery: I) how execution failures are diagnosed, and II) which lessons enter the shared skill library. Failure diagnosis requires the agent to reconstruct physical action effects from multimodal traces, while independent reproduction of a proposed repair does not require adequate task performance from its source program. We introduce Skeptic, a training-free protocol that uses measured action outcomes to guide repair and measured task success to govern skill admission. A grounded event log reports action effects and object end states from depth images and proprioception, separating sensor observations from inferred diagnoses. Verified promotion adds a development-success requirement on the source program to the existing independent reproduction check. Across 60 audited tasks, the mean number of tasks with false impossibility judgments decreases from 19.5 to 8.5 per campaign. On 80 LIBERO-Pro tasks and seven Robosuite tasks, Skeptic improves held-out success by 14.9 and 11.7 percentage points, with frozen model weights and the same program-revision budget.
#14 h=57

WISER-RL: A Wake-Sleep Learning Mechanism for Robot Reinforcement Learning from One Demonstration

2026-11-25 cs.RO 👤 Gitta Kutyniok (h=57)
Kwunhang Wong, Yang Bai, Qingyang Bao, Liudi Yang, Chenzhi Liu
Core Contributions
  • Targets the one-demo regime where RL starves for reward: a 'wake' phase lets a frozen GPT controller take over when diffusion-policy samples disagree (GPTDAgger), turning assisted practice into successful episodes.
  • A 'sleep' phase applies Conflict-Aware Experience Reweighting, comparing visually matched successes and down-weighting action windows that conflict and are farther from success — a cleaner learning signal than naive imitation of all successes.
  • Combines imitation of reweighted successes with offline advantage-guided RL on successes and failures, then a final online PPO stage to remove the need for the assistant.
  • The resulting autonomous policy hits 100% on each of six LIBERO tasks and on two real-robot tasks, from a single human demonstration.
Show abstract
A robot learning from one human demonstration may rarely succeed on its own, leaving reinforcement learning (RL) with little reward feedback. We introduce WISER-RL ({W}ake--sleep policy improvement loop, {I}mitation, and {S}elective {E}xperience {R}eplay), a composite reinforcement learning framework that turns assisted practice into autonomous control. During wake, GPTDAgger monitors disagreement among actions sampled by a diffusion policy. When disagreement crosses a threshold, a frozen GPT controller uses retrieved experience to continue the episode. During sleep, Conflict-Aware Experience Reweighting (CAER) compares visually matched successful trajectories and downweights conflicting action windows with more steps remaining to success. The policy imitates the reweighted successes and learns from both successes and failures through offline advantage-guided RL before collecting the next round. After final online PPO refinement, the autonomous policy succeeded in 100% evaluations on each of six LIBERO tasks and 100% trials on each of two real-robot tasks.

Keep or Roll Back? Out-of-Loop Judges for Self-Improving VLA and End-to-End Embodied Policies

2026-11-25 cs.AIcs.CVcs.RO 👤 Si-Kai Chen (h=29)
Zilin Huang, Zhengyang Wan, Kangan Qian, Zihao Sheng, Sikai Chen
Core Contributions
  • Points out that self-improving embodied policies typically use an in-loop judge, so the evidence for 'improvement' is not independent of the policy being improved.
  • SealedLoop adds a sealed judge the improver cannot see and a sequential test that bounds the probability of keeping a worse policy.
  • Separates two sources of false gain — judge error vs. transfer gap — and shows both are large: a success classifier accepted 65 of 196 failures, and driving gains on loop frames reversed in an unseen city.
  • With the sealed test deciding, a driving loop ends better than with its own critic (0.674 vs 0.649 PDMS, from 0.638), accepting 11/12 real gains and 0/59 losses; tested also on a full-scale vehicle.
Show abstract
Self-improvement is now a practical recipe for embodied policies: a policy collects experience, is retrained, and is kept when a judge rates it higher. However, the judge is usually trained or chosen inside the same loop, so its evidence is not independent of the policy. We introduce SealedLoop, an evaluation framework that adds a sealed judge: an evaluator outside the loop that the improver cannot see or optimize against. A new policy is kept only when a sequential test on the sealed judge confirms the gain, with a bounded probability of keeping a worse one. Running the same loop under each judge, we separate two sources of a false gain, which we call judge error (the loop's judge is wrong on its own data) and transfer gap (a real gain there does not carry over to unseen conditions). We evaluate on LIBERO manipulation, NAVSIM driving, and a full-scale real vehicle. In manipulation, a success classifier accepted 65 of 196 failures in one generation. In driving, policies that gain on the loop's own frames lose in an unseen city, so even an exact in-loop judge would keep 8/15 of them. When the sealed test decides, a driving loop built to improve ends with a better policy than under its own critic (0.674 against 0.649 PDMS, from 0.638), and on driving pairs with known sealed gain the test accepted 11 of 12 real gains and none of 59 losses. In a real-world field experiment on a published improvement step, the reinforced version drove further into a curve than the version it started from in all 6 blind pairs, though neither completed it. These results show that evidence from inside the loop, however accurate, is not enough to decide whether a policy has improved. SealedLoop offers a foundation for trustworthy self-improvement of embodied policies, in which progress is judged by evidence the improving system cannot influence.

ELMO: Emergent Representations In Imitation Learning Via Dynamic Hierarchical Action Chunking

2026-11-25 cs.RO 👤 Igor Gilitschenski (h=41)
Jasper Gerigk, Yang Xu, Nicholas Rhinehart, Igor Gilitschenski
Core Contributions
  • Argues MSE action regression hides the phase structure of demonstrations (reach, grasp, lift), and that existing latent action models learn context-blind motion codes like 'move down' rather than intent.
  • Trains a VLA planner and an observation-conditioned decoder end-to-end over dynamically sized action chunks, so the latent codes become grounded in the current scene and task — no separate pretraining stage.
  • Introduces Blocks, a shortcut-free sim + real benchmark designed so policies cannot succeed by memorizing spurious cues.
  • +9.6 pp on LIBERO-Plus and +0.75 average task length on CALVIN; on Blocks it reaches 38.6% while all baselines stay below 5%.
Show abstract
Robot trajectories naturally decompose into variable-length phases with distinct semantic intents, such as reaching, grasping, and lifting. However, training policies to minimize MSE treats all actions as equal, obscuring their semantic structure. While latent action models attempt to create a structured prediction target by compressing action chunks into latent representations, existing approaches rely on two-stage training and context-blind decoders. Consequently, their representations are context-agnostic motion codes (e.g., "move down") which fail to capture behavioural intent (e.g., "pick up the block"). To overcome these bottlenecks, we introduce the Emergent Latent-action MOdel (ELMO), an end-to-end framework that encodes robot trajectories as grounded semantic codes -- representations that ground task-level behavioural intent in the current environment. ELMO jointly trains a VLA planner and an observation-conditioned decoder over dynamically chunked trajectories, learning a semantically grounded action space without separate pretraining. Across standard benchmarks, ELMO outperforms comparable prior methods by 9.6 percentage points on LIBERO-Plus and by 0.75 in average task length on CALVIN. Furthermore, on Blocks -- our shortcut-free simulation and real-world generalization benchmark -- ELMO reaches a 38.6% success rate while all baselines remain below 5%, demonstrating its ability to capture the semantic structure of demonstrations. Website: https://uoft-isl.github.io/elmo.github.io/

Aegis-VLA: Reliability-Gated Action Refinement via a Trajectory World Model for Dynamic Object Manipulation

2026-11-25 cs.ROcs.AI 👤 Jian-Hua Han (h=31)
Lennox Wei, Jiaqi Yao, Shixin Sun, Yuanzheng Zhang, Jianhua Han
Core Contributions
  • Separates two questions most world-model-guided policies conflate: what the future will look like, and whether changing the action is actually beneficial.
  • A Trajectory World Model mixes kinematic extrapolation with learned residual dynamics and outputs positional uncertainty, which is then used to decide how much to trust predictions.
  • A learned Reliability Gate admits bounded corrections only when they are expected to improve on the base action; rejected corrections fall back to the base VLA, making the add-on fail-safe.
  • With only ~2.45M trainable parameters, it reaches 56.72% on the DOM benchmark (1,800 trials), +11.28 pp over a reproduced DynamicVLA.
Show abstract
Dynamic object manipulation requires maintaining spatial and temporal alignment with targets that continue to move during inference and execution. Although future prediction can inform action correction, an accurate forecast does not establish whether modifying an existing action would be beneficial. We present Aegis-VLA, a world-model-guided vision-language-action (VLA) framework that generates predictive action corrections and separately determines whether and how strongly to apply them. A Trajectory World Model combines kinematic extrapolation with learned residual dynamics to predict object motion and estimate positional uncertainty. Discrepancy-Guided Action Refinement uses differences between history-only and base-action-conditioned end-effector forecasts to propose bounded arm corrections. A Reliability Gate learns when to admit corrections based on demonstration-relative improvements over base actions, and regulates their application during inference using predictive uncertainty and anticipated object--end-effector proximity. Rejected corrections preserve the corresponding base actions. Aegis-VLA introduces only approximately 2.45M trainable parameters. On the simulated Dynamic Object Manipulation (DOM) benchmark, Aegis-VLA achieves 56.72\% success over 1,800 evaluation trials, exceeding our reproduced DynamicVLA baseline of 45.44\% by 11.28 percentage points.

Dynamic Task Update Benchmark (DTU-Bench): Evaluating Runtime Task Adaptation in Robotic Manipulation

2026-11-25 cs.RO 👤 D. Kulić (h=47)
Yanran Jiang, Cécile Paris, Dana Kulić, Leimin Tian
Core Contributions
  • Tests something fixed-instruction benchmarks ignore: whether VLAs can follow a changed instruction mid-execution, across 40 revisions, 7 update types and 3 execution stages on LIBERO.
  • The gap is dramatic: 13 VLA configurations average 95.8% on original tasks but just 1.6% when the target object changes during execution.
  • Diagnostics show policies often finish the original task first and that responses depend on the robot's physical state (e.g., already grasping), suggesting instruction conditioning is weak relative to visual-proprioceptive momentum.
  • A simple scripted 'release and retract' reset raises success from 2% to 38% (π0.5) and 8% to 48% (DM0.5), but causes unwanted object motion — highlighting the need for principled task-switching.
Show abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies perform well on fixed-instruction robotic manipulation benchmarks, but their ability to adapt to instruction changes during execution remains understudied. We introduce the Dynamic Task Update Benchmark (DTU-Bench), evaluating 40 task revisions across seven update types and three execution stages using LIBERO, a simulated robotic manipulation benchmark. We compare performance when a new instruction is given before execution starts versus during execution, and examine whether the new task instructions are followed. Across 13 representative VLA configurations, average task success is 95.8% on the original object-manipulation tasks, compared with only 1.6% when the instruction changes which object to manipulate during execution. Controlled diagnostics show that policies often complete the original task before the updated one, and that their responses depend on the robot's physical state. We further test whether a simple scripted controller that releases the grasped object and moves the arm back helps robots respond to instruction changes. This improves success from 2% to 38% for π0.5 and from 8% to 48% for DM0.5, but can cause unwanted object motion. These findings reveal limitations that fixed-instruction benchmarks and task-success metrics alone can overlook in evaluating robotic manipulation. Project page: https://dtu-bench.github.io/

Triage Before Fine-Tuning: Feasibility-Gated Selection and Monitored Adaptation of Embodied Foundation Models for a Fixed Physical Platform

2026-11-25 cs.ROcs.AIcs.CV 👤 Si-Kai Chen (h=29)
Zilin Huang, Zhengyang Wan, Zihao Sheng, Boyue Wang, Ruolin Yang
Core Contributions
  • Reframes foundation-model deployment as triage: decompose a checkpoint's loss on a fixed platform into sensing, latency and distribution gaps, since only the last can be fixed by fine-tuning.
  • Five-stage procedure with hard gates on sensor contract and end-to-end latency that no benchmark score can override, followed by probes on ten minutes of local logs and monitored, closed-loop-checked fine-tuning.
  • Quantifies why gates matter: a deadline below a leading model's latency causes collisions on 80–90% of routes, and losing field of view shifts plans 2.2–5.7× more than losing resolution.
  • On a real vehicle, the latency gate removed the two top-scoring checkpoints (neither completed a trip) and the closed-loop check withheld a fine-tuned model that only improved offline; the selected model passed 4 of 5 trips.
Show abstract
Embodied foundation models are increasingly deployed by selection: a practitioner picks a public checkpoint by benchmark rank and fine-tunes it on local data. The platform is fixed before the model is chosen, with little or no local data. We ask what such a checkpoint loses on a fixed platform and which part of the loss local data can buy back. We split the gap by what can repair it. A sensing gap, information the sensors do not capture, cannot be closed with their data; a latency gap, output arriving after the control loop needs it, belongs to the loop, not the weights; only a distribution gap is fine-tuning's to repair. TRIAGE turns this into feasibility-gated selection and monitored adaptation in five stages: gates on the sensor contract and on end-to-end latency that no score can override, probes on ten minutes of local logs, a ranking by target-relevant score among the candidates that pass, and fine-tuning that is monitored and checked in closed loop before release. On driving benchmarks, a deadline below a leading closed-loop model's latency makes it collide on 80 to 90% of routes, and losing field of view moves plans 2.2 to 5.7 times more than losing resolution at equal pixels. Local frames close 75% of a stability gap but raise perturbation sensitivity at small budgets, and picking by score violates a gate in two of four deployment profiles. On a full-scale vehicle, the factory camera failed the sensing gate and an added camera repaired it. The latency gate removed the two highest-scoring candidates, neither of which completed a closed-loop trip. The selected model passed acceptance in 4 of 5 closed-loop trips, and the closed-loop check withheld a fine-tuned model that had improved offline. TRIAGE lets organizations that cannot train foundation models from scratch find out whether a public checkpoint can run on the robot or vehicle they own, and what to fix if not.

DemoFoundry: Toward Scalable Photorealistic Robot Data with Coding Agents and Generative Rendering

2026-11-25 cs.ROcs.AI 👤 K. Jia (h=63)
Ruixiang Wang, Xinyue Yao, Xuanning Zhou, Yueci Deng, Yongyi Su
Core Contributions
  • Pairs physics simulation (for correct actions) with a learned video renderer (for photorealism), sidestepping both the sim visual gap and the action-label problem of pure video generation.
  • Coding agents do double duty: they build aligned real/proxy video pairs from existing episodes to train the renderer, and they write executable interaction programs for new tasks from a scene reference and instruction.
  • No target-task human demonstrations are needed; automated execution checks filter trajectories before rendering, keeping the data both photorealistic and physically grounded.
  • Policies trained on the generated demonstrations beat raw-simulation training and approach those trained on an equal number of real demos.
Show abstract
Scaling robot learning requires demonstrations that combine realistic visual observations with reliable action supervision. Simulation-based synthesis faces a substantial visual gap relative to real environments, while video-only generation struggles to maintain physically consistent interactions and provide aligned action labels. We present DemoFoundry, an automated framework that couples coding agents with generative rendering to synthesize photorealistic and physically grounded robot demonstrations. Coding agents both construct aligned real--proxy video pairs from existing robot episodes to train a video renderer and write executable interaction programs to generate new demonstrations. Given a scene reference and task instruction, an agent reconstructs task-relevant geometry and writes a control program without requiring target-task human demonstrations. Program execution in a physics simulator produces proxy videos with aligned robot states and actions. The video renderer translates these proxy videos into scene-consistent, photorealistic observations, reducing the need for detailed appearance modeling in each scene. Automated task-execution checks select successful trajectories for rendering and policy learning. Experiments show improved visual quality and action--video consistency. Policies trained on the generated demonstrations outperform raw-simulation training and approach the performance of training on an equal number of real demonstrations.

DataForgeVLA: Fully Synthetic Manipulation Demonstrations for Vision-Language-Action Training

2026-11-25 cs.RO 👤 D. Tsetserukou (h=29)
Sergei Kurchev, Iaroslav Kolomiets, Filipp Maksimov, Elizaveta Semenyakina, Miguel Altamirano Cabrera
Core Contributions
  • Releases 340 fully synthetic manipulation demos (32,340 frames, LeRobot v2.1 format) produced with zero extra teleoperation from real seed data.
  • Pipeline: a LoRA-tuned Cosmos-3 video model generates manipulation videos, and RoboHaMeR recovers robot trajectories from RGB, turning generated video into action-labeled data.

Show abstract
We present a dataset of 340 fully synthetic manipulation demonstrations generated from real seed data for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) training. The corpus contains 32,340 frames with synchronized RGB and actions across eight object categories, represented as LeRobot v2.1 episodes with H.264 video, per-frame robot states and pose-valued action labels, language task annotations, summary statistics and quality decisions. Each synthetic demonstration is produced without additional teleoperation: a LoRA-fine-tuned Cosmos-3 model generates a manipulation video, and RoboHaMeR recovers the corresponding robot trajectory from RGB. Quality screening accepted 300 of 340 episodes for policy fine-tuning. As preliminary evidence of feasibility, a pretrained GR00T N1.6 policy fine-tuned exclusively on the 300 accepted episodes reaches 16/20 (80%) real-world pick-and-place success on the Oreo-package task. A policy fine-tuned on 105 real teleoperated episodes achieved 18/20 (90%) under the same protocol. These results are based on one object and 20 trials per policy and are therefore reported as preliminary evidence rather than a general performance claim.

Lab2Sim: Agentic Generation of Interactive Laboratory Equipment from Operation Videos

2026-11-25 cs.RO 👤 Zhengping Che (h=30)
Yuxin Lin, Pei Ren, Yirong Yang, Xuxin Lv, Xingchuang Liao
Core Contributions
  • Generates editable, simulation-ready laboratory equipment — including articulations and internal cavities — directly from egocentric or multi-view operation videos.
  • Splits the job across four agents (Evidence, Asset, Verifier, Simulation Integration), with an independent verifier checking geometry and appearance against video evidence before assets are simulated.
  • Uses execution feedback in simulation to tune physical properties and joints, so assets behave correctly, not just look right — e.g., valve-controlled drainage.

Show abstract
Generating laboratory assets that faithfully reproduce the appearance, structure, and joint motions of real equipment remains challenging. Existing asset generation methods struggle to directly generate simulation assets for all equipment observed in operation videos and to faithfully reproduce the appearance, joint motions, and internal cavities of the real equipment. Autonomous robotic palpation offers repeatable, quantitative estimates of tissue properties for stiff inclusion localization. However, existing methods often rely on prior mesh models or planar surface assumptions that do not generalize to complex human anatomy. We propose an ergodic palpation framework that operates directly on curved soft surfaces using point cloud data. Central to our approach is the Laplace--Beltrami operator, which grounds both online estimation and exploration in the surface intrinsic geometry. The design of robots able to locomote effectively over a diversity of terrain requires detailed ground interaction models; unfortunately such models are lacking due to the complicated response of real world substrates which can yield and flow in response to loading. To advance our understanding of the relevant modeling and design issues, we conduct a comparative study of the performance of DASH and RoACH, two small, biologically inspired, six legged, lightweight (10 cm, 20 g) robots fabricated using the smart composite microstructure (SCM) process.
